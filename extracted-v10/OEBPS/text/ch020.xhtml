Chapter Twenty

An Old Friend: Irene

Still the café · after the scout’s bow

The window seat · one name surfacing in a contact list

Where thinking of a person and being messaged by her land in the same minute

After the scout left, the talk in the café sank lower by degrees.

Now and then, though, someone still glanced secretly toward Bae Do-yoon — curiosity in those looks, and a little envy.

Being handed an SM Entertainment scout’s card on the spot was, for many young people, close enough to a scene from a drama.

Bae Do-yoon felt little movement in himself. And then, a name surfaced in his head.

Come to think of it: he knew someone who had debuted at SM. An old friend, at that.

He set the coffee down, took out his phone, and opened the contact list.

He scrolled down a few times.

Soon a name appeared on the screen.

Irene.

Bae Do-yoon’s finger rested on the name. It had been a while since they last spoke.

Precisely: since her official debut, the two of them had talked much less than before.

That was normal. A debuted entertainer is no longer only herself.

Bae Do-yoon understood this well, so he had never once disturbed her on his own initiative.

And she was the same. She was not the type to seek people out for idle chat.

Most of the time, the two of them only exchanged a line of greeting at holidays, or a few short sentences when one of them had important news.

Looking at the name, a little pale nostalgia entered Bae Do-yoon’s eyes.

When he first met her, she was not yet Irene, and not yet the leader of Red Velvet.

She was only a girl who had come up from Daegu to Seoul, dragging a suitcase, standing near a station thick with people — a little lost, and working hard to stay calm anyway.

She did not yet have the ease the lenses later polished into her.

Pretty, of course, she had been. But more than pretty: stubborn.

A girl from out of town, alone in Seoul, wanting to stand on a stage — that is not an easy thing.

And then his phone vibrated suddenly in his hand, and a new message jumped up at the top of the screen.

The sender’s name was exactly the one he had been looking at.

Bae Do-yoon’s motion stilled faintly; he looked at the screen, surprise showing in his eyes.

This coincidental? I had only just thought of her, and she messages.

He opened the chat. What she had sent was very simple.

Bae Do-yoon looked at those lines, his brow lifting a little.

As a debuted entertainer, even meeting an old acquaintance required her to plan the route, the entrance, and whether anyone would see.

The halo the stage brought also brought another form of binding.

Four steps for one cup of coffee between two people who have known each other for years. The stage’s halo has its own gravity, and it pulls doors shut behind it.

· · ·

7:50 in the evening · a dim alley, a sliver of warm light

Cheongdam-dong · the side door, and the practice room below

Where a prosecutor learns that seeing an old friend now takes four steps and two disguises

At ten to eight in the evening, Bae Do-yoon arrived in Cheongdam-dong.

The address she had sent sat in an unremarkable commercial building. No obvious signage outside; nothing eye-catching at the entrance. Without knowing the address in advance, one would never imagine a private practice room inside.

Bae Do-yoon did not use the main entrance.

Following the warning in her message, he went around and in by the side door.

The side door stood in a narrow alley between two buildings, the lighting a little dim, a few cars parked along the wall.

Bae Do-yoon took out his phone and sent her one message.

“I’ve arrived.”

Under half a minute later, light footsteps came from inside the side door.

The door was opened from within.

Bae Do-yoon lifted his eyes. Behind the door stood a woman.

Side-door inventory: the woman behind the door

Cap black baseball cap, pulled low

Mask white, high over the nose; nearly the whole face gone

Hoodie loose, light grey, swallowing the shoulders

Trousers black sweatpants; the whole assembly packaged tight

Eyes clear, quiet, carrying a little familiar smile — the part that does the recognizing

Almost nothing of the face is left to see, and yet he knew her in one glance: not by the outline, but by the eyes.

Identity-locked to her card portrait (id-23): same clear quiet eyes, same long black wave escaping the cap. Plate set from the side-door scene, Chapter 20.

Across the half-open door, the two of them looked at each other for two seconds.

She bent her eyes first.

“Long time no see.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, and smiled too.

“Long time no see.”

She opened the door a little wider and turned sideways to let him in.

“Come in first.”

After Bae Do-yoon stepped inside, the door closed quickly behind him.

Within was a short corridor; at its far end, practice-room light showed.

She led him inward.

Bae Do-yoon glanced once at her fully-armored getup, a little smile in his tone.

“Seeing you now is more trouble than summoning a suspect at the Prosecutors’ Office.”

Her step checked.

Then she looked back at him once, laughter in her eyes.

“Is that how Prosecutor Bae jokes, ordinarily?”

“Mm. Rarely,” Bae Do-yoon said, smile on his face.

“Then should I feel honored?” she asked, looking at him, returning the question.

Bae Do-yoon considered it seriously.

“You may.”

She could not help laughing out loud.

Her laugh was very light, as if afraid of being heard outside. But the smile in it was real — not the business smile practiced ten thousand times toward lenses on stage; the naturally loosened look of someone meeting an old friend.

Inside the practice room, she took off the cap and the mask.

The light fell, and her face was finally, wholly, visible.

Irene

Leader of Red Velvet; an old friend from before the name existed

SM Entertainment; debuted after trainee years measured in monthly evaluations

Met at a station: a girl from Daegu with a suitcase, pretending very hard not to be afraid

Speaks in few lines and no surplus stickers; plans doors, routes and basements before coffee. Stage-verified beauty, and underneath it the same stubborn girl who once rehearsed doubt in an empty room — the one he sees when he looks.

The old friend

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, his gaze resting a moment.

She had indeed changed a great deal. The last time he saw her, she had not yet debuted.

Then, she had still been only an SM trainee. Pretty, yes — but more than pretty: green, and careful. She had a habit of speaking very softly, as if afraid of troubling people; her smiles too she held back by reflex, with a little stiffness in them.

Now she was entirely different.

The features were still cool and pretty, but the bearing had been polished by lenses and stages into something more mature. Standing there in the plainest hoodie and sweatpants, she made it hard to move one’s eyes from her.

That was a kind of beauty verified by the stage.

Observed across one half-open door and two seconds of eye contact. Sample size: every year since a station afternoon.

She turned around and caught him looking.

She stilled first, then a little smile rose onto her face.

“What is it?”

Bae Do-yoon drew his gaze back. “Nothing.”

But she did not let him go at once; she looked Bae Do-yoon up and down in return. From his face, to his shoulders, to his build.

After a moment she lifted her brows, lightly.

“You’ve changed a lot.”

Bae Do-yoon sat on the sofa. “Changed where?”

She went to the small table and pushed a coffee she had prepared earlier toward him.

“More mature. And more charming.”

Having said it, she seemed to feel it had been a little direct; her eyes slid lightly aside.

Bae Do-yoon was calm; he took up the coffee and said, with a faint laugh:

“So the prosecutor’s profession has cosmetic effects as well.”

She stilled, then laughed.

“How had I never noticed you were this good at joking?”

“Perhaps you were too nervous, back then.”

She thought about it and nodded. “Also true.”

Back then she had indeed been nervous, always.

In the trainee period, so much was uncertain. Praised by a teacher today; criticized into doubting herself tomorrow. Passing this month’s evaluation did not mean next month was safe; many things pressed on her at once.

She had rarely, truly, relaxed.

Bae Do-yoon drank a sip of coffee — the temperature exactly right — and lifted his eyes to her.

“So. Why did you suddenly think of me?”

She did not answer at once. She sat in the chair opposite, both hands around her cup, eyes on him.

After a moment she smiled. “Then guess.”

Bae Do-yoon set the cup down. “You want me to guess?”

“Mm.” She bent her eyes. “Aren’t you a prosecutor? You should be good at reasoning.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “Prosecutors are not fortune-tellers.”

“But you always were good at reading people,” she said. “So guess.”

Bae Do-yoon leaned against the sofa, his gaze settling on her.

This time he did not stop briefly as before; he looked at her properly.

She noticed his line of sight quickly.

At first she kept her composure; a few seconds later, the smile on her face began not to hold.

She lowered her head, drank a sip of coffee, and set the cup down.

At last she could not help asking: “What are you looking at?”

Bae Do-yoon answered very naturally. “You.”

The tips of her ears went a little red almost at once; she had not expected him to say it so plainly.

In her impression Bae Do-yoon had always been that kind of restrained person. He rarely circled in speech, but rarely showed emotion plainly either — more as if everything were pressed down under calm.

So when he said those two characters — looking at you — so openly, it left a person not knowing how to take it.

She pressed her lips, lightly.

“What… what is there to look at, in me?”

“A great deal,” Bae Do-yoon said, tone calm, without a grain of perfunctoriness. “Prettier than before.”

Hearing that, her face, which had only been a little warm, went truly hot.

She looked down at the cup, fingers rubbing its wall. “Do you compliment people this directly now?”

“No.” Bae Do-yoon shook his head.

“Then why say it so?” she asked, curious.

“Because it is a fact,” Bae Do-yoon said. “The last time I saw you, you hadn’t debuted. You were green then, and nervous. Now it’s different.”

“Different how?”

“Brighter.”

She stilled, faintly.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “Before, you were like someone striving toward the stage. Now you are standing on it.”

Her fingers around the cup tightened, a little. That sentence was, to her, somewhat special.

Because many people praised her pretty, said she suited lenses, called her the visual center.

But Bae Do-yoon’s sentence was different.

What he saw was not the stage name, not the visual center. It was the girl who, before debuting, had rehearsed over and over in a practice room and sometimes doubted whether she could hold on at all.

She finally could not help laughing out.

The long-absent conversation loosened something in her.

After debut, there were many people around her. It looked lively. But the number of people with whom she needed to explain nothing was, in truth, very few.

Bae Do-yoon was one of them.

When he knew her, she had no stage name, no stage makeup, no fan screams, no life examined by countless eyes. Then she had only been herself — a girl from Daegu with a suitcase. And he had only been Bae Do-yoon, not today’s increasingly severe-looking Prosecutor Bae.

Bae Do-yoon lifted his coffee and drank again. “So — may I hear it now?”

She blinked.

“Hear what?”

“Why you suddenly asked to meet.”

The smile on her face gathered in a little.

She did not answer at once; she turned to look at the mirror on the practice-room wall.

The mirror held the two of them. Bae Do-yoon sat opposite her, a dark jacket over a white shirt, posture relaxed and still carrying a prosecutor’s particular calm.

Neither of them was the person they had been when they first met.

And strangely, sitting together, they did not seem so far apart either.

She was silent a moment, then said, softly: “Actually, there is no special matter.”

“Today someone at the company brought up old trainee-period things, and I suddenly thought of many people.”

She paused.

“And thought of you.”

She smiled once, only the smile carried a little pale tiredness.

“And then I wanted to see you once.”

The reason sounded simple — even willful.

But Bae Do-yoon did not press on whether it was true; he gave a light laugh. “So I was caught in nostalgia’s splash?”

She stilled, then laughed. “You may say that.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “Then I’m rather honored.”

She looked at him, her eyes a degree softer.

“Actually I thought you would ask whether I had met trouble.”

“If you met trouble, you would say.”

“And if I don’t say?”

“Then you don’t want to say yet.”

She was quiet a few seconds. “You have always been like this.”

“Like what?”

“Not pressing people.”

Bae Do-yoon said, lightly: “Pressing is a work technique. It isn’t for use on friends.”

Friends.

Hearing that word, her expression moved, faintly.

She lowered her head and drank a sip of coffee, hiding a little smile at the corner of her mouth.

“So we are friends, are we.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “Otherwise?”

She lifted her eyes, half joke, half serious.

“I had thought Prosecutor Bae’s friend list required formal approval.”

Bae Do-yoon answered: “You passed approval a very long time ago.”

She froze, looking at him; after a few seconds she asked, softly:

“When?”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. “The first time you got lost in Seoul and insisted on looking calm about it.”

Her face stiffened. “You still remember that?”

“I remember.”

“Can it be forgotten?”

“Not really.”

She covered her forehead. “Aiy — that was truly embarrassing.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “It wasn’t that bad.”

“Not that bad, how?”

“At least you didn’t get into the unlicensed car.”

She put her hand down and looked at him, helpless. “That was because you happened to appear.”

The station, years ago · a suitcase and a bad driver

It was very early — so early that remembering it now feels like remembering another person’s life. She had just arrived in Seoul, suitcase in hand, standing near the station, working hard to pretend she was not afraid. Seoul was too big; too many people, too complicated roads, everything strange.

While an unlicensed-taxi driver hassled her into not knowing what to do, Bae Do-yoon appeared. He did not act like a hero; he only asked the driver, very calmly, a few questions: did he hold an operating license; should the transportation authority be contacted. The driver left, cursing, soon after.

Then Bae Do-yoon looked up her route for her and put her into a safe taxi. For him it may have been only a convenience done in passing. For her it was the first kindness a stranger gave her in Seoul — which is why she has kept it all this time.

She said, softly: “Actually I thought about it later. If I hadn’t met you that day, my first impression of Seoul might have been a very bad one.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “Even without me, you would have adapted.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you have adapted now.”

She smiled. “You always reverse-deduce your conclusions like this.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “Results are a kind of evidence too.”

She: …

She really should not try to argue logic with a prosecutor.

On friend lists and formal approvals

Some relationships are never applied for, never interviewed, never stamped — and yet, produced on demand years later, turn out to have been approved all along, by a courthouse of one. The evidence is thin: a suitcase, an unlicensed driver, a route looked up on a stranger’s phone. The verdict, however, has never been appealed. Meeting now takes four steps and two disguises; the approval, mercifully, takes none.
