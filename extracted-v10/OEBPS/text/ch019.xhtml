Chapter Nineteen

Power Is a Man’s Best Tonic

The morning after enrollment · mats, wooden dummy, honest sweat

Inmudang Chinese Martial Arts Hall · training area

Where a prosecutor learns that understanding and doing are two different things

The middle-aged man looked Bae Do-yoon up and down once.

He was looking at the shoulders, the waist, the knees, and the stance of the feet.

After a moment he asked: “Trained before?”

Bae Do-yoon shook his head. “No systematic training.”

The master nodded. “What do you want to learn?”

Bae Do-yoon answered directly.

“Self-defense.”

The master asked again: “Defend against whom?”

Bae Do-yoon’s expression checked faintly; then he said: “People taller than me, bigger than me, and possibly not open to reason.”

The master was silent a few seconds, then nodded.

“Then first learn how to stand firm.”

Bae Do-yoon startled faintly, puzzled. “Stand firm?”

The master said: “Yes.”

He walked to the center of the mats and signaled Bae Do-yoon over.

First lesson · standing firm, delivered at the center of the mats

Ordinary people learning self-defense think first of how to put the other man down. In real danger the first thing is not putting him down — it is not being controlled by him in one motion.

Lose your center and you can only be dragged where he pulls. When you are caught, if your feet are not even steady under you, the finest technique in the world is useless.

Understanding and doing are two different things.

It did not sound hot-blooded. It sounded true, which was better.

Bae Do-yoon walked over.

“I understand,” he said.

The master looked at him once — and suddenly reached out and pushed, lightly, at Bae Do-yoon’s shoulder.

The force was not large. So Bae Do-yoon believed.

Yet the next second his body swayed backward out of his own control, and his right foot retreated half a step on its own.

The master drew his hand back.

“That is not standing firm.”

Bae Do-yoon looked down at his own feet.

That push had not been heavy — it had been, one could say, very light. And yet he had been pushed.

The master said, lightly: “Your physical conditions are good and your reactions are not slow. But you do not know how to use your body. Shoulder is shoulder, waist is waist, feet are feet — all of it scattered.”

The master was right.

What Bae Do-yoon ordinarily relied on was his brain, his position, his language, and the rules.

As for the body: he had, in truth, never really trained it.

The master signaled a student beside them to make room.

“Today we train basics.”

Bae Do-yoon asked: “What do the basics include?”

The master raised three fingers.

The basics, as counted on three fingers

01Stance — the thing he had just failed in front of the whole class

02Footwork — the half step his right foot had taken on its own

03Escapes — the art of not being held

04And falls — added after the three fingers went down, in a perfectly flat voice

“People learning self-defense first learn how to fall less disastrously.” Bae Do-yoon: … He felt, suddenly, that he had come to the right place. This master did not like to boast at all.

The whole morning that followed, Bae Do-yoon experienced for the first time, systematically, what basic training means.

There was no clean one-strike takedown from the films, no handsome wooden-dummy combination.

What the master made him drill was simple and dull; the movements looked uncomplicated. Doing them was an entirely different matter.

And when the master corrected him, he spared almost no face.

“Feet too dead.”

“Shoulders loose.”

“Waist — don’t lock it.”

“Eyes off the floor.”

“Not pulling with the hand. Turning with the body.”

“Caught like that, you are only handing yourself over.”

Bae Do-yoon repeated, and was corrected, and repeated again.

At the start he could still keep his breathing even.

After half an hour his forehead had begun to seep sweat; after an hour the back of his shirt was wet through in a patch.

After two hours he finally understood why the front-desk girl had said the adult self-defense class was harder than ordinary fitness.

Every movement had to find its center again.

Every push, every grab, every deflection had to be re-adjusted from zero.

Bae Do-yoon rarely had this kind of experience.

In the Prosecutors’ Office his thinking always faced outward. He judged people, moved people, took people apart.

Here, his attention was for the first time forced entirely back onto himself.

Sweat ran down from his temple into his eye, stinging a little.

He lifted his arm and wiped it.

The master stood in front of him and said: “Once more.”

Bae Do-yoon said nothing; he only set his posture again.

A few students beside them could not help glancing over.

Ordinary new adult students, drilled to this level, mostly found reasons to rest.

Bae Do-yoon did not. It was not that he was not tired — on the contrary, he looked thoroughly tired.

But after every correction he listened to, he did it again: no impatience, no going through the motions.

The morning, counted

30 minutessweat at the forehead; breathing still his own

60 minutesthe back of the shirt wet through in a patch

120 minutesunderstanding, at last, the front desk’s warning

Corrections receivedall of them accepted; none of them argued; every one redone

New adult students at this depth usually invent a rest. This one invented nothing, which is how the master noticed him.

The master had plainly noticed too. He reached out again and took Bae Do-yoon’s wrist.

“Escape.”

Bae Do-yoon’s wrist turned; the shoulder loosened with it; the feet stepped sideways; the body shifted a degree out of line.

This time he did not simply haul outward with arm strength. He borrowed the turn and the step to cut the angle at which he was held.

The master’s fingers loosened a fraction.

Bae Do-yoon flowed out of the hold, withdrew half a step, and opened the distance again.

The movement was not yet beautiful — it was even a little raw — but the logic was right.

A flicker of satisfaction crossed the master’s eyes.

“Not bad.”

Bae Do-yoon’s breathing was heavy. “Was that right, just now?”

The master nodded.

“The direction was right.”

Bae Do-yoon let out a light breath.

The master said: “You grasp things quickly.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled.

“Perhaps because I am rather afraid of being lifted up again.”

The master looked at him once.

He had not expected it said so plainly.

“Fear is not a bad thing,” the master said. “Knowing you fear, you learn seriously. The truly troublesome ones are those who do not know they will be afraid.”

That sentence made Bae Do-yoon nod. “Taught well.”

The master looked at him and said: “Your coordination is good, your reactions are fast, and most importantly your head is clear.”

Bae Do-yoon wiped sweat. “Martial arts looks at the head too?”

“Of course it looks,” the master said.

He pointed at his own head.

“Many people, the moment they are caught, panic; panicking, they use force wildly; the more wildly, the easier they are to control. You are a beginner, but you think about why a movement works. That is good.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “Professional habit.”

The master asked: “What work do you do?”

Bae Do-yoon paused a moment. “Civil servant.”

The master looked at him and did not press further; he said only, lightly: “No wonder.”

Bae Do-yoon asked: “No wonder what?”

The master said: “You don’t look like a man who came for fitness.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled. “Then what do I look like?”

The master thought about it. “Like a man who came to solve a problem.”

Bae Do-yoon did not deny it. From a certain angle, it was exactly so.

He had not come for interest, nor for shaping his body, but to fill one very practical gap.

He could face many people with the law.

But if someone reached out and caught him before the law arrived, he needed at least a little capacity to react.

· · ·

When the morning’s training ended, Bae Do-yoon was soaked in sweat.

The training uniform stuck to his back; the hair at his forehead was wet through.

His wrists were sore. Thighs and calves had begun to swell with ache. The shoulders and back especially, after being corrected over and over, seemed finally to realize how little they had ever been seriously used.

Strangely, Bae Do-yoon did not dislike the feeling.

On the contrary, he found it comfortable.

Once the body was tired past a certain point, the head turned clear instead. The sweat carried certain irritations out with it.

He sat at the edge of the mats, accepted the water the front-desk girl handed over, and thanked her in a low voice.

Twisting the cap open, he tilted his head and drank.

Cold water slid down his throat; he breathed out, lightly.

And then, before his eyes, a line of characters only he could see surfaced.

Bae Do-yoon’s hand stilled, faintly.

The next second, new prompts continued to surface.

Entry notice · white rank, conservatively worded

Loggedyou have undergone preliminary, systematic combat training

Understoodcenter of gravity · distance · escapes · basic self-defense movements

New entryCombat Beginner (White)

Effectyou are no longer an ordinary person with no combat foundation whatever; against simple grabs, shoves and close-range entanglement, you possess preliminary response capacity

A white entry. Low rank, plain description, no chime of celebration. It read like a receipt for the morning rather than a reward for it — which, he thought, was exactly right.

Bae Do-yoon looked down at his own wrist.

From the repeated escape drills it had gone a little red.

It ached faintly — and the ache felt solid.

The master walked over beside him and looked at him once.

“How does it feel?”

Bae Do-yoon drew his gaze back and closed the bottle cap.

“Very tired.”

The master nodded.

“Normal.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled. “But not bad.”

The master looked at him. “Next week, continue?”

Bae Do-yoon did not hesitate. “Continue.”

The master said: “You have talent and you learn fast. But self-defense is not a one-or-two-day matter. If you want it truly useful, you persist.”

Bae Do-yoon stood up and nodded, serious.

“I understand.”

The master said: “From next time I will add basic conditioning and simple sparring.”

Bae Do-yoon asked: “Simple sparring?”

The master said, lightly: “Learning the movement is one thing. Being truly caught, truly pushed, is another.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent a moment.

Then he nodded.

“Good.”

Seeing him agree so crisply, a little more smile entered the master’s eyes.

“You don’t fear hardship, at least.”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it, then said: “Compared with being lifted up like a chicken, hardship is acceptable.”

Master: …

Front-desk girl, beside them: …

Having said it, Bae Do-yoon also felt the sentence had been excessively dull. But he did not explain; he only took up his jacket and nodded slightly to the master.

“Thank you for today.”

The master waved a hand.

“Stretch when you get back, and soak in a hot bath. Otherwise tomorrow you will learn what pain is.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “Good.”

· · ·

Near noon · brighter sun, floor-to-ceiling windows

A café near the martial arts school

One iced americano, one window seat, and one gray-suited interruption

When he left the school it was already close to noon.

The sunlight was much brighter than in the morning.

Bae Do-yoon walked down and stood at the street edge, lifting a hand against the glare.

His body was exhausted; his mood, unexpectedly, was good.

Near the school was a café with a wall of floor-to-ceiling windows at its front, and quite a few young people seated inside.

Bae Do-yoon had only wanted a place to sit a while and drink an iced coffee, letting his body come down slowly from the training’s excitement.

So he pushed the door open and went in.

Inside, a soft English song played. The air held the smell of coffee beans and baked bread.

Bae Do-yoon ordered an iced americano and sat by the window.

Sunlight fell through the glass onto the tabletop.

He draped his jacket over the chair back and lifted the coffee for a sip.

The cold bitterness went down his throat, and the post-training heat in him was pressed down a little.

Bae Do-yoon suddenly felt that a rest day was not, perhaps, entirely without meaning.

And then a man arrived at his table.

“Hello.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his eyes.

The man standing there was in his thirties, in a light gray suit, hair neatly set, a professional smile on his face.

His manner was not offensive; he even inclined himself slightly before speaking.

“Sorry to trouble you — may I take a little of your time?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at him once.

“What is it?”

The man drew a card from a card case and handed it over with both hands.

“I’m with SM Entertainment’s New Development Team. My main work is discovering new talent.”

Bae Do-yoon’s hand, taking the card, stopped a fraction.

SM?

Guests at the neighboring tables seemed to hear the name too. Someone looked up. Two women near the bar, mid-conversation in low voices, stopped in unison.

The man did not mind the surrounding gazes; he went on: “I noticed you outside just now. Your visual conditions are excellent, and your bearing is very particular.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at the card in his hand and did not speak for a moment.

Seeing that he had not refused at once, the man continued: “You aren’t the merely-handsome type. You have recognizability — the shoulder-and-neck line, the body’s proportions, the contour of the face: all of it suits the lens…”

Bae Do-yoon: …

It had been a very long time since anyone had evaluated him in this manner.

The man’s tone was sincere.

“If you’re willing, come to our company for one simple interview. With your conditions — toward acting, or toward debuting as an artist after systematic training — there is great potential either way.”

Reaching there he paused a little, and added:

“SM can give you a stage large enough.”

When that sentence landed, the low talk in the café could no longer be pressed down.

The café, in undertones, around one window seat

table 2

SM? He was just picked by an SM scout?

bar side

Daebak… he really does look like an entertainer. If it were me, I’d say yes on the spot.

table 5

With that bearing, if he debuted he’d be popular, wouldn’t he? Though he looks a little older? But really handsome.

For many of the young people in the room, being handed an SM scout’s card was a scene sitting very close to a dream. The man at the window only listened, and found it equally funny and hopeless.

Bae Do-yoon heard the fine scattered voices and did not know whether to laugh or cry inside.

He had only trained, and wanted one iced americano, and had met an SM scout.

Set many years earlier, this might have made him consider seriously.

For a returned person, after all, the entertainment world was not an entirely impossible road. He knew the coming trends; he knew which groups would burn; he knew which styles the market would accept. And on top of it all, he had the entries.

Had he chosen the artist’s road back then, with looks, experience and the cheat both in hand, a starlit boulevard was not out of reach. He might even have gone farther than many.

But now was no longer then.

Bae Do-yoon set the card on the table and looked up at the man.

“Thank you for the recognition.”

Expectation showed in the man’s eyes.

Bae Do-yoon continued: “But I have formal work now. The artist direction is not suitable for me, I’m afraid.”

The man looked at him, still not quite giving up.

“Actually, if you’re willing, the company can arrange timing around your situation. The artist route isn’t only the trainee way these days — the acting direction has great room too.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled.

“I understand your kindness.”

His tone was mild, but the refusal was clear.

“I have no intention of changing professions at present.”

The man looked at him, regret plainly in his eyes.

In all his years in the trade he had seen no few good-looking people. But this young man in front of him was indeed different. The visual conditions were outstanding, certainly. More important was the bearing: a very rare calm and plainness about him; sitting there doing nothing at all, he was easy to notice. That bearing, set before a lens, would have memory-points.

The man sighed, but did not press further.

He put his professional smile back on.

“Then it’s a real pity.”

Saying it, he pointed at the card on the table.

“But keep the card. If you change your mind later, or know someone suitable, you can contact me.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“Good.”

The man inclined himself once more.

“Sorry to have troubled you.”

“Not at all.”

After the man left, the talk in the café sank again, though now and then someone still glanced secretly at Bae Do-yoon — curiosity and a little envy in those looks.

And Bae Do-yoon only looked down at the card on the table, and suddenly gave a light laugh.

The card, as it travelled

SM Entertainment · New Development Team · new-talent discovery. Handed over with both hands at a window seat; set down on the table; picked up again; and finally slipped into a wallet — not because its holder intends to debut, but because the whole affair is a little funny: a man already a prosecutor, invited on a rest day, over an iced americano, to become an idol.

SM Entertainment.

As a returned person carrying the entry cheat, Bae Do-yoon had assumed that this life, even if it could not easily change the whole world, would at least hold more choices than an ordinary man’s. The entertainment world was of course among them. Looks, memory, information advantage, system ability — poured onto a stage, they might have let him lead the field alone. Idol, actor, even a darling of crossover capital riding the era’s changes: none of it was impossible.

But life rarely walks by plan.

Especially in Korea.

That year, Lee Boo-jin had delivered what could only be called a strike from another dimension. The road that might have run toward practice rooms, stage lights and fan screams was folded, by force, toward the other side.

The folding, remembered without resentment now

He had been dissatisfied once. Nobody likes his life rewritten by another’s hand without asking. But time passed, and the resentment toward Lee Boo-jin thinned instead of thickening. He no longer looked at that year through a boy’s eyes.

Because she made him see one thing very early: in this country, glamour is not freedom. An idol on stage — dazzling lights, screaming fans, advertisements flooding every surface — seems to hold everything. And still, an idol is in the end an idol. Contracts, companies, capital, opinion, chaebol, connections, media: layer upon layer pressing down, the halo on stage does not necessarily protect the person under it. However red, before a true chaebol group and the prosecutors’ system, an entertainer may remain merely an expensive commodity — pretty, rare, valuable, and still tradable, packageable, shelfable, discardable.

Power is different. Power is not necessarily dazzling. It hides in procedure, in documents, behind one signature, one summons, one search warrant. And it can make many seemingly towering people sit down and listen to you speak. It can make many who are used to solving everything with money learn, for the first time, that money does not solve everything.

Bae Do-yoon lifted his coffee and drank again.

The ice had melted some; the taste was thinner than before.

He looked at the SM card on the table, eyes calm.

Without Lee Boo-jin that year, he might truly have walked the other road.

Thinking of it now, he did not feel regret. Because he had already tasted another thing’s flavor — a thing not hot as applause and not brilliant as lights.

He looked down at his wrist once more. It still ached faintly. The escape learned this morning seemed to remain in the muscle’s memory.

Bae Do-yoon suddenly remembered the master’s words.

When someone catches you, do not hurry into wild force. First stand firm; see the other’s direction; then decide how to come away.

When the people Lee Boo-jin sent had lifted him away like a chicken, he could only memorize that powerlessness.

But now, he stood in another place already.

He gave a light laugh.

In a certain sense, he should thank Lee Boo-jin. Because by a very rough method — rough as a hand closing on a child’s collar — she had told him in advance the true operating rules of this country.

In Korea, however bright the light on stage, it does not shine on every place.

Power is a man’s best tonic.

On tonics, and other things absorbed through the skin

He spent one morning learning that a body, like a case, must be built from its center outward: stance before strike, footing before flourish. He spent one coffee learning that a stage, like a halo, is rented light — bright, directional, and switchable by other hands. What he absorbed through the sweat and what he absorbed through the americano were, in the end, the same lesson in two solvents: stand firm first, and choose which room’s rules you intend to be standing under.
