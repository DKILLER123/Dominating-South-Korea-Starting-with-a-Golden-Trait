Chapter Nine

The New Neighbor Is a Girl-Group Member?

Day one in office · dusk by the time he clocks out

Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office · desks, corridors, the file system

A first day is mostly being handed things

By the time Bae Do-yoon left work, the sky had already gone dark.

Today was his first official day. Tiring, strictly speaking, it was not: the real cases had not reached him yet, and nothing complicated was his to carry alone. The problem was that there was no rest anywhere in it. The whole day was movement.

Day one, exactly as the building assigned it

01Personnel section: confirm the paperwork

02Office: greet the chief prosecutor

03Meet the seniors of his own division

04Learn the case-file management system

05Sit with the administrative officer and hear how the workflow actually runs

Not one of these arrived with a rest break attached. Between them, at even intervals: Prosecutor Bae, run this material over. Prosecutor Bae, copy these while you’re at it. Newcomer — come here a moment.

On the outside he kept the polite smile on the whole time.

“Yes, senior.”

“Of course, senior.”

“Understood, senior.”

On the inside, he had been rolling his eyes since lunch.

The sunbae-hubae machine, and everywhere it also runs

Korea’s senior-junior culture: the entertainment business has it, the companies have it, and — as of this morning — the Prosecutors’ Office has it in exactly the same flavor. A newcomer bows to everyone. The chief prosecutor, fine, that is hierarchy with a reason behind it. But some of those demanding the title senior from him today arrived barely half a year before he did, and said it with a completely straight face.

Under somebody else’s eaves, you lower your head when the beam is low.

Especially on day one: never make an enemy over a small thing.

He chanted endure at himself before every sentence. If he were a born-and-bred Korean, perhaps the habit would sit on him more naturally. He is only half one. Respect for superiors he understands; bowing low to a same-aged colleague who started six months earlier is a different tax, and it lands somewhere behind his molars.

Still, it was only internal complaint. After these years he had grown somewhat used to the rules. And today, of all days, was not the day to grow enemies.

· · ·

The same evening · nearly dark

The supermarket near his apartment · the checkout line

Two cans of beer, one bag of chips, and a misunderstanding already in motion

He ate something quick at home, then went down to the supermarket near the apartment for drinks and snacks. First day on the job: nothing in it worth celebrating, but a man may at least buy himself a small consolation.

The store was not crowded. He took two cans of beer, a bag of chips, and on impulse a pack of ready-to-eat chicken breast.

In the checkout line he noticed the woman ahead of him.

Checkout-line inventory: the woman in front of him

Cap brim pressed low, hiding very nearly half the face

Coat oversized, swallowing the whole figure

Stature small; a small frame carrying a large bundle

Arms full of snacks and canned drinks, hugged tight

The whole assembly reads as one sentence: a person who very much does not wish to be recognized.

No rain, no strong sun, and a cap at night. Seoul is full of strange people; he decided not to spend attention on it.

Plate set from the supermarket scene, Chapter 9 — cap low, coat oversized, arms full

She paid and left in a hurry with her bundle. Soon it was his turn; he paid, and walked toward the apartment with his bag.

He had not gone far when he saw her again, a short distance ahead.

Same road. Same direction.

At first he thought nothing of it: this is a residential neighborhood, and sharing a route is ordinary. But then the two of them turned corner after corner, and the route stayed exactly the same.

The woman ahead seemed to notice too. Her steps hesitated for a fraction of a second — and then quickened.

Bae Do-yoon: …

He looked down at the supermarket bag in his hand, then back over his shoulder. An empty street; streetlamps, and a car passing now and then. No suspicious figure anywhere.

Then why is she running?

He kept walking at his own pace, and she went faster and faster: fast walk shading into a jog. The wind nearly lifted her cap and she clamped a hand to it.

Only then did the understanding arrive, late.

Could it be — she has taken me for one of those stalking perverts?

For a moment he did not know whether to laugh or weep. Heaven and earth as his witnesses: he had worked the whole day and wanted nothing but a shower and a beer; he had no heart left for stalking anyone. And even if he were some kind of pervert, would a pervert set out to commit crimes carrying a bag of chips and chicken breast?

But explaining now seemed equally wrong. He could hardly shout across a dozen meters: Miss, don’t misunderstand, I am not a pervert. Shouted aloud, that sentence sounds even more like a pervert.

So he held his pace, unhurried, and let the coincidence do the rest.

The rest was this: the woman walked into his apartment building.

Bae Do-yoon: …

His steps paused. No way. What are the odds?

He went in after her. She stood at the elevator, heard the footsteps behind her, and her shoulders visibly went stiff. She stabbed at the call button. He stopped a few paces off, kept his distance, and pretended not to see her tension.

The elevator came. She practically threw herself in. A step behind, he entered too.

The cab was small, and the atmosphere turned delicate at once. She kept her head down, cap pressed lower. He stood at the opposite wall, eyes strictly ahead. Neither spoke. The numbers climbed one floor at a time.

At their floor the doors opened and she was out at a trot.

Bae Do-yoon watched her back and gave up on words entirely. Same building. Same floor. This misunderstanding was never going to wash clean.

Then he saw her run to the door of the unit beside his own, and begin frantically digging through her bag for keys.

His brow lifted. So: the new neighbor who moved in this morning.

The more nervous she got, the clumsier her hands became. One arm hugging the grocery bag, the other in the bag’s mouth, muttering something under her breath.

And then the keys slid out of the bag and hit the floor.

Clack.

She froze. He happened to be arriving beside her at exactly that moment, and the corridor’s atmosphere went delicate all over again.

Bae Do-yoon looked at the keys on the floor, then at the small woman with the cap pressed low. He did not move at once, mainly so as not to startle her: from where she stood, the evidence did read a certain way. Same road from the supermarket, same building, same elevator, same floor.

The case against the man in the corridor, as it stood

AccusationStalking pervert, assembled from one shared route and one shared elevator

Evidence against himSame road · same turns · same building · same elevator · same floor

Evidence for himOne supermarket bag (beer, chips, chicken breast), one pressed suit, one face too tired to scheme

From her angle it reads as stalking. From his, no predator shops for chicken breast on the way to a crime.

Neither file is complete. Both are honest. The corridor will settle the matter in about ten seconds.

She did not move either. Her back against her door, one arm locked around the grocery bag, the other hand already inside her pocket, in the posture of a person two seconds from a phone.

They held that standoff for several seconds.

At last Bae Do-yoon sighed, very softly, and stepped forward to pick the keys up.

His foot had barely moved when she triggered like an alarm.

“Don’t move!”

He stopped mid-step.

Her voice was taut. “Don’t come closer. One more step and I’m calling the police.”

Bae Do-yoon: …

The corridor went quiet again.

He looked down at his supermarket bag. Beer. Chips. Snacks. Then he looked at his own suit.

Does this combination honestly read as a man who commits crimes on the way home from work?

He took two silent seconds, then made his voice as harmless as he could.

“Uh. Miss. Please don’t misunderstand.”

“I’m not some bad person.”

She kept watching him, guard fully up.

He had no choice but to lift a hand and point at the door beside her.

“I should be your neighbor. That one is my apartment.”

She followed his finger. The number plate next door. Then back to him. Then down at the keys on the floor.

The wariness in her eyes loosened by a visible degree.

“Ah.” A small cry. “Is that so?”

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “Yes.”

“I saw people moving in next door this morning. I didn’t expect to meet the person herself by evening.”

The tension on her face converted, on the spot, into embarrassment. She seemed to realize her reaction had been somewhat large.

“I… I thought you were…” She did not finish the sentence.

Bae Do-yoon nodded along, helpfully.

“I understand. Stalking pervert.”

Is this woman too simple? She believed me without a single verification step.

Her ears went red at once, and she bowed in a hurry.

“Sorry. Sorry.”

“I’m really very sorry.”

Bae Do-yoon did not hold it against her. Life offering a moment like this now and then is, honestly, rather fun; and after a whole day of being handed errands by seniors, the misunderstanding rinsed a good deal of the day’s flavor off him.

“It’s fine,” he said, and bent to pick up the keys.

This time she did not stop him.

He held the keys out. “Here.”

She took them, small-voiced: “Thank you.”

And then, at last, she lifted her head.

The face under the cap came out into the corridor light.

Boram

Member of the girl group T-ara. The new neighbor next door.

Small stature, small face, fine features, and eyes far too bright for a cap to hide

Moved out of the group dormitory to live alone; the unit beside his, empty half a year, taken in one morning

Her group fell from the top of the country’s girl groups into a long valley after a public-opinion storm a year ago. She dresses like a person who does not wish to be recognized, and tonight she very nearly reported her own neighbor to the police for walking home behind her.

The neighbor

Bae Do-yoon looked at her properly and stopped for a beat.

Small, yes, plainly dressed, cap and all — and yet that face was obviously not an ordinary person’s face.

He studied it for two seconds and lifted a brow, faintly.

“Hm?”

Studied like that, she went tense again. “Wh-what is it?”

He rubbed his chin. “How come you look so much like that… what’s-her-name?”

Her body stiffened by one degree.

He frowned into his memory.

“Some girl group, wasn’t it…”

“I can’t place it just now.”

She was silent. She seemed already to sense what was coming.

Bae Do-yoon slapped his forehead.

“Oh. I remember.”

“That T-ara member, right?”

“Called…”

He jammed. The name stood at the tip of his tongue and refused to come out.

The shortest one. The one with the biggest chest.

“Jeon… Jeon Doo-ram?”

…

She pressed her lips together and said it the way a person surrenders: “Jeon Bo-ram.”

Good heavens — he had welded her name onto a certain much bigger historical figure’s. He nodded at once, hard.

“Yes!”

“T-ara’s Jeon Bo-ram!”

He said it, then looked at her once more. And then the gears engaged.

Bae Do-yoon blinked.

“Wait.” He looked at the small woman in front of him. “You’re not actually Jeon Bo-ram, are you?”

Jeon Bo-ram hugged her grocery bag, her expression complicated.

After a moment she nodded, lightly.

“…Yes.”

…

“You’re really Jeon Bo-ram?” Bae Do-yoon looked at her, his tone carrying an honest share of doubt.

It was not that he disbelieved her. It was that the coincidence was too much: in the morning he discovers a new neighbor moving in next door, and by evening the new neighbor is a girl-group member — and not just any, but T-ara’s Jeon Bo-ram. Are the odds of that not a little absurd?

Jeon Bo-ram held her bag and said, small: “Really.”

He looked her over twice more. Cap pressed low, clothes utterly ordinary; the whole person nothing at all like the idol member on a stage. If anything, like a primary-school child afraid of being caught by a teacher.

“That doesn’t add up.” He frowned. “A person of your standing, moving in next door to me?”

Jeon Bo-ram looked up at him.

Bae Do-yoon said it entirely seriously: “Shouldn’t you be living in a big villa?”

Jeon Bo-ram: …

She looked at him, and for a moment could not tell whether he was joking or conducting an inquiry. After a few seconds she sighed, lightly.

“Why would I live in a big villa? It’s not as if I’m some especially rich person.”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. Fair, actually. Korean entertainers’ incomes are nowhere near as exaggerated as the public imagines — idols least of all. They look glossy; then the company’s cut, the activity splits, the styling and wardrobe costs, the lodging management, fee after fee, and what actually lands in a person’s own hands can be surprisingly thin. And T-ara’s present situation was, to put it mildly, delicate.

A year ago, after that public-opinion storm, the group’s name took a heavy blow. He did not move in fan circles and had not followed the details, but the outline had reached him: from a top-tier girl group, straight down into a valley.

He looked at Jeon Bo-ram, and let his tone go a shade more serious.

“Don’t you have schedules right now? Why move here, all of a sudden?”

Jeon Bo-ram did not answer at once.

First she looked up at him — and this look was more careful than the ones before it. Earlier she had been too frightened to see anything but a man following her home. Now, with the misunderstanding rinsed away, she noticed that he was nothing like the stalking pervert she had imagined. Tall. The suit fitted properly. And up close, his face carried no trace of makeup at all — none of the foundation, contour and hair gel she saw on every male entertainer at the broadcast stations. This face was clean to an almost excessive degree. Entirely natural, no technology. In Korea, such a thing israre on purpose.

Jeon Bo-ram’s feelings went strange. She had just mistaken a man like this for an anti-fan, and very nearly called the police on him.

Stranger still: this was plainly their first meeting, and yet she found, without any reason she could name, that this man did not seem like a bad person.

The feeling arrived without warrant. She could not know why.

Jeon Bo-ram tightened her arms around the bag, and her voice dropped.

“You probably know about our situation, right?”

Bae Do-yoon did not play dumb. He nodded. “I’ve heard a little.”

Jeon Bo-ram sighed, lightly. “In the state we’re in now, what schedules could there be?”

The corridor light fell on the edge of her cap. She kept her head down; the tone carried little complaint, mostly tiredness.

“Lately the group has almost no activities arranged. The dormitory is stifling. So I wanted to move out and be quiet by myself for a while.”

Bae Do-yoon listened and nodded. “I see.”

He said it, then felt the sentence was dry. The person in front of him was plainly standing in a valley; and they had met four minutes ago, nowhere near close enough for comfort. Some cheap it will get better would have cost more than it paid.

So he thought about it, and said only: “Living alone isn’t bad. At least nobody makes noise at you.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked up.

He added: “Though if you ever feel someone following you at night, go to the security office first.”

Jeon Bo-ram’s face went red on the spot.

“I’m really sorry about just now,” she said, small.

Bae Do-yoon smiled. “It’s fine. Being on your guard isn’t a bad thing — especially for you, since you’re an entertainer.”

Being on your guard isn’t a bad thing — especially for you, since you’re an entertainer.

Jeon Bo-ram stilled.

She had expected him to laugh at her for being too sensitive. She had not expected this. The embarrassment in her chest thinned by a degree.

Bae Do-yoon glanced at the bag in her arms.

“Keys secure?”

She looked down at once to check. “Secure.”

“Good.” He took out his own keys, crossed to the door beside hers, and opened it. “I’ll head in first.”

Jeon Bo-ram nodded, lightly. “Mm.”

On mistaken identities

For readers without a Korean civics class loaded: the name Bae Do-yoon mangling into is one letter off a former president’s — the sort of error that, said aloud in a Seoul corridor, converts an apology scene into a comedy scene instantly. She corrected him the way people correct a man who has confused their name with a dynasty’s. He has never been so glad to be wrong about anything in his life.
