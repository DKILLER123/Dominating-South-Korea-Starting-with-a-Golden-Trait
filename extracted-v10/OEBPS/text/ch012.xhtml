Chapter Twelve

Wanting to Keep a Low Profile, and Being Taken for a Soft Persimmon?

Day three in office · morning, filed under ordinary

Criminal Division 3 · his desk, and the pile on it

A welcome ceremony, or a backlog dump — the pile does not say which

Bae Do-yoon looked at the stack of files piled on the desk, and was silent for a moment.

Is this the new prosecutor’s welcome ceremony, or is the Prosecutors’ Office planning to hand me its entire backlog?

The thought made one circuit of his head and was pressed down again.

Forget it. More has more’s advantage.

He was inside the Prosecutors’ Office now. Rather than adapting slowly, better to use the occasion to learn the building’s working rhythm and handling flow at speed.

Bae Do-yoon drew the chair out, sat, and opened the first file.

The office was not quiet. Telephone rings, keyboard strikes, the copier turning, the low conversation of investigative officers and investigators mixed together: the most ordinary background a working day in the Prosecutors’ Office has.

At first he was not quite used to it. Then, quickly, his attention sank in.

The files turned, page by page.

His speed was fast.

But watch closely and it was not idle flipping. His gaze stayed on each page only an instant, and always landed precisely on the few positions that mattered. He was not swallowing every character; he was raising the skeleton of a case in the shortest possible time.

Are the facts clear. Is the evidence closed. Are there obvious flaws in procedure. Does the police referral opinion hold weight.

Each of these sorted itself in his head as it arrived.

His desk, sorted while reading

01Facts clear, evidence closed — the pile that can proceed normally

02Evidence with gaps — the pile that needs supplementary investigation

03Pulled out singly, sticky note on the corner — the few that want a second pair of eyes later

Nobody asked for the sorting. Nobody watched it happen. By noon the desk had geography.

At some point a light, slow set of footsteps stopped beside him.

Kim Mi-ju stood at his desk with a paper cup in her hand.

Kim Mi-ju

Investigative officer assigned to Criminal Division 3 — his officer

A famous Seoul university graduate who once sat the Judicial Examination herself, and knows exactly what the road costs

Heard his name before he badged in; watched his page-turning speed and doubted it out loud

She carries a paper cup and a wager-loser’s lunch with the same seriousness, warns of risk without fanning flames or picking sides, and hides one pop-music loyalty so deep that only a prosecutor’s memory will ever find it.

The officer

By her post, an investigative officer works under the prosecutor’s direct command: assisting with case materials, contacting the relevant people, keeping the schedule, handling a hundred practical things. Said plainly — the prosecutor’s most direct and most important assistant inside the building.

Kim Mi-ju had only meant to ask whether he wanted coffee.

But coming close, she saw the speed at which Bae Do-yoon was turning files.

One page. Two pages. Three pages.

Almost no pause at all.

Kim Mi-ju’s brow gathered, despite herself.

Too fast.

She had been in the Prosecutors’ Office no short while, and had watched plenty of prosecutors work files: the cautious ones, the rough ones, the ones who read the conclusion first, the ones who backwards-solve the facts from the evidence index. But turning pages this fast — she was seeing it for the first time.

If it was only for show, that would be bad. These files end up inside the prosecutor’s judgment; turning a page is not the same as handling a case.

Kim Mi-ju pressed her doubt down and asked, softly: “Prosecutor Bae, would you like coffee, or milk?”

Bae Do-yoon was at a file’s evidence index; he did not lift his head. “Not right now, thank you.”

Kim Mi-ju did not leave at once.

She looked at the thick stack already turned on the desk, hesitated a moment, and still could not hold it in: “Reading this fast — can you truly remember it?”

Only then did Bae Do-yoon stop and look up at her.

Kim Mi-ju’s expression was very controlled, but the doubt in her eyes was not hard to see.

Bae Do-yoon did not take offense; he laughed, instead.

“My memory isn’t bad. This speed is exactly right for me.”

“That’s…”

Kim Mi-ju did not know how to take it up. In the time another man finished one file, Bae Do-yoon had turned four or five. If an ordinary person said such a thing, she would have called it conceit. But Bae Do-yoon was not an ordinary person.

What she already knew before he badged in

Seoul National University, College of Law. Top grades at the Judicial Research and Training Institute. Real cases during the internship, and a performance people still mentioned. A person like that earns the word excellent without argument.

Kim Mi-ju herself graduated from a famous Seoul university and once sat the Judicial Examination. That road never lacks geniuses; she had not become a prosecutor, and had taken the second door into the Prosecutors’ Office as an investigative officer. Precisely because she had tried, she knew better than most that nobody reaches his seat by accident.

And even so: excellent is excellent. The speed in front of her eyes sat outside the normal range.

Bae Do-yoon saw her half-swallowed look and asked: “You don’t believe me?”

Kim Mi-ju hesitated, and in the end nodded.

“I had long heard Prosecutor Bae was remarkable, but…” She did not finish the sentence.

Files are not exam questions. An exam question has a standard answer; a file is alive. Inside it are facts, evidence, procedure, contradictions in statements, and grey parts that require judgment. Reading fast does not mean reading well, and still less means being able to handle it.

Bae Do-yoon understood her meaning well enough.

He did not explain at length. He only pushed the stack he had already turned a little forward across the desk.

“Then pull any one of them.”

Kim Mi-ju paused, faintly.

Bae Do-yoon said: “Test whether I’m lying.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at the stack, then at Bae Do-yoon.

She did not decline.

“Good.”

She had just reached out when Bae Do-yoon suddenly put his hand down on the files.

Kim Mi-ju looked up at him.

Bae Do-yoon smiled. “But asking straight out like this — it isn’t very interesting, is it?”

Kim Mi-ju asked: “What does Prosecutor Bae mean?”

“Let’s make it a wager. On whether I can answer.”

Kim Mi-ju’s eyes moved.

She had only wanted to confirm he had read the files seriously; she had not expected him to turn the confirmation into a bet. Then again — fine. At least it meant he had confidence in himself.

Kim Mi-ju asked: “The stake?”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. “One lunch, plus one iced americano.”

The stake was not heavy; if anything, light.

Kim Mi-ju relaxed a degree and nodded. “No problem.”

Only then did Bae Do-yoon take his hand off the stack.

Kim Mi-ju did not pull from the top.

Her fingers slid slowly down the edge of the pile, and at the end drew out the very bottom file.

The lower the file, the earlier he had turned it. And the earlier it was turned, the harder it should be to remember.

“Officer Kim is very serious about this.”

Kim Mi-ju held the file to her chest and said, in a level tone: “If it’s a test, then of course it should be fair.”

She opened the file and let her eyes run quickly over the contents.

The file itself was not complex: an ordinary criminal case, its facts, evidence and referral opinion nothing special.

Kim Mi-ju did not hunt for cunning corners. She asked only the questions that best judge whether a man has truly read: the charge; the referring agency; the relationship between suspect and victim; what the key evidence is; what the police referral opinion rests on most.

Bae Do-yoon answered them one by one, almost without pause.

Kim Mi-ju began half-believing, half-doubting. Then, as his answers grew more complete, the speed at which she turned pages slowed by degrees.

Because Bae Do-yoon was not merely remembering the file.

He even pointed, in passing, at one place in it that wanted attention.

“Not a serious problem, but better patched in later handling. Otherwise, at the review-and-prosecution stage, the defense will catch it.”

Kim Mi-ju bent her head over the file and checked.

Several seconds later, she was silent.

He was right.

It was not the most conspicuous part of the file — and it was exactly the kind of part practice overlooks.

The wager, entered at a desk, mid-morning

StakeOne lunch and one iced americano, owed by the loser

DrawThe bottom file of the stack, chosen by the skeptic

QuestionsCharge · referring agency · suspect-victim relationship · key evidence · main basis of the police opinion

ResultAnswered without pause; plus one practical flaw flagged and confirmed against the paper

Doubt went in at one end of the morning; seriousness, and a little unconcealed respect, came out the other.

“I lost,” she said, closing the file. “The lunch and the americano are on the books,” he said. “Not in a hurry.”

Kim Mi-ju nodded, and her tone came out much more natural than before.

“I’ll remember it, Prosecutor Bae.”

Bae Do-yoon took up another file and said, in passing: “Rather than that — the sorting of these files later will need your cooperation. I’ve divided them by handling direction for now. When I’ve finished reading, we’ll make the records together.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at the several neat stacks on the desk, and only then understood: he had not merely been turning pages just now. He had already been handling them — and handling them clearly.

She drew a small breath and said, seriously: “Understood.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded and bent his head again.

The sound of paper turning resumed.

· · ·

At lunchtime Bae Do-yoon had just closed the last file and was rising to eat when a voice came from behind him.

“Prosecutor Bae.”

He turned. Kim Mi-ju was coming after him with her phone and wallet.

Her expression was entirely serious. “I’ve come to fulfill the wager.”

Bae Do-yoon stilled, then laughed. “Officer Kim remembered?”

“Of course,” Kim Mi-ju said. “A loss is a loss.”

“Then I won’t be polite.”

Noon · the rice-and-soup hour

The restaurant near the Prosecutors’ Office · a window table

Trays, badges, and one lowered voice

For all that, Bae Do-yoon did not deliberately choose anything expensive.

The restaurant near the Prosecutors’ Office is always crowded at noon: look out and it is prosecutors in suits, investigative officers and investigators with IDs on their chests.

In the queue he asked only for an ordinary set. Then, as Kim Mi-ju was about to pay, he pointed at the small sign beside it.

“Add one portion of grilled meat.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at him once.

Bae Do-yoon said: “It’s a wager stake, after all. It should differ from an ordinary meal by something.”

Kim Mi-ju could not help a laugh, and paid for him.

The two took a seat by the window. The room was full, but at lunch everyone speaks low, and the low voices make a kind of noisy cover.

Kim Mi-ju split her chopsticks and asked, taking the lead: “Prosecutor Bae, you’ve been at the Prosecutors’ Office two days now. How does it feel? Any difficulties at work?”

“So far, fine,” Bae Do-yoon said. “A little more files than expected — but exactly right for learning the flow.”

He said it evenly.

Kim Mi-ju heard it with some helplessness. That was not a little more files. An ordinary new prosecutor would already have begun complaining to his investigative officer. Bae Do-yoon had not only not complained, he had sorted the pile by handling direction — and that alone had already changed several attitudes in the office.

Bae Do-yoon ate two mouthfuls, then said suddenly: “There is one question, though, that bothers me.”

Kim Mi-ju lifted her head. “What question?”

“On my first day, the seniors in the office all seemed rather keen to make use of me. Copying papers, delivering materials, calling the police station — even files outside my charge, somebody wanted me to glance at them on the way past. Why has it gone so quiet these two days?”

Kim Mi-ju’s hand stilled, a fraction.

She glanced around by instinct, confirmed there were no familiar faces near, and only then lowered her voice.

“Because Prosecutor Bae is not what they imagined.”

“What do you mean?”

“Before you arrived, the office had already heard things. Seoul National University Law, first in the Training Institute, an excellent internship… everyone knew a very fine new prosecutor was coming.”

Bae Do-yoon’s chopsticks did not stop.

Kim Mi-ju went on: “But in a place like this, a new man who is too excellent does not only get welcomed. Some seniors are curious whether you are truly that good. Others hold that good grades do not mean knowing practice — and a newcomer especially is easily filed under knows only exams, not the rules.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled. “So they wanted to test me?”

“About that,” Kim Mi-ju said. “Refuse, and they can say you are too proud to respect your seniors. Fail, and they decide you are only a pretty record. Take all of it, and they will keep handing you more that is not yours.”

Bae Do-yoon set his chopsticks down; his tone cooled a shade.

“Called politely, it’s testing. Called honestly, it’s checking whether I’m easy to bully.”

Mm. Very Korea. Another kind of bullying.

Kim Mi-ju did not argue; she only smiled, a little awkwardly.

“The Prosecutors’ Office is a workplace too. And the senior-junior order here is plainer than in an ordinary company.”

Bae Do-yoon looked out of the window.

The restaurant came and went; people in suits carried trays past. Now and then someone recognized him, looked once more, and moved their eyes away.

He had come in with a simple plan: stay low at first; take no spotlight; make no enemies; learn the flow. But now it seemed that sometimes keeping low does not make trouble disappear. People can even read the silence as a soft persimmon.

Bae Do-yoon gave a light laugh. “I’m a little regretful now, actually.”

Kim Mi-ju asked: “Regretful of what?”

“Regretting that I was too quiet on day one. I thought speaking little and doing much would save trouble. Now I see: in here, silence can be read as permission to keep testing.”

Kim Mi-ju did not take that up.

Bae Do-yoon turned to her: “They don’t truly take me for the kind of man who steps back when shoved once, do they?”

Kim Mi-ju’s face went a little troubled.

“Not to that degree,” she said carefully. “At least most people now know Prosecutor Bae is not an exam-only man.”

“And the minority?”

Kim Mi-ju was silent for a moment.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “Officer Kim — since today’s meal is your treat, I’ll take it as after-service and ask one more question.”

Kim Mi-ju blinked. “After-service?”

“In the office, who holds the largest opinion of me?”

Kim Mi-ju did not answer at once.

She lowered her head and drank a mouthful of water, as if weighing whether the sentence should be said at all.

After a few seconds she said it, lightly: “Prosecutor Park Jung-hwan.”

Bae Do-yoon filed the name.

Recordday one, had the newcomer deliver materials; got no reaction; then watched the files come back handled flawlessly; silent these two days — for now. Only for now

A file kept open, not closed. She neither fanned flames nor chose sides; she named the risk from the correct seat, which for a week-old prosecutor is rarer than it sounds.

Watching his expression, Kim Mi-ju could not help adding one warning: “Prosecutor Bae — if someone truly keeps testing you, best not to clash head-on. Here it is not always the reasonable one who wins; words travel, and come back in another shape.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her once.

This officer was sharper than he had assumed.

He took up his chopsticks again. “Don’t worry. I’m not a man who enjoys slamming desks in an office.”

Kim Mi-ju had just begun to relax when she heard him add:

“But I also don’t like people keeping their hands on my desk.”

Kim Mi-ju did not know what to say to that. The sentence sounded perfectly calm, and was not a joke.

Near the end of lunch the two rose with their trays.

Bae Do-yoon had gone a few steps when he noticed a man not far off, looking their way.

Mid-thirties, suit pressed sharp, a coffee in his hand, two young investigative officers standing beside him. No expression on his face; only, when Bae Do-yoon’s glance reached him, a light moving-away of the eyes.

Kim Mi-ju saw him too.

Her voice went very low.

“That is Prosecutor Park Jung-hwan.”

Bae Do-yoon drew his gaze back and set his tray at the return.

“I see.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at him. “Prosecutor Bae?”

Bae Do-yoon smiled.

“Nothing. Only thinking that the afternoon’s work shouldn’t be too boring.”

· · ·

He had expected some trouble after lunch. Kim Mi-ju had said it plainly enough: this new prosecutor was not entirely unwatched in the office. Testing paused did not mean testing gone.

Yet, to his mild surprise, the whole afternoon stayed quiet.

No senior suddenly dropping non-owned papers on his desk; nobody sending him on errands under the senior-junior order. Everyone returned to ordinary working state: phones ringing, files read, an occasional glance past his desk and withdrawn.

Bae Do-yoon found the quiet unfamiliar, rather.

It was not the sound of trouble gone. It was the sound of something gathering.

He did not rush to judge. He went on with the morning’s unread files, and the sound of paper turned under his fingers.

At some point Kim Mi-ju came over with a new file in her arms.

Unlike the morning, her face now carried several degrees more caution.

“Prosecutor Bae.”

Bae Do-yoon looked up.

Kim Mi-ju set the file on his desk. “This is a case the chief prosecutor had me bring to you. You are to follow it first.”

“A case?” Bae Do-yoon was plainly surprised.

How many days had he been in the building? By the ordinary order, a new prosecutor first learns the flow, handles supporting work, shadows a senior’s case progress; truly touching a case alone comes later. Even “following” one does not usually arrive this fast.

Criminal Division 3 · the chief prosecutor’s instruction

Delivered by the assigned investigative officer, mid-afternoon, with caution in her face

Preliminary review: compile a handling opinion and submit it upward

Not a formal assignment of the case; not authority to decide its direction alone. Only: look first, sort first, opine first. And yet in practice a great many things begin exactly at look first — whoever touches the file first, whoever draws its thread first, whoever offers the first judgment, is the one the later direction tends to follow.

Bae Do-yoon looked at the file on the desk and did not open it at once. He asked, first: “What were the chief prosecutor’s exact words?”

Kim Mi-ju seemed to have expected the question. “The chief prosecutor said: have him do a preliminary review first, and compile a handling opinion to send up.”

“Only a preliminary review?”

“Yes.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

The sentence sounded safe. He set his pen down and opened the file’s first page.

The next second, his gaze stopped on the case name.

Petition materials concerning the suspected stalking of JYP entertainer Bae Suzy.

On soft persimmons and quiet afternoons

A persimmon that does not resist gets squeezed: that is the whole of the office’s first-week curriculum, and the wager in this chapter is only its friendlier exam. Notice what the quiet afternoon actually is — not peace, but a held breath. Buildings like this one do not stop testing a man; they merely change the instrument.
