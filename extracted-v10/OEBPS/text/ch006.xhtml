Chapter Six

Krystal, the Younger Sister

The morning after the Shilla

Jessica’s home · the living room

Two lies, one coat on a hanger, and a sister who counts micro-expressions

Jessica lifted her head and squeezed out a smile that cost her something. “Sorry for worrying you.”

“There was a business dinner last night. I drank more than I should have.”

“My phone died, so I never heard your calls.”

Krystal looked at her for several seconds. “A business dinner?”

Jessica nodded. “Mm.”

Krystal’s brows drew together. “You hate those rooms more than anyone. Don’t you?”

Jessica took off her coat and kept her tone as ordinary as she could make it.

“Hating them doesn’t get me out of them.”

“The label has collaborations to negotiate lately. I can’t hide from them forever.”

The sentence was half true.

Which was exactly why it would pass with the people who knew her best.

Krystal did not press. She watched her sister’s plainly too-pale face and let her voice soften.

“Drink less next time. Your stomach was never good to begin with.”

Something stung, suddenly, behind Jessica’s ribs.

With her back to her sister she hung the coat on the stand, and did not let Krystal see the face she almost lost control of for one second.

“I know,” she said quietly.

Krystal asked again, “Did you eat?”

“Not yet.”

“Shall I make you something?”

“No.” Jessica shook her head. “I’m a little tired. I want to rest first.”

Krystal watched her. “Eonni, are you really all right?”

Jessica turned around and smiled. “What could possibly be wrong with me?”

She even added, on purpose, in her usual slightly cold register:

“Don’t look at me like I’m about to collapse.”

Krystal twisted her mouth. “It’s not like I’m worried about you.”

She said it, but her eyes followed Jessica the whole way across the room.

Jessica did not explain further. The more she said, the more seams would show.

So she turned and walked toward her own room.

Before the door closed she heard Krystal mutter, small, out in the living room:

Through a door, mostly closed

Something is definitely wrong with her…

The handle stopped under her sister’s hand for exactly one second.

Then the door closed, and the two of them pretended about it, in opposite rooms.

A household runs on the things its women agree not to say.

· · ·

The familiar perfume, the wardrobe, the vanity, the bedside lamp, the shelves with their magazines and albums in orderly rows.

She did not shower first. She did not fall onto the bed.

Her body was tired nearly to breaking, and the sensible thing — the thing she should have been doing — was to wash last night off her skin and out of her memory. Instead she went to the shelves.

She needed to confirm one thing.

That man. I have definitely met him somewhere before.

Jessica pulled open the cabinet under the shelves and began to dig.

Old magazines came out one by one. Photobooks. Event commemorative books. Proof copies from brand shoots. Albums fans had given her. Envelopes of event photographs she had kept herself.

She turned pages fast. Books and albums went to the floor around her knees.

Shhk. Shhk. Shhk.

Pages rattling like an old dream being torn open again.

Not there.

Still not there.

Her hands grew faster; her temper thinned.

She did not know why she had to look. Because last night was too absurd, perhaps — she needed an earlier memory to prove he was not a stranger who had burst into her life from nowhere.

Or perhaps, somewhere under thought, she wanted to know whether the first man in her life had passed along its edge once before, and left no mark she could find.

At the very bottom of the shelf her hands found a thick album.

The cover was worn. It was from the years when she still stood often in her group’s activities: offline variety tapings, fan meetings, public events, a commemorative photograph for everything.

Jessica knelt on the carpet, laid the album open across her knees, and turned pages one by one.

The photographs held an older her. Back then she still stood inside Girls’ Generation’s halo — tired, unfree, but not yet so badly handled as later.

Page after page. Her fingertips hurried. Near the bottom of the album her patience was nearly gone.

Was it only my own misremembering?

Or was that face merely so unusual that it manufactured a sense of prior meeting?

She was about to close the album when her fingertips stopped.

It was a group photograph from an offline variety event. The backdrop board carried the program’s logo; beside it, a few members’ signature stickers.

In the photograph she stood next to a young man, smiling slightly at the camera.

The young man wore a plain white shirt and a black jacket. Clean features. And eyes that were not excited the way an ordinary fan’s eyes would have been.

He stood easily — even calmly. As if cooperating with an event, or as if observing everyone on the stage.

Jessica looked at that face. Her breathing slowed by degrees.

Two years younger than last night: greener, with less edge in him and less shadow. But the eyes had not changed. Calm. Awake. Carrying a steadiness his age should not have owned.

Her fingertips came to rest lightly on the photograph.

“No way…” she murmured. “It really was you.”

And in that instant the broken fragments of last night slid over the brief grouped photograph of two years before, and fit.

Two years before the hotel, in one bright minute

There were lights that day, and applause, and fans shouting names over a barrier.

The host asked him which member he wanted a photograph with. Somebody yelled Yoona. Somebody yelled Taeyeon. Somebody yelled her name.

He did not hesitate long. He looked at her once, and said, “Jessica.”

She walked over, stood politely at his side, and the photographer’s shutter closed.

Click. That was all of it.

Click.

An ordinary, negligible little event in an idol’s career. If last night had not put Bae Do-yoon in front of her again, she might never in this life have pulled this photograph out of the album.

But fate, in its foul humor, had handed the old print back to her precisely this way.

Fate had taken their photograph two years early, filed it under nothing at all, and waited for the night it would hand the print back.

Jessica looked down at the picture. The self in it smiled professionally. The Bae Do-yoon in it was just as calm. Between the two of them stood exactly the polite distance.

She reached out, suddenly, and lifted the photograph free of the album.

On the back of the print, in small pen

One line, in a hand not hers: the date of the event, and the venue. She read it the way a person reads a gravestone — twice, and slowly.

She thought of last night, in the room: the half-second pause in Bae Do-yoon’s look when it crossed her. At the time she had filed it as a man’s reaction to a celebrity.

Now, perhaps, it had not been.

But he had said nothing.

Why?

Because he did not want to say it?

Or because, in that room, saying it aloud would only have made everything uglier?

Jessica closed her fingers on the photograph, hard.

Emotion with no clean name came up: shame, anger, and a little vertigo.

She hated last night’s self, and last night’s Bae Do-yoon. Most of all she hated Lee Boo-jin, who had pushed the two of them into one room.

And yet, with this old photograph in her hand, she could not file Bae Do-yoon wholly under stranger, or wholly under harm.

Then, outside the door, Krystal’s voice.

“Eonni?”

Jessica surfaced hard. She folded the photograph into her palm by instinct. “What is it?”

“What are you looking for?” Krystal, through the door, puzzled. “I heard things falling all over the floor.”

Jessica looked at the floor of books and albums and made herself level.

“Nothing. An old file I needed.”

Two seconds of quiet. Then: “Do you need help?”

“No.” Jessica put softness into it. “Go watch your television. I’ll tidy up myself.”

Krystal did not leave at once. Through a door she seemed to want one more sentence.

In the end she only said, “Then remember to eat something later.”

Jessica answered low.

“Mm.”

Footsteps, fading.

Jessica looked at the photograph in her hand a long while without moving.

At last she let out a breath, took it into the bathroom with her, and washed — the night off her skin, his trace off her body, and what was still inside her down the drain where none of it could be kept.

A while later the water stopped.

Jessica stood at the mirror and wiped the fog away with the heel of her hand.

The mirror gave her back her face. Pale. Tired.

She looked at it a long time.

The mirror, itemized

FacePale, tired, and still holding

SleepNone worth the word

TellsOne, at the door: the handle stopped for a second

HeldOne night at the Shilla, one photograph two years old, one sister outside the bath door

Do not crack. There is still a younger sister.

A private reckoning, labeled as one: nothing in this room pinged, no ledger opened. It was only a woman counting what she must carry before breakfast.

She dressed, came out, and had just picked up the towel for her hair when Krystal’s voice arrived from outside — urgent, and bright.

“Eonni!”

Jessica’s hands stopped. “What is it?”

The door flew open. Krystal came in at a run, phone in her hand, face full of the unbelievable.

“Eonni, I just got a call.”

“Do you know what happened?”

Jessica looked at her sister’s lit eyes. “What?”

Krystal could not keep the excitement level in her voice. “The company called. They said they’re putting me on activities again soon!”

“And, and — they said two endorsement deals are in talks for me.”

“One apparel brand, one beauty.”

“Isn’t that strange? Has the sun started rising in the west?”

She said it laughing at herself, because even she could hear how absurd it was.

These past few months, since Jessica’s departure from the group, Krystal’s position at the company had gone visibly awkward. Nothing was said to her face. But fewer resources, stalled activities, lowered exposure — those things anyone could see.

Her being home today was not rest. It was that nobody had scheduled her for anything.

She had been set in a corner and, for the moment, forgotten. Not shelved outright; certainly not valued.

That state is the most grinding of all. Nobody tells you when it ends. Nobody tells you what the next step is. You wait — while the heat cools, while the fans leak away, while you begin to suspect it was never the stage that was wrong.

And now one phone call, and everything seemed to turn at once. Activities. Endorsements. New arrangements.

Krystal was excited, and puzzled: she could not see why the company had changed its mind so suddenly.

Jessica could. She had only not expected it this fast.

I thought they would wait a few days, at least.

Which means it was arranged beforehand. The moment I walked into that Shilla room, certain calls had perhaps already been made.

Krystal was happy now; the light was back in those eyes. Jessica had not seen that look on her sister’s face in a long time.

She pressed the ache in her chest down, crossed the room, and touched Krystal’s cheek. “Isn’t this good? You get to go back on stage.”

Krystal blinked. “It is good. But it’s strange, that’s all.” She frowned, a note of suspicion in it. “Why the sudden change of heart? I thought they were planning to wait me out until I quit on my own.”

Jessica’s chest tightened.

The sentence sounded like a joke. It was not entirely a joke. This sister of hers swallowed most of her grievances without saying them; unsaid was not the same as absent.

Jessica lowered her eyes. “Don’t talk nonsense.”

Krystal twisted her mouth. “It’s true. Before, every one of them played dead. Now suddenly activities, and ‘the company has always valued you.’” She shivered on purpose. “Gooseflesh, hearing it.”

She imitated the manager’s voice from the call:

“Soo-jung-ah, the company has been considering your direction for a while. There are a few good opportunities lately — we’d like you to prepare well for them.”

Then she rolled her eyes at herself, first.

“Where were they all before?”

Jessica laughed a little, truly; and the laugh was gone before it reached her eyes.

Krystal looked at her sister and leaned in, suddenly, a fraction closer.

“Eonni — do you know something?”

Jessica’s heart jumped; she looked up. “What could I know?”

Krystal held her gaze.

“You’ve been strange all day. Strange from the moment you came in — your color is bad, your words are wrong.”

“Now the company calls me out of nowhere, and you aren’t even surprised.”

“Are you—”

“Soo-jung.” Jessica cut in. “Stop chasing tangles. If the company gives you work, do the work well.”

“When an opportunity comes, take it. Don’t let unnecessary suspicion cost you anything.”

Krystal frowned. “But…”

“There is no but.” Jessica reached out and smoothed the hair at her sister’s forehead. “You’ve wanted the stage back all along. Then go back. The rest — leave it to me.”

The sentence landed, and both of them were quiet for a second.

Krystal looked at her sister. It struck her that the words weighed more than words, like a promise being set down somewhere.

“Eonni…”

She would have asked more. And Jessica’s phone rang.

Jessica picked it up and looked at the caller display.

Kwon Young-il.

Seeing the name, her expression went subtle.

In the old days she would not have taken his call in front of Krystal. She knew exactly how much her sister hated Kwon Young-il. Hated him enough that the name alone changed her face.

And sure enough: Krystal saw the screen, and her face went cold in an instant. The excitement of a minute ago, gone.

She folded her arms, tone acid:

“Well. If it isn’t the busiest man in Korea.”

Jessica said nothing. The phone rang on.

Krystal watched her. “If you’re taking it, take it outside. You always did.”

There was grievance in the sentence, and Jessica heard it.

Every call from him, in the old days, had been taken on the balcony, away from her sister. She had told herself it was to reduce conflict.

Now, thinking it over: in a sister’s eyes, perhaps it had looked less like peace-keeping and more like taking his side.

Kwon Young-il

The name on a screen that ruins a younger sister’s mood

Business — the brand’s other half, on paper and elsewhere

Known to this book so far only as an incoming call and a balcony habit

Jessica’s, in the way the industry means it: partner, investor, the man Krystal never forgave. He has not appeared yet; his ringtone has.

The call

Incoming · Kwon Young-il · still ringing

The screen lights the room’s bad mood. Nobody answers. A younger sister’s crossed arms keep the old time.

Somewhere on the other end, a man with three theories about why her resources just moved.

A note on half-truths

The lie that survives a family is never a whole cloth. It is a true sentence with one beam removed: there really was a label to protect, and there really was a night she cannot describe. Krystal did not fail to catch the lie; she caught it, priced it, and let it pass, because the alternative was to demand the beam.

That is what the sisters in this book do instead of shouting. They audit each other’s faces and call it concern.
