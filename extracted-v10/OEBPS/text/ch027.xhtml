Chapter Twenty-Seven

Prosecutor Bae, Do You Have a Girlfriend?

The same afternoon · the meeting room, cleared

Two people, one closed door, one half-melted iced americano

Where the press version is formally retired and the real one asked to sit down

Only Bae Do-yoon and Bae Suzy remained in the room.

Bae Suzy leaned back against the chair, lifted her iced americano, drank a sip, and set the cup down before looking at him.

“Prosecutor Bae — you asked everyone to leave. For a moment just now I truly thought I had some serious problem.”

Bae Do-yoon pushed the materials a little to the side.

“You do not have a serious problem.”

“Then why talk alone?”

“Because just now you were speaking far too much like a press release.”

Bae Suzy stilled a moment, then laughed.

“Was it that obvious?”

“Very obvious,” Bae Do-yoon said. “Timing accurate, wording safe, emotion stable — and you even took care of the manager’s and the legal head’s feelings along the way. If this had been a press conference, your performance just now was good.”

Bae Suzy lowered her head and gave one laugh.

“And if this was a case discussion?”

“Information content: on the low side.”

Bae Suzy looked at him, as if amused by exactly this sentence.

“Prosecutor Bae, do you usually talk to people like this too? You must make enemies easily.”

“Which is why I usually say it only when necessary.”

“And I count as necessary now?”

“You count.”

She leaned against the chair back, her gaze resting on his face, staying there two seconds.

“Then that’s rather an honor.”

Bae Do-yoon did not take up that probing line; he only lowered his head and closed his pen.

Bae Suzy watched the motion, her smile a little more plain.

“Were you just pretending not to understand?”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his eyes.

“Understand what?”

“Nothing,” Bae Suzy said, lifting her coffee and drinking again. “It seems prosecutors are indeed skilled at avoiding dangerous statements.”

Bae Do-yoon said, lightly: “It is a professional capability.”

“Artists need that capability too,” Bae Suzy joined at once. “Only, we call it controlling the room.”

“You controlled it well just now.”

“Thank you for the praise.”

“Not praise,” Bae Do-yoon said. “Pointing out a problem.”

Bae Suzy paused, then laughed out loud.

“You are really interesting, you know. Your speech isn’t warm, but it isn’t cold either. Every sentence sounds perfectly businesslike — and yet on reflection, it seems not entirely businesslike.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Is this also professional analysis?”

“No,” she said, very naturally. “Only a personal impression.”

Having said it, Bae Suzy stirred the ice in her cup lightly with the straw; the ice touched the cup wall with one clear note.

After a while, Bae Do-yoon said: “Then I may take it as a positive evaluation.”

Bae Suzy lifted her eyes and smiled.

“You may.”

The air, after these few sentences, grew a little subtle.

Both of them were perfectly clear-headed; both knew there was a little warmth in the other’s words beyond ordinary work relations — and neither punctured it.

What the closed door makes possible

Source: two participants, no manager, no legal head, no record

Permittedthe real state, unperformed: complaints, annoyance, and the ranking of standard answers, spoken aloud at last

Observedboth parties note a warmth beyond work relations in the other’s wording; neither punctures it; both remain clear-headed about not puncturing it

Pendingone question, declared in advance as unrelated to the case, with an escape hatch offered and declined

Room status: door closed. Corridor status: one manager, second lap.

Bae Do-yoon drew the topic back onto its rails.

“Now there is no manager, no legal head, and no record. You don’t have to speak the press-release version.”

Bae Suzy leaned against the chair back and thought about it.

“Then shall I tell the truth?”

“Mm.”

“The truth is — I am not as frightened as they imagine.”

She said it without forcing bravado; it was as if, finally not having to care for the worries outside the door, she could simply set her real state out on the table.

“I know this matter is dangerous, and I know that this time the other side mentioned a real-world position — this isn’t ordinary random messaging. But if you ask whether I can’t sleep every day now, or am too scared to go out — no, not that either.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“I can see that.”

Bae Suzy looked at him.

“What do you see?”

“You are not failing to hold up,” Bae Do-yoon said. “You are refusing to be seen by those people outside as failing to hold up.”

Bae Suzy’s easy expression paused; the sentence had plainly struck true.

She looked down at her cup, and only after a good while said:

“Yes.”

She paused, then lifted her head to look at him again.

“Actually I know everyone means well. The manager worries a great deal; the company is very tense. But the more tense they are, the less I can show too much annoyance. If I say I’m sick of it, they’ll think my pressure is already enormous; if I say I’m fine, they’ll think I’m forcing myself to hold up.”

“So just now you chose to say so-so.”

“Exactly,” Bae Suzy said, opening her hands a little. “What a safe answer. It makes me look neither dismissive nor on the verge of collapse. Top three of the artist standard-answer ranking.”

The artist standard-answer ranking · as recited on request

01“I’m fine.” — deployed today, twice, under company observation

02“I’ll work hard to adjust, so please don’t worry.” — recited without thinking, on demand, full intonation

03The third — never disclosed in this room; presumed retired from active service

Comparative edition, Prosecutors’ Office: “I’ll cooperate fully.” “I only want to explain the matter clearly.” “I really had no other meaning.” Verdict of the listener: indeed very much alike. Conclusion: every industry has its standard answers; which is why everyone was asked out of the room.

Bae Do-yoon asked: “What are the other two?”

Bae Suzy answered without a moment’s thought.

“I’ll work hard to adjust, so please don’t worry.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent two seconds. “Indeed very much alike.”

Bae Suzy laughed.

“See — you know it too.”

“I’ve heard them too often.”

“You often hear people say them too?”

“The Prosecutors’ Office versions are: ‘I’ll cooperate fully.’ ‘I only want to explain the matter clearly.’ ‘I really had no other meaning.’”

Bae Suzy stilled first, then laughed until her shoulders moved, lightly.

“It sounds as if every industry has its own standard answers.”

“Which is why I asked them all out,” Bae Do-yoon said. “You don’t have to keep performing here.”

Bae Suzy’s smile remained, but her eyes grew a little softer.

“The word performing, from your mouth — a little strange.”

“Used wrongly?”

“No. Very accurate,” she said, seriously. “It’s just that it’s too accurate, so it’s strange. People outside this industry rarely put it that way.”

Bae Do-yoon knew Irene; of course he knew. But he did not explain too much; he only said:

“Just now, sitting there, before answering each question you glanced once at your manager. Not for help — to check whether he would grow more tense.”

Bae Suzy stilled; she had not expected him to have noticed this.

“Do you observe this finely?”

“It is work.”

“Only work?” Having asked, she laughed first herself, as if making a casual joke.

But the question still made the air pause half a beat.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her and said, lightly: “At present, yes.”

Bae Suzy blinked.

The answer was very restrained — but not entirely without margin.

She looked at him a few seconds, then suddenly turned her head aside and laughed.

“Prosecutor Bae, answering like that — it’s very easy to make people misunderstand.”

Bae Do-yoon’s expression stayed calm.

“Your question just now also didn’t look entirely like one wanting to avoid being misunderstood.”

Bae Suzy’s hand around the cup paused, faintly; then her smile deepened.

“So you weren’t failing to understand.”

“I merely don’t rush to catch it.”

“Why not?”

“In case you are only professional habit.”

Bae Suzy looked at him; after a long moment she laughed out, low. She leaned back against the chair, her gaze resting on the window outside.

“Prosecutor Bae — if my manager heard you say that, he might truly knock and come in.”

“Which is why the door is closed.”

“That sounds more dangerous.”

“Then shall I open it now?” Bae Do-yoon said, as if truly about to stand.

Bae Suzy raised a hand at once.

“Don’t don’t don’t — I was joking.”

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her and sat back.

Bae Suzy watched the motion and smiled, shaking her head.

“You really… sometimes it’s hard to judge whether you’re doing it on purpose.”

“You may take it as not.”

“And if I want to take it as yes?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, tone still level.

“Then it shows your state right now is indeed quite good.”

Bae Suzy stilled one second, then laughed again.

After laughing she drew a light breath.

“Mm. My state is indeed quite good.”

She finally let the topic fall back onto the matter itself.

“Honestly, my biggest problem right now isn’t fear. It’s annoyance.”

“Really annoying. An artist has so little time of her own to begin with — schedules, shoots… so many things; even sitting down quietly for a cup of coffee has to be counted into the timetable. And then I have to spare attention for some inexplicable person.”

She frowned. “By what right of his?”

Bae Do-yoon did not interrupt.

Bae Suzy went on:

“He writes those words because he wants me to know he saw, doesn’t he? He wants me to think of him next time I get out of a car, to think of him when I choose clothes, to think of him whether I look left or right. In the end, he wants to stuff himself into my life.”

She looked at Bae Do-yoon; in her eyes there was no fragility — more displeasure, and clear-sightedness.

“I won’t let him succeed.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded once.

Bae Suzy said: “If it were only insults, or sending strange words, the company could handle it. But he speaks too specifically. I can be unafraid — but I cannot pretend this isn’t dangerous.”

She paused, then added one more line:

“And I hate that the moment people hear the word harassment, they automatically picture me as something very pitiable.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“You don’t like being cast as the victim?”

Bae Suzy thought a while.

“Not that I dislike it. Facts are facts; I won’t deny that out of bravado. It’s only — victims also come in many states, don’t they? Some are very afraid; some collapse; some are angry. I, right now, am very annoyed, very angry, and I also think this matter must be solved.”

She lifted her chin a little, her tone carrying a thread of self-mockery.

“But the way my manager looks at me, it’s as if I’ll fail to hold up in the next second.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “From his angle, you are the person he is responsible for.”

“I know. So I can’t blame him either,” Bae Suzy sighed. “He works very hard. It’s only — the moment he works hard, I have to turn around and comfort him. Look: in the end, I’m the one doing overtime.”

Hearing this, the corner of Bae Do-yoon’s mouth moved, almost imperceptibly.

“That formulation is rather accurate.”

“Right?” Bae Suzy said, like one who had finally found a person in agreement. “This morning I was already harassed; this afternoon I still have to comfort the manager, cooperate with legal, answer questions, control my expression. Thinking about it now, I should apply for overtime pay.”

“Apply to whom?”

“To the person harassing me.”

“I don’t recommend private collection.”

“Then to the company?”

“That, I don’t manage.”

Bae Suzy laughed.

“Prosecutor Bae passes the buck very fast.”

“Duty boundaries are clear.”

“And does your duty include listening to me complain?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “If complaining helps me understand your real state — it can be included.”

Bae Suzy rested her chin on her hand, her eyes curving a little.

“Then am I assisting the investigation right now?”

“It may be understood so.”

“Then if I complain a little more, does that count as cooperating rather actively?”

“We look at quality, not quantity.”

Bae Suzy lowered her head and gave one laugh.

“You are really suited to being a prosecutor.”

“Thank you.”

“But not very suited to comforting people, oh.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his eyes. “Do you need comforting?”

His tone was not ambiguous. But because there were only two people in the room, and because the conversation just now already carried a thread of something like probing, the sentence suddenly acquired another layer of meaning as it sounded.

Bae Suzy looked at him, the smile in her eyes slowly deepening.

“If I said I need it — would you comfort me?”

Bae Do-yoon did not answer at once.

He looked at her, and only after a few seconds said: “I can try. No guarantee of effect.”

Bae Suzy bit the straw lightly, as if holding in laughter.

“Then never mind. If you comforted too formally, I might want to laugh even more.”

“For instance?” Bae Do-yoon asked.

Bae Suzy cleared her throat and imitated his perfectly straight-faced tone:

“Ms. Bae, please maintain emotional stability. Relevant risks will be eliminated step by step within statutory procedure.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“I wouldn’t say that.”

“Really?”

“Probably. My emotional intelligence isn’t that low.”

Bae Suzy could not hold it; she laughed out loud.

Outside the door there seemed to come a little footstep.

She lowered her voice at once, looking toward the door. “Is my manager pacing outside again?”

Bae Do-yoon tilted his ear a moment.

“If the footsteps belong to the same person — that is the second lap.”

Bae Suzy laughed, low.

“He really is extraordinary. When I go out later, if I’m laughing too happily, he may suspect you said something strange to me.”

Bae Do-yoon said, lightly: “Then you may control your expression.”

“Didn’t you just say I don’t have to perform?”

“After going out is counted separately.”

Bae Suzy looked at him and suddenly said: “You are very good at pulling people back to reality.”

“Is that a merit or a flaw?”

“Depends,” she thought about it. “Just now, a merit.”

Not failing to hold up — refusing to be seen failing to hold up. The distinction, entered here exactly as she drew it.

Bae Suzy looked at him a while, then suddenly said:

“Prosecutor Bae.”

“Mm?”

“May I ask you one question unrelated to the case?”

As if afraid he might misread it, Bae Suzy smiled and added first:

“Of course, if you feel it inconvenient, you may not answer. I only suddenly grew a little curious.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Ask.”

Bae Suzy’s fingers rubbed the cup wall lightly; her tone sounded very casual — but her eyes kept on him the whole while.

“You are this excellent, and good-looking besides. Do you have a girlfriend?”

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her, somewhat taken aback.

“This question is indeed not within today’s questioning scope.”

“Which is why I declared it in advance just now,” Bae Suzy said, smiling. “Unrelated to the case. You may not say if you don’t want to — after all, I’m only curious.”

She said it very naturally, as if truly only asking in passing; and yet the way she looked at Bae Do-yoon did not seem entirely in passing.

Bae Do-yoon lowered his eyes to the materials at his hand; a moment later, neither deflecting the topic nor fudging it:

“No.”

“Currently single.”

The sentence out, Bae Suzy’s eyes lit, quite plainly, once.

In that instant her probing gaze turned suddenly brighter — as if she had heard an answer more satisfactory than the one she had predicted.

She quickly lowered her head and drank a sip of coffee, as if to press the reaction down with the motion.

But the cup had just touched her lips when she could not help laughing again.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Does this answer surprise you very much?”

Bae Suzy set the cup down and lifted her eyes to him.

“Not surprise.”

“Then what?”

She thought about it, and then said: “It’s that I feel… a little pity.”

Terms of the closed door · agreed without being written, kept without being signed

I. The press-release version is retired within this room. The real state may be spoken aloud; annoyance is admissible; the standard-answer ranking may be recited in full, third place excepted.

II. Complaints are admitted as cooperation. Measurement: quality, not quantity. The complainant’s ranking of herself: assisting the investigation, rather actively.

III. The comforting clause: attempt permitted; effect not guaranteed; the formally worded comfort (Ms. Bae, please maintain emotional stability…) is barred from exhibit and from practice, the comforter’s emotional intelligence being, on his own estimate, not that low.

IV. The door stays closed. Knock threshold: the manager’s suspicion of strange words. Opening offered once, declined thrice (don’t don’t don’t).

It’s that I feel… a little pity.

On standard answers and closed doors

Every industry keeps its top three: I’m fine; I’ll work hard to adjust, so please don’t worry; and a third no one admits to using. A prosecutor empties a room for no sentimental reason: press releases carry no evidence, and a witness who has spent all morning taking care of other people’s worry deserves one hour of not performing. What walked in when the door closed was not fear — she had already filed fear under manageable — but annoyance, and the far more dangerous discovery that this man reads the glance she throws her manager before every answer. He called her state good; she called his answer easy to misunderstand; both called it work, and neither believed that entirely. And then, with an escape hatch offered and declined, she asked the question that is never in the questioning scope — and the room, which had held a stalker’s four screenshots an hour earlier, held its breath for two words: currently single.
