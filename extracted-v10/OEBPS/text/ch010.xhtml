Chapter Ten

Jeon Bo-ram Wants to Help

That night · after his own door closed

His room · the table, one supermarket bag, one opened beer

Curiosity, of the prosecutorial kind

Back inside, Bae Do-yoon set the supermarket bag on the table.

The first day at work had cost more energy than he had budgeted for. The air inside Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office was not much lighter than the air outside it.

He opened the beer and tipped his head back for a long pull. The cold liquid slid down his throat, and only then did his head feel marginally clear.

Thinking of the small woman in the corridor hugging her grocery bag, the one who had very nearly filed him under stalking pervert, he laughed out loud once.

“Jeon Bo-ram…”

He picked up his phone and typed the name into the search box.

Search · Jeon Bo-ram

Entered on a phone that carries almost no private searches at all

First wave of results — T-ara · Jeon Bo-ram · the bullying incident · Ryu Hwayoung

Second wave — apology · boycott · anti-fans · leaving the group

His finger paused over the screen.

Of course he had heard of T-ara. Even a man who is nobody’s fan cannot fully avoid that name. A few years ago T-ara stood among the hottest girl groups in Korea: Bo Peep Bo Peep, Roly-Poly, Lovey-Dovey — songs he had never deliberately listened to and had still heard from streets, malls and variety-show clips. Back then they were very nearly the top girl group in the country: high popularity, endless topics, stage after stage.

And then a single so-called incident dragged the group down from that height by main force.

He drank his beer and scrolled through old news.

The origin of the matter was not complicated. The friction between T-ara and their former member Ryu Hwayoung became public. After that, the internet filled with claims: in-group bullying, members being ostracized, the whole group turning on one person.

Old reports · forum exposés · fan-compiled timelines · anonymous staff statements · so-called insider testimony — the whole pile, stacked

The so-called bullying incident: what the pile actually contains

Some said several T-ara members ostracized Ryu Hwayoung over a long period. Some said the details proved it: stages, shows, social-media posts, all readable as evidence of isolation. Others said the reverse — that Ryu Hwayoung’s own ability fell short, that her attitude was the problem, that she failed to cooperate in group activities, and that she may even have ridden public opinion to bite back at the group.

And what the truth actually was: nowhere in the screen is there a settled answer. The more he scrolled, the deeper his brow folded. Too messy. Not too little information — too much. So much that nearly everyone could find, inside it, exactly the part they wanted to believe.

Those on Ryu Hwayoung’s side could produce evidence of the members’ coldness, exclusion and sarcasm. Those on T-ara’s side could produce evidence of her slacking, lying and working the opinion weather. Each side held its own version, and neither would set it down.

Bae Do-yoon set the beer down and looked at the pile the way he looked at work.

From a prosecutor’s angle, all of it was troublesome — because most of it was not evidence in any real sense. It was emotional material: fragments, screenshots, hearsay, secondhand retellings, anonymous leaks. Such material can stir people up; it does not necessarily prove facts. And yet the arena of public opinion does not require a complete chain of evidence. It requires one sufficiently stimulating keyword.

And in Korea, the word bullying is obviously stimulating enough. School bullying. Workplace bullying. Senior-junior bullying. This society carries a near-instinctive sensitivity to the collective squeezing the weak. The moment someone stands up and says I was bullied, many people’s first reaction is not to wait for an investigation; it is to step into the victim’s place at once.

They need no court. They need no prosecutor.

What a prosecutor counts, and what a comment section counts

The internet is the courtroom. The trending search is the verdict. The comment section is the execution ground.

T-ara’s explanations at the time did almost nothing. In that opinion weather, explaining had already lost its meaning: the net did not want to hear what they said, it wanted to hear them admit guilt.

Silence is a guilty conscience. Explanation is sophistry. Tears are performance. Ordinary activity is a lack of reflection.

From the moment the label bully was stuck on them, every act of theirs could be re-read as evidence of guilt. And once the public has chosen its script — victim here, bullies there — everyone begins filling in details, because a story with a cast is easier to hold than a file with holes.

He scrolled on, and the old comments surfaced with their like counts still attached.

Old comments, preserved exactly as archived

1st floor · several thousand likes“A bullying group like this — why haven’t they disbanded yet?”

2nd floor“Seeing their faces makes me sick.”

3rd floor“Ryu Hwayoung is too pitiful. T-ara, all of you, get out of the entertainment industry.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at those lines for a while.

And what rose in his head was not the group on a stage. It was Jeon Bo-ram in the corridor an hour ago.

The corridor, one hour ago

Cap pressed low. Grocery bag hugged to the chest. Tense to the edge of shaking, and still forcing a level voice to ask whether he had been following her.

That figure refuses, stubbornly, to connect itself to the labels in the comment section: venomous perpetrator, bullying-group member.

Of course one meeting proves nothing. People are complicated. A victim can lie; a perpetrator can be gentle. How a person stands in a corridor does not prove how she stood inside her group.

But equally: a few screenshots, a few rumors, a few comments do not prove a person’s entire life.

He leaned back on the sofa and drank slowly.

Then his mind wandered to a file he had read at the office today, and the two cases laid themselves side by side without being asked.

A file from today’s pile, still warm

AccusationA suspect identified by his neighbor as a thief

Basis offeredHe keeps to himself; his finances are poor; he once quarreled with the victim

Evidence he actually stoleAlmost none in the file

Much of what people call reasonable is only a story assembled out of prejudice.

He likes evidence: a clear timeline, verifiable witness statements, physical evidence consistent front to back, a chain of facts that corroborates itself. Not a crowd standing on a high place, imagining itself justice, throwing stones downward.

The T-ara matter had plainly gone past the range of an ordinary in-group quarrel. It had become a collective discharge. As for the original facts — those had stopped mattering somewhere along the way.

He scrolled to the later reports. Group schedules reduced. Advertisements replaced. Show appearances cut in the edit. Performance venues going cold on them. The members meeting malicious comments, boycotts, abuse. A once-brilliant top girl group falling into the valley inside a short season.

He looked at the stage screenshots. The lights very bright. The girls in their broadcast outfits, standing under the spotlights. And beneath the pictures, the comment section: mockery and cursing, wall to wall.

He thought again of that small face.

If a person lives inside that environment for years, what does she become?

Vigilant. Sensitive. Afraid of strangers. Unable to trust easily. To the point where meeting a same-floor resident at her own door produces, as a first response, the question of whether he is following her.

Seen that way, her reaction tonight was not so hard to understand after all.

He set the phone down and took a chip.

Crunch.

He had only meant to look his new neighbor up out of idle curiosity. And the more he looked, the less this resembled entertainment news. Too much in it was familiar to him: opinion labels, insufficient evidence, preconclusion. And the social punishment that follows a conviction, which a person then carries long after.

The difference is only this: a conviction in the Prosecutors’ Office requires legal procedure. A conviction on the internet requires only anger.

He killed the screen. He sat silent for a while, then smiled a little.

“What a pain.”

A female idol neighbor who has just moved in. An old opinion incident that has not fully sunk. A group of people the whole internet once tried. And a bullying story nobody can say is true or false.

Bae Do-yoon had not planned to touch the entertainment world’s business. He is, after all, only a prosecutor who started this week; the office’s piles and its seniors already fill both hands.

But now, suddenly, he was curious. Not curious whether Jeon Bo-ram was the person the internet described. Curious, in that huge and noisy trial, how much of what was said was true — and how much was only the truth people preferred to believe.

Past nine in the evening · freshly showered

His door · peephole first, door second

A small figure, hands clasped in front of her, in pale blue

Around nine, he had just come out of the shower and was about to work through the few documents he had not finished reading in the daytime.

He has no night life to speak of. His phone carries almost no private contacts; only the office group chat pops now and then with a senior’s message. The living-room light was on; the television played the news as background noise.

He sat on the sofa with a can of beer, weighing whether to go in early tomorrow to learn the office’s files, when several knocks came at the door.

Knock. Knock. Knock.

Not loud. If anything, careful.

Bae Do-yoon looked up.

At this hour — who?

He has no friends in Seoul, and he ordered no delivery. Building management? Or the wrong door?

He set the beer on the low table, went to the door, and looked through the peephole first.

A small figure stood outside.

He stilled for a beat. Jeon Bo-ram.

She stood at his door with both hands clasped awkwardly in front of her, in a set of pale blue loungewear; her hair not hidden under a cap tonight but hanging loose. She looked several degrees less guarded than in the daytime, and several degrees softer.

Doorlight inventory, second time tonight

Loungewear pale blue, soft, a size generous on a small frame

Hair loose, out of the cap for once

Hands clasped in front of her, fingers working at each other

Bearing less vigilance than the corridor version; more softness

Off duty twice over: no cap, no coat, no stage — a neighbor at a neighbor’s door.

Standing in a doorway like that, she gives the unfair illusion of a child wearing an adult’s clothes — an observation he kept inside his own skull, where it belonged.

Plate set from the doorway scene, Chapter 10 — pale blue loungewear, hair loose

He opened the door.

Hearing it, Jeon Bo-ram looked up at once. Seeing that he was not yet asleep, her eyes plainly brightened, and a little relief came onto her face.

“Um… I’m really sorry to disturb you this late.”

She said it and started, by reflex, to bow.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “What’s wrong?”

Jeon Bo-ram pressed her lips together, a little embarrassed.

“It’s just… the light in my room broke.”

Bae Do-yoon waited for the rest.

Her voice went smaller. “I do have a spare bulb. But… I can’t reach it.”

By the end of the sentence she was embarrassed at herself.

Bae Do-yoon’s glance went, without permission, downward by a few centimeters. Jeon Bo-ram is indeed not tall; in loungewear at a doorway she completes the illusion entirely. He did not say any of this aloud. Said aloud, it would very likely earn him hatred.

He only nodded. “Do you have a stepladder?”

Jeon Bo-ram blinked. “Huh?”

“A chair works too,” Bae Do-yoon said. “I can’t exactly fly up there and change it.”

She caught up with him and nodded fast. “Yes. Yes, there’s a chair.”

“Then let’s go.” He picked his phone off the entry cabinet as he spoke.

Jeon Bo-ram had not expected him to be this brisk about it, and bent again to thank him. “Thank you. Really, thank you.”

Bae Do-yoon closed his door and followed her the few steps next door.

She opened up and stood aside, a little stiffly, to let him in.

“Please, come in.”

He changed into the slippers she handed him and went into the living room.

It was his first time inside Jeon Bo-ram’s home. The two units share almost the same layout, and yet the feeling could not have been more different. His own room still carried a new mover’s coldness: furniture placed square, traces of living almost nonexistent. Hers had already gathered soft things. Cushions on the sofa. A few magazines open at the table’s edge. A corner stacked with cartons not yet fully unpacked. From the kitchen direction, faintly, the smell of food. And in the air a light, clean fragrance.

Bae Do-yoon drew in a breath through his nose.

An entertainer’s room, indeed. Moved in days ago, and it already smells more lived-in than mine.

Jeon Bo-ram noticed the motion and asked, a little nervously: “Is there a strange smell?”

He came back to himself and said, without a change of expression: “No. It smells better than my room.”

She stilled — and then could not help a small laugh. The laugh was short, but it took some of the tightness out of her whole person.

“The bedroom is this way.” She led him in.

The living room and kitchen lights were on; only the bedroom stood dark. From the doorway one could make out no more than the blurred outline of the bed.

Jeon Bo-ram stopped at the bedroom door and explained, a little awkwardly:

“I meant to call building management tomorrow. But… the room is too dark at night. I can’t sleep well like that.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her once. She said it lightly, but the unease under the lightness was not hard to hear. He did not ask more; he only reached out and took the spare bulb she offered.

“Is the bulb the same model?”

“It should be,” she said quickly. “Management gave it me when I moved in.”

He opened the package and checked. An ordinary bulb. Nothing complicated.

“Where’s the chair?”

“Here.” She hurried to bring the dining chair over.

She meant to help steady it, but Bae Do-yoon had already moved it one-handed under the light, tested its stability, and stepped up onto it.

“Is the switch off?”

“Off,” Jeon Bo-ram nodded.

“And the breaker?”

Jeon Bo-ram blinked. “I need to turn the breaker off too?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

Jeon Bo-ram lowered her head at once. “I didn’t know…”

He sighed and stepped down off the chair.

“Electrical things — if you don’t know, don’t touch them. It’s very dangerous.”

Standing beside him, she looked uncannily like a primary-school child being lectured by a teacher.

“I’m sorry.”

“I didn’t ask you to apologize,” Bae Do-yoon said. “Where’s the breaker?”

“Next to the entry.”

He went and cut the bedroom’s circuit, confirmed it safe, and only then stepped back up onto the chair.

Jeon Bo-ram stood below, both hands holding the chair’s back by instinct.

Bae Do-yoon looked down at her. “You don’t need to be this tense.”

“I’m afraid you’ll fall,” she said, small.

His hands did not stop.

“Falling from this height shouldn’t make the news.”

Jeon Bo-ram blinked, then caught up with the fact that he was joking. She laughed despite herself.

“Even so, it wouldn’t be good.”

“True.” Bae Do-yoon unscrewed the old bulb. “The headline might read: man enters female idol’s home at night, falls unexpectedly from chair.”

Jeon Bo-ram’s face went red at once. “It would not!”

The new bulb went in with a small, exact turn. He stepped down, flipped the wall switch, and the bedroom filled with light.

Jeon Bo-ram squinted into it, then smiled — the second real laugh of the night, and the wider of the two.

At her door, a minute later, she stopped him. Her hands had gone back to working at each other.

“Prosecutor-nim. At the supermarket… your bag was beer, chips, and that chicken thing.”

“Do you eat like that every day?”

Bae Do-yoon considered the strength of his defense and offered none.

“I cook,” she said, quickly, before she could lose her nerve. “Not well. But edible.”

“So. If you ever want a real meal — or anything, really — knock. I’m right next door.”

She looked down at her own hands, then up again, earnest to the point of solemnity.

“You helped me tonight. I want to help with something too. Please let me help with something.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her for a second longer than the sentence required.

A person the internet convicted without a trial, offering help with no trial at all.

“All right,” he said. “Next time my bulb goes out, I’ll knock first.”

She laughed again, and went in, and her door clicked shut.

The corridor went quiet. Bae Do-yoon stood a moment at his own door: a rookie prosecutor, an idol neighbor, an old case with no verdict anywhere in it.

What a pain, he thought — and realized, unlocking his door, that he did not mind the trouble at all.

On verdicts without courts

This book reports the 2012 controversy the way the record stands: claims from one side, claims from the other, and no court anywhere that settled which were true. It will not adjudicate what no court adjudicated. What the chapter borrows from the affair is only a prosecutor’s question, and it is the same question this whole volume keeps asking in different rooms: what would actually count as evidence here — and who decided the answer before asking?
