Chapter Twenty-Nine

The Extremely Curious Kim Mi-ju

The same afternoon · JYP, fifth floor

The meeting-room door, opening at last

One manager standing straight, one clerk looking up from her phone, one witness with something uncollected in her eyes

When the meeting-room door was opened, the manager straightened his body at once, as if finally waiting out the end.

Kim Mi-ju also lifted her head from her phone screen, looked first at Bae Do-yoon, and then, without meaning to, moved her gaze to the face of Bae Suzy behind him.

Bae Do-yoon looked no different from usual: shirt collar neat, the materials in his hand already sorted, expression calm.

But on Bae Suzy’s side, Kim Mi-ju’s attention stayed one beat longer.

The smile on her face was still natural — even more composed than before; the cap brim pressed low, only a pair of eyes showing.

Yet in those eyes there seemed to remain a little emotion not quite gathered back in. Not obvious. And still it made a person feel, for no nameable reason, that something was off.

The manager stepped forward and asked her in a low voice:

“All talked out? Why so long? I thought they were adding interrogation records inside.”

Bae Suzy lifted a hand and pressed the cap brim a little lower; her tone sounded light:

“Nothing much. Just confirmed a few details, so a few extra words. Don’t make that face like I was interrogated alone — as if I could recant my statement in half an hour inside.”

The manager, choked by her, could not help laughing:

“You still have mood for jokes. Then good — as long as nothing went wrong.”

Bae Do-yoon now nodded slightly and said to the manager:

“That’s all for today. If there is anything needing supplementary explanation later, I will contact you through formal procedure. I won’t disturb you privately.”

“Yes, thank you for your trouble,” the manager answered quickly, then added one more polite line: “Today really troubled you both to make this trip.”

Bae Suzy also turned her head to look at Bae Do-yoon; her eyes curved a little; her tone very proper:

“Thank you for today, Prosecutor Bae. If any of my answers just now were not accurate enough, I will cooperate again later if needed.”

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her and said, lightly:

“You have already cooperated very well.”

“The information you provided is detailed enough. It will help us organize the timeline.”

The words themselves held no problem at all. And yet Kim Mi-ju, standing beside them, felt precisely that it was not only that.

Because after hearing that sentence, Bae Suzy’s gaze fell on Bae Do-yoon’s face and stayed there one brief instant — as if confirming something, or as if a question in her heart had not quite been set down.

That glance was extremely restrained; another person would probably have overlooked it. But Kim Mi-ju was precisely the kind who remembers details.

Before she could think more, Bae Do-yoon had already withdrawn his gaze and turned toward the elevator, saying to her in passing:

“Let’s go. Back at the office we still need to organize today’s record.”

“Ah — oh. Got it.” Kim Mi-ju came back to herself and caught up quickly.

Walking, she still could not help glancing back once at Bae Suzy.

And exactly met Bae Suzy, also as if unconsciously, throwing one glance toward Bae Do-yoon’s retreating back.

In Kim Mi-ju’s heart there sounded, instantly, an oh.

Something is going on.

She could not say what exactly; but at least this much she could confirm: what had been talked about in the meeting room just now was definitely not only cold case details.

Minutes later · the elevator

Doors closed; floor numbers descending slow

Where a clerk’s casual tone stops being casual

After the elevator doors opened, the two of them walked in one after the other.

The doors had just closed when Kim Mi-ju could no longer hold it in.

She tilted her head to glance at Bae Do-yoon, her tone trying its best to sound casual:

“Prosecutor Bae — you talked quite a while inside. Did she later remember some new clue?”

Bae Do-yoon watched the floor numbers descending slowly on the display and answered very calmly:

“At first supplementing details, then following up with a few questions. So a bit of time was lost.”

This answer was about the same as not answering.

Kim Mi-ju, not giving up, asked again:

“Was what she supplemented important? If important, I need to mark it separately when I organize it back at the office — otherwise I’m afraid I’ll miss the key points in the afternoon report.”

Only then did Bae Do-yoon turn his head to look at her. The look was light, but plainly had already seen through her intent.

“Important content I will write into the report. No need for you to guess separately.”

Kim Mi-ju, exposed, was not embarrassed; rather, she went with it and smiled:

“I’m not guessing. Isn’t this normal confirmation of work content? Besides — when Ms. Bae came out her expression was quite subtle. I thought she’d heard something unexpected.”

Bae Do-yoon let that line pass; he only counter-asked:

“Your observation is quite detailed.”

“Of course. Professional habit,” Kim Mi-ju said — and then, realizing herself that this sentence sounded a little like imitating him, could not help a light cough. “Mainly, her state just now really didn’t look like someone who’d simply finished case questions and come out. She also looked at you several times. Didn’t you notice?”

“Didn’t specially pay attention,” Bae Do-yoon answered very calmly, as if truly not keeping it in his heart.

Kim Mi-ju very nearly laughed out.

“Prosecutor Bae — that sentence of yours is already very suspicious. A person like you would normally remember even how many times another person’s fingers moved while speaking. Now you’re telling me you didn’t specially pay attention to whether she looked at you. Is that reasonable?”

Bae Do-yoon, hearing this, did not refute; he only replied in a tone neither light nor heavy:

“Have you been too relaxed at work recently, that you have mood to research these things?”

“I research this also for the stability of the work atmosphere,” Kim Mi-ju said, perfectly straight-faced. “If the witness develops extra interest in the prosecutor in charge and I grasp the situation in advance, later arrangements for contact will have more measure. See — how is this not part of work?”

Bae Do-yoon finally looked a little helpless; he glanced at her, low:

“Save that set of talk for the afternoon meeting. See whether the bu-jang-nim will make you responsible for the feelings-analysis report.”

Kim Mi-ju could not hold it and laughed out. Then she climbed the pole at once:

“So something really is a bit off, isn’t it? Otherwise you wouldn’t joke with me like this.”

Bae Do-yoon did not answer; he only took out his phone and glanced at it, as if planning to end the topic with that motion.

But Kim Mi-ju was not so easily given up on.

She leaned a little closer and asked, voice lowered:

“Did she ask you private questions inside?”

This time, Bae Do-yoon’s motion paused half a second.

And it was precisely that half-second that made Kim Mi-ju’s eyes light up.

“I guessed it, right?”

She connected almost at once:

“Ms. Bae’s type of personality really seems possible. On the surface she looks polite, but actually she’s quite bold. Especially toward cold people like you — the colder you are, the more she’ll want to test where the boundary is.”

Bae Do-yoon killed the phone screen, put it back in his pocket, his tone still without ripple:

“She did indeed ask a few questions not directly related to the case. But none affected the questioning, and no need for you to additionally exert imagination.”

Kim Mi-ju, hearing this, only got more into it.

“What do you mean by questions not directly related to the case? This phrasing already has quite a lot of space, okay.”

She stared at Bae Do-yoon’s face, finding it more interesting the more she looked.

“So — did you answer? Or did you directly block the topic back?”

“Some I answered. Some didn’t need answering,” Bae Do-yoon said.

“Doesn’t sound like completely blocked back.” Kim Mi-ju rubbed her chin, in the pose of serious reasoning. “So what on earth did she ask, that you have this reaction now?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, lightly:

“What reaction do I have now?”

“The reaction of surface extremely calm but actually continuously avoiding the key point.”

Kim Mi-ju said it very sincerely:

“Prosecutor Bae, you’re not like this usually. If you really felt it boring, you’d have long since blocked me dead with one sentence ‘none of your business.’ But now you answer every sentence yet circle around every sentence. What does this show? It shows you yourself also know this matter is a bit subtle.”

The elevator, voices lowered between floors

Did she ask you private questions inside? — half a second of stopped motion — I guessed it, right?

She did indeed ask a few questions not directly related to the case. But none affected the questioning, and no need for you to additionally exert imagination.

On the surface polite, but actually quite bold; especially toward cold people — the colder you are, the more she’ll want to test where the boundary is.

Two people, one descending box, and one half-second louder than every sentence spoken in it.

Bae Do-yoon did not speak at once.

Kim Mi-ju had only meant to probe. But watching this instant’s brief silence of his, she suddenly, sharply, realized: the matter might be a little more complex than she had thought.

Because Bae Do-yoon’s present state did not look like the reaction of a man simply asked a few ambiguous questions by Bae Suzy.

It looked more as if those questions had made him think of something else.

Something more important than Bae Suzy herself — and more something he did not want to touch lightly.

She blinked; her tone finally grew a little serious:

“Prosecutor Bae — I’ll just ask casually. Your silence so long inside just now: it wasn’t because of Bae Suzy, but because her question made you think of someone else, right?”

Bae Do-yoon this time lifted his eyes to look at her. The gaze was very light, yet quieter than before.

In Kim Mi-ju’s heart there was a thud; she almost immediately knew she had guessed something.

She did not yet know who that person was. But she could already confirm: the true reason for Bae Do-yoon’s strangeness did not lie on Bae Suzy at all.

The elevator doors opened exactly then.

Bae Do-yoon stepped out first, as if using the moment to cut the topic off, leaving only a very ordinary sentence:

“Back at the office, first send me today’s organized timeline. I want to look through it once before the afternoon meeting.”

Kim Mi-ju followed behind him, mouth answering got it, heart already thoroughly hooked.

All the way back to the car, she still could not hold it in.

After fastening her seatbelt, she turned to look at Bae Do-yoon and asked, half joking, half serious:

“Then I’ll change the way I ask. Is Ms. Bae a bit interested in you? This can’t count as my over-interpretation, right?”

Bae Do-yoon started the car, gaze on the road ahead, answering unhurried:

“She is very smart, and knows how to lead the atmosphere in the direction she wants. If you interpret all this kind of ability as interest, the range is inevitably too large.”

“This talk is wrong,” Kim Mi-ju rebutted at once. “Creating atmosphere is one thing; truly placing attention on a particular person is another. That look she gave you after coming out just now is clearly not one she’d give to everyone.”

Bae Do-yoon let the remark fall.

Kim Mi-ju, observing his profile, suddenly changed direction:

“Then you? What do you think of her?”

Bae Do-yoon, as if long expecting this question, did not pause at all and said directly:

“Her cooperation is high, her expression clear, her emotion stable, and the information she provided today is very valuable. As a contact object, no problem.”

“This isn’t an answer — this is writing a work evaluation,” Kim Mi-ju said, looking at him speechless. “I asked for private thoughts, not a case-closing opinion.”

Bae Do-yoon, hearing this, actually pulled the corner of his lip very lightly, as if finding her sentence a little funny.

“Then that shows I have no private thoughts needing extra supplement.”

Kim Mi-ju leaned back against the seat, stared at the windshield quietly for a few seconds, and suddenly muttered, low:

“Not right.”

“What’s not right?” Bae Do-yoon asked.

“That you have no thoughts about Bae Suzy — I now roughly believe.”

Kim Mi-ju slowly turned her head to look at him.

“But you today indeed have a problem. That problem isn’t the reaction of a man teased a few sentences by a beautiful female artist. It’s more like… she said something that flipped out something already in your heart.”

Bae Do-yoon’s hands on the steering wheel were very steady; his face also showed no change.

But precisely because it was too steady, Kim Mi-ju was only more certain her intuition was not wrong.

She did not press further; she only let her tone soften a little:

“If you don’t want to say, of course I won’t chase and ask. But Prosecutor Bae — this is the first time I’ve seen you like this. Not panic, not disorder. Just clearly already noticing something, and yet not planning to admit it.”

Sunlight outside the window passed over the car body section by section. The red light ahead lit; the car slowed to a stop.

Bae Do-yoon watched the dense traffic ahead, his expression without ripple. And in his heart, that sentence Bae Suzy had asked him in the meeting room surfaced once more.

That sentence of Bae Suzy’s in the meeting room — the shape of the girl he liked, asked in one light question — surfaced once more, uninvited.

And in that instant, the person he had thought of was Irene.

Those images, once flipped out, were like things that could no longer be stuffed back into place.

At the red light · one question, resurfaced

The cleared meeting room; the half-melted iced americano; the question asked like a joke and not wanting only a joke’s answer.

And the person who surfaced before any safe answer could be assembled: Irene — suitcase, street corner, uncorrected accent, stairwell, late-night call, hot drink.

Images that, once flipped out, refuse to be stuffed back into place; a drawer, long locked, loosened by one light question from a woman in a cap.

The light turns green. The car moves. The images ride along.

Kim Mi-ju waited a while; seeing him consistently not speak, she sensibly did not press further.

She was not a person who did not know measure. Gossip was gossip; but truly touching a place another did not want touched, she would not go on endlessly.

When the light turned green and the car moved forward again, after a good while, Kim Mi-ju — as if finding a step down for this conversation — said, deliberately light:

“Forget it, I won’t ask. Anyway a person like you, if you really want to think something through, won’t tell others in advance. When someday you’re willing to say it yourself, I’ll hear it then — not too late.”

Bae Do-yoon this time answered, lightly:

“For you to have this kind of self-awareness is quite rare.”

“See — now even your sarcasm isn’t as sharp as usual,” Kim Mi-ju sighed, her tone summarizing with exaggeration. “Done. Looks like this time it’s really not light.”

Bae Do-yoon did not deal with her again.

Near afternoon · the Prosecutors’ Office

Lobby busy as ever: badge reader, stairs, nodded greetings

One man returning to his familiar work state, followed by one clerk reading his back

When they returned to the Prosecutors’ Office, it was already near afternoon.

The lobby was as busy as ever.

Bae Do-yoon entered, badged in, went upstairs, nodded to passing colleagues — the whole person returning to his familiar work state. Calm, restrained, extremely efficient, as if nothing had happened.

The restored work state · assembled again by three in the afternoon

Collar shirt collar neat, exactly as it went into the meeting room; no witness’s question left any crease in it

Hands materials sorted and carried; badge presented without looking; pen already uncapped by the desk

Manner nods to passing colleagues, level and brief; efficiency at full restoration

The tell none visible. Which is itself the tell, per the clerk walking two steps behind

Assembly identical to every ordinary workday. The ordinary workday, per the same clerk, is precisely what today was not.

But Kim Mi-ju, following behind and watching his back, felt more and more clearly: Bae Do-yoon was not without emotion — he was only too good at pressing emotion down.

Before, she had always felt that a person like Bae Do-yoon could probably file even private feelings the way one organizes case files: which page is what, which slot it sits in, when it may be taken out — all perfectly clear.

But now she suddenly suspected: perhaps it was not that he organized too well, but that some things had been put away too long — so long that he himself was too lazy to confirm what was inside.

Until today, when Bae Suzy lightly asked that one sentence, and that one locked drawer lightly loosened.

Back in the office, Bae Do-yoon put the materials on the desk, opened the computer, and went directly into work state.

Kim Mi-ju stood at the doorway, looked at him for two seconds, and still could not hold back a smile.

“Prosecutor Bae — I guarantee this is my last question today,” she said.

Bae Do-yoon raised his head to meet her; his gaze already carried a little of the meaning you had better keep your word.

Kim Mi-ju, strongly holding in her laugh, deliberately imitated that earlier very serious tone:

“I just want to confirm: if I meet Ms. Bae again in the future, should I default that she’ll pay special attention to you a bit? That way I can also do psychological preparation in advance.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her expressionless for a few seconds, and finally threw her only one sentence:

“If you don’t go organize the record again, I’ll default your workload today is insufficient.”

“Good — I’ll go now.” Kim Mi-ju raised her hands in surrender and turned to go; at the doorway she turned back and added one more line: “But seriously, Prosecutor Bae. You today are different from usual.”

Bae Do-yoon did not take up this line; his gaze had already fallen back to the computer screen.

Kim Mi-ju, seeing he would not speak, did not linger; she closed the door lightly for him.

The office quieted.

The computer screen lit; the document blank; the cursor blinking at the very top.

Bae Do-yoon sat there, hand on the keyboard, but did not immediately begin typing.

After a moment he lifted a hand, pressed his brow, and lowered his head, closing his eyes.

The answer, in truth, was already very clear.

He simply had not yet grown used to admitting it.

The last-question guarantee · spoken at a doorway, tested at a doorway

I. The guarantor’s questioning for the day ends at the moment of guarantee (I guarantee this is my last question today).

II. Confirmations of future default settings (should I default that she’ll pay special attention to you?) are reclassified as work confirmations and fall outside the guarantee.

III. Doorway addenda (you today are different from usual) are statements, not questions, and therefore lawful.

IV. Breach penalty: the guarantor’s workload for the day deemed insufficient; surrender by raised hands accepted in mitigation.

Witnessed by one computer screen, one blank document, and one cursor blinking at the very top.

Lifespan of the guarantee: from doorway to doorway. Renewal prospects, per the guarantor’s own sigh: poor. Per the prosecutor’s silence: unaddressed.

On clerks who remember details and drawers that loosen

A clerk who studied psychology once reads a good mood from two seconds of empty staring; the same clerk now reads a whole concealed weather system off half a second of stopped motion and a pair of hands too steady on a wheel. She is wrong about the target and right about the event, which is the classic condition of excellent intelligence work: the witness in the cap is not the cause, only the key. And the man she interrogates in an elevator answers every sentence while circling every sentence, because the true answer is sitting in a drawer he filed years ago and never labeled, and a woman he met only a few times asked one light question and the lock gave. He will write the timeline before the afternoon meeting. He will not write the rest. The cursor blinks at the top of a blank document, and the answer — already very clear, merely unadmitted — waits for him at the keyboard with the patience of everything he has ever put away too long.
