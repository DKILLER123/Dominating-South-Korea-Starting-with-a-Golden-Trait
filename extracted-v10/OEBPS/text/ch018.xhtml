Chapter Eighteen

Learning Self-Defense

The same evening · the meal, continued

Jeon Bo-ram’s living room · around the low table

Where a man asks nothing he should not, and is noticed for it

Through the meal, Jeon Bo-ram spoke now and then of the day’s small matters, and Park Ji-yeon filled in a few sentences beside her.

She was livelier than Jeon Bo-ram, and talked more.

Bae Do-yoon mostly listened, answering occasionally.

He did not ask about their group on his own, and did not press into entertainment-world topics.

That, to Park Ji-yeon, was somewhat unexpected.

Most people, meeting entertainers, cannot control their curiosity.

They ask about schedules, about relationships between members, about private lives, about income — about every kind of thing one should not ask.

Bae Do-yoon did not.

He looked as if he had truly come only to eat one ordinary dinner — and that attitude, rather, put people at ease.

Halfway through eating, Park Ji-yeon suddenly asked: “Bae Do-yoon-ssi — when you learned we were T-ara members, didn’t you find it strange?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Why should I find it strange?”

Park Ji-yeon said: “Because an ordinary person, suddenly eating with entertainers, should react at least a little, shouldn’t they.”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it, and asked in return: “What reaction would you like from me?”

…

Park Ji-yeon was stopped by the question.

Jeon Bo-ram looked at her too, and could not help laughing out loud.

Park Ji-yeon thought it over and said: “A bit surprised, for instance. Or asking a few questions?”

Bae Do-yoon said: “I was, in fact, somewhat surprised.”

Park Ji-yeon blinked.

“It doesn’t show at all.”

“Then my expression management is decent, I suppose.”

Jeon Bo-ram gave a light laugh.

Park Ji-yeon laughed too.

“Bae Do-yoon-ssi is interesting.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “You entertainers should need expression management more.”

Park Ji-yeon nodded.

“Yes. Especially in front of lenses.”

Having said it, her tone suddenly went a degree lighter.

“Sometimes, even when you aren’t happy, you have to smile.”

Jeon Bo-ram’s hand, holding her chopsticks, stopped very slightly.

Bae Do-yoon looked at the two of them once.

He did not press into it; he said only, evenly: “That must be very hard.”

Park Ji-yeon went still for a moment; Jeon Bo-ram also lifted her head to look at him.

Bae Do-yoon’s tone was very natural.

“When ordinary people are unhappy, they can stay in, stay silent. Entertainers don’t seem to have that choice.”

The smile on Park Ji-yeon’s face thinned a little.

Then she nodded, lightly.

“Mm. Sometimes it is like that.”

Jeon Bo-ram lowered her head and picked up a piece of fried chicken, saying nothing.

Park Ji-yeon looked at Bae Do-yoon, and the curiosity in her heart went deeper.

He had said nothing particularly stirring; he had only, very plainly, acknowledged their hardship.

And precisely because it was plain, it came out true.

After a while Park Ji-yeon’s smile returned.

“But fried chicken can make a person happy.”

Jeon Bo-ram nodded at once.

“Right.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “That conclusion is very persuasive.”

Park Ji-yeon, crinkling her eyes, placed a piece of fried chicken onto Jeon Bo-ram’s plate.

“Unnie, eat more.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked at her.

“Are you trying to make me swell up by tomorrow?”

Park Ji-yeon, perfectly straight-faced: “No. I am sharing happiness.”

Bae Do-yoon lowered his head and laughed a little.

Jeon Bo-ram looked at Park Ji-yeon, helpless.

“You and Bae Do-yoon-ssi learned that very quickly just now.”

Park Ji-yeon turned to Bae Do-yoon at once.

“Bae Do-yoon-ssi — we are on the same front now.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “I don’t recall agreeing.”

“You laughed just now. That is tacit consent.”

“So those are the rules?”

“They are.”

Jeon Bo-ram watched the two of them trading lines, and suddenly regretted a little having brought Park Ji-yeon up.

She felt Ji-yeon’s interest in Bae Do-yoon growing plainer and plainer.

Though that interest was more curiosity than anything else.

After eating, the three of them cleared the low table together.

Bae Do-yoon stacked the empty boxes on his own initiative.

Jeon Bo-ram said quickly: “No, no — you’re the guest.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “I ate no small amount either.”

Park Ji-yeon said, laughing beside them: “Bae Do-yoon-ssi is very conscientious.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Is that a compliment?”

“Of course.”

“Then thank you.”

Park Ji-yeon laughed until her eyes bent.

When the clearing was done, Bae Do-yoon glanced at the time.

“I should head back.”

Jeon Bo-ram blinked.

“This soon?”

Having said it, as if feeling the sentence had come out too naturally, she hurriedly added: “I mean — it really is late.”

Park Ji-yeon turned to look at Jeon Bo-ram at once, her gaze carrying a somewhat knowing smile.

Jeon Bo-ram glared at her lightly.

Park Ji-yeon pretended not to see.

Bae Do-yoon stood up.

“Thank you both for dinner tonight.”

Jeon Bo-ram stood too.

“We’re the ones thanking you for helping with the chicken.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled.

“Next time, don’t buy so much.”

Jeon Bo-ram said, in a small voice: “That would depend on whether we’re hungry.”

Park Ji-yeon added at once: “Unnie buys too much every time she’s hungry.”

“Ji-yeon!”

Park Ji-yeon raised her hand, laughing.

“I’m shutting up.”

Bae Do-yoon went to the door to change his shoes.

Park Ji-yeon followed over and said, smiling: “Bae Do-yoon-ssi — I’m glad I met you today.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded politely.

“Likewise.”

Park Ji-yeon looked at him, and suddenly said: “But I still think you don’t look like an ordinary civil servant.”

Bae Do-yoon’s motion checked faintly; then he lifted his eyes to her.

Park Ji-yeon said, crinkling her eyes: “But I won’t press.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Thank you for understanding.”

“So — if there’s a chance later, let’s eat together again.”

Park Ji-yeon’s tone was light.

“I’d rather like to know what kind of person Bae Do-yoon-ssi really is.”

Hearing that sentence, Jeon Bo-ram turned to look at her at once.

Park Ji-yeon smiled in perfect innocence.

“I’m only curious.”

Bae Do-yoon gave a low laugh.

“If there’s a chance.”

Jeon Bo-ram stood by the door, looking at him.

“Bae Do-yoon-ssi — rest early.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“You two as well.”

He opened the door, turned, and left; the door closed softly.

Behind the closed door · two voices and one cushion

Unnie. Is he really just a neighbor? Of course he is just a neighbor. Ohhh— Don’t use that tone. But unnie smiled very happily when you saw him just now. When you meet someone you know, of course you greet them. Just someone you know? Mm.

But Bae Do-yoon-ssi also looks very good to unnie. …Does he? He does. He was plainly hungry, yet first considered whether it would affect you; at the door he waited for us to enter; before sitting he waited for the host; at the table he asked nothing he shouldn’t about us.

And when he looks at unnie, his eyes are very gentle. …You observe far too closely. Because I’m curious. Are you this curious about everyone? No.

Bae Do-yoon-ssi is a little different. Different how? I can’t say. Just — he isn’t like an ordinary person. Because he’s handsome? That is one of the reasons. Ya, Ji-yeon.

Park Ji-yeon laughed a little, then her tone grew a degree more serious.

“No, that isn’t it. Mainly — he is too steady.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked at her.

Park Ji-yeon went on: “Ordinary people, learning we are entertainers — even if they don’t act exaggerated, they go a little unnatural. He doesn’t. It’s as if, from the very start, he has been looking at us as ordinary people.”

Jeon Bo-ram grew quiet.

Park Ji-yeon said, softly: “That feeling is actually quite rare.”

Jeon Bo-ram lowered her head, her fingers lightly pinching the edge of the cushion.

After a while she said, in a small voice: “Mm.”

She felt the same.

From the very first time she met Bae Do-yoon, he had seemed not to look at her with those complicated gazes.

Precisely because of that, she had relaxed without ever noticing it herself.

Park Ji-yeon watched Jeon Bo-ram’s expression, and suddenly smiled.

“Unnie.”

“Mm?”

“Do you trust him a great deal?”

Jeon Bo-ram was silent for a moment; this time she did not deny it at once.

After a while she said, quietly: “He helped me.”

Park Ji-yeon looked at her.

Jeon Bo-ram lifted her head.

“And — he doesn’t look like someone who would hurt people.”

Park Ji-yeon teased no further; she only nodded.

“Then get to know him slowly.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked at her.

Park Ji-yeon smiled.

“I’d rather like to get to know him slowly too.”

Jeon Bo-ram was on guard at once.

“Get to know what, exactly?”

Park Ji-yeon hugged the cushion and laughed.

“Unnie’s neighbor, of course.”

“Ji-yeon.”

“Just kidding.”

Park Ji-yeon laughed, very happily.

But after laughing, she glanced once more toward the door.

The days after · then a day off, seven in the morning

A street that is not busy · a Chinese restaurant below, red lanterns at its door

A case set aside for now, and an address confirmed on a phone map over breakfast

Over the next several days, Bae Do-yoon kept pushing Bae Suzy’s case forward.

Only, progress did not go smoothly.

So he had no choice but to set the case aside for the time being.

On the day of his rest, Bae Do-yoon did not sleep until noon at home.

At seven in the morning he was already awake.

Washed, dressed, ate breakfast.

Then he sat at the dining table, opened the map on his phone, and confirmed the address once more.

Half an hour later, Bae Do-yoon went out.

The destination was not the Prosecutors’ Office, not the court, and certainly not JYP. It was a martial arts school.

The school stood on the second floor of a street that could not be called busy. Below it was a Chinese restaurant, red lanterns hung at the door, and a sticker on the glass read Authentic Handmade Dumplings.

Bae Do-yoon looked up once.

On the second-floor signboard, several very eye-catching Hanja characters stood written: Inmudang Chinese Martial Arts Hall.

Beneath them ran one more line, in Hangul: wing chun, qinna joint-locks, sanshou, self-defense, children’s martial arts, adult practical combat class.

Bae Do-yoon stood below and looked for a few seconds.

The reason he had found this place was that he had searched online for a long time last night.

Korea held no shortage of taekwondo halls, hapkido halls, boxing gyms, mixed-martial-arts gyms.

But in the end Bae Do-yoon had chosen this Chinese martial arts hall.

The reason was simple. The online listing said:

Inmudang Chinese Martial Arts Hall · listing page

searched late last night · read twice · believed approximately half

Curriculum: wing chun · qinna joint-locks · sanshou · practical self-defense · children’s martial arts · adult combat class.

Our master studied under a lineage disciple of a how-many-th generation inheritor of the wing chun fist, and concurrently trains in baji quan, qinna techniques, and practical self-defense skills.

A sentence that sounds enormously pedigreed — and, on inspection, contains not one precisely verifiable fact. The how-many-th generation remains, to this day, a variable.

When Bae Do-yoon had first read the words a how-many-th generation inheritor’s lineage disciple, he had been silent for a long time.

How-many-th generation. Lineage disciple?

The sentence sounded as if it carried great provenance.

Think carefully, though, and it seemed to contain nothing at all that could be verified precisely.

The comment section, however, was not bad.

What the comment section said

floor 1

The master doesn’t look tall, but he can push a grown man away with one hand.

floor 2

This place isn’t flower-stance showmanship. The self-defense class is very practical.

floor 3

When the master pinned me to the mat, I understood for the first time what traditional martial arts means.

Floor 3 was the one Bae Do-yoon remembered most clearly. So he came.

As for why he had suddenly wanted to learn self-defense? The reason was not complicated.

Because of Lee Boo-jin.

More precisely: because he had remembered the day Lee Boo-jin first came to find him.

The Lee Boo-jin of now appeared before him elegant and composed.

But Bae Do-yoon had always remembered that their first meeting was not like that.

The school gate, years ago · a bear in a black suit

That year he was still very small. Not long after leaving the school gate, several men in black blocked his way: black suits, earpieces, expressions cold. One of them was especially exaggerated — nearly two meters tall, shoulders wide as door panels, standing in front of a child and blocking out nearly all of the daylight.

The young Bae Do-yoon looked up at him, and his first reaction was not fear. It was puzzlement. Does this country really allow minors to encounter bears on the road? The next second, that bear lifted him. One hand. Grabbed by the back of the collar, the way one lifts a bag of snacks bought at a convenience store. Feet off the ground; the uniform collar pressing his neck; his whole body swinging once in midair while the man looked down at him with cold eyes.

Law, reason, dignity — before a man nearly two meters tall and built like a bear, all of them seemed very far away. Especially when your feet have left the ground.

That day left no small shadow in him. So that now, years later, a prosecutor able to face many people with law and procedure, he still keeps one plain and unshakable idea in the deepest drawer.

Does this country really allow minors to encounter bears on the road?

If next time someone tries to lift him like a chicken —

at the very least, he must have the ability to make that person pay a little price.

Bae Do-yoon drew his thoughts back and went up to the second floor.

Pushing open the school’s glass door, a neat shout came from inside.

“Ha!”

The floor was laid with thick mats.

On the walls hung a wooden dummy, a weapon rack, and several pieces of calligraphy.

Two scrolls on the training-hall wall

Cultivate the self through martial arts.

First be a person; then train the fist.

Bae Do-yoon looked once, and felt this place was at least more reliable than its online listing appeared.

A young girl sat at the front desk; seeing him enter, she stood up at once.

“Hello — are you here to ask about classes?”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“I’d like to enroll in the adult self-defense class.”

The girl brought out a registration form.

“Have you trained in martial arts, boxing, taekwondo, or any other combat discipline before?”

Bae Do-yoon said: “No systematic training.”

The girl nodded.

“Then is your aim fitness, or practical self-defense?”

Bae Do-yoon did not hesitate.

“Practical self-defense.”

The girl looked up at him once.

Perhaps because Bae Do-yoon looked too much like a person who would not be bullied.

She confirmed again.

“You are certain it is the self-defense class? Our self-defense class is harder than the ordinary fitness courses.”

Bae Do-yoon said, evenly: “Certain.”

The girl asked: “May I ask the reason? Work requirements, for instance, or personal interest?”

Bae Do-yoon was silent for a moment.

In his head rose again the man in black, nearly two meters tall. And himself, feet off the ground, held in midair.

After a while he said: “Childhood shadow.”

Front-desk girl: …

Her hand, holding the pen, stopped for a moment.

Plainly, this answer was not among the enrollment reasons she commonly met.

After a while she gave an embarrassed, polite smile.

“I see.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“Mm.”

The registration form, as finally filled

Prior training: none systematic. Aim: practical self-defense. Reason, as written by the girl’s considerate pen: personal self-defense need. What her pen hovered over, and kindly did not write: childhood shadow.

When the paperwork was done, the girl led Bae Do-yoon into the training area.

“The master is teaching a class now. You may observe first.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

At the center of the training area, a middle-aged man of no great height was guiding students.

He looked a little over forty, in a black training uniform, hair cut very short, shoulders and spine straight, gaze steady.

By build alone, he was not as exaggerated as Bae Do-yoon had imagined.

He could even be called very ordinary.

The Master of Inmudang

Head instructor, Inmudang Chinese Martial Arts Hall; second floor, above the dumpling restaurant

Lineage: a how-many-th generation inheritor’s lineage disciple, per the listing; the comment section prefers “the man who pinned me to the mat”

Met mid-class, mid-throw, on a day off at half past eight in the morning

Short-haired, straight-backed, unimpressive in silhouette and unarguable in result. Speaks Korean with a little Chinese accent in it, and teaches force the way accountants teach ledgers: by showing where it leaks.

The master

But when a male student over a meter eighty rushed in at him, the middle-aged man only turned his body, raised a hand, closed in, and issued force.

The movement was very short, with almost no large opening or sweeping arc.

Yet that student looked as if his center of gravity had been drawn out from under him in an instant; he staggered back several steps and ended sitting on the mat.

A few students beside them gave low exclamations.

The middle-aged man showed no pride; he said only, lightly:

First lesson, overheard before enrollment

Side-step · raise hand · close distance · issue force — four beats, none of them large

The opponent’s center left before his feet did; the fall was arithmetic, not violence

Force is not used like this. The more you want to use brute force, the easier it is for the other side to borrow yours.

Said in Korean, with a little Chinese accent in it. No flying over eaves, no striking across the air, no palm that breaks bricks — and precisely therefore, Bae Do-yoon believed all of it.

Bae Do-yoon stood beside them watching, his gaze moving faintly.

This seemed different from the martial arts he had imagined.

No leaping roofs and running along walls; no striking a man from across the air; no performance of smashing bricks with one palm.

But that one exchange just now had been practical.

Simple. Direct. And effective.

The middle-aged man seemed to notice his gaze, and turned to look over.

The front-desk girl said quickly: “Master — this is Bae Do-yoon-ssi, here to ask about the adult self-defense class.”

On childhood shadows and reasonable force

Every man carries one measurement he never chose: the day he learned exactly how much of the world can pick him up. For most people that measurement rusts quietly. For him it stayed bright in a drawer, next to the law books, waiting for a gym above a dumpling shop and a master who teaches that force is a ledger — spend it wildly and the other side reads your balance better than you do.
