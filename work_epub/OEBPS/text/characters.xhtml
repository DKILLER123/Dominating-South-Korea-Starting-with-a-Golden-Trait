In this story

His first life ended with a fishing line under a high-voltage cable, which is a joke the serial makes once and then never again. He woke up in a parallel Korea with the same face and one plan: finish high school, walk into SM Entertainment, debut, and quietly date a woman other men only saw through a screen.

Somebody removed that option before he reached the building, offered two others — a law degree and a prosecutor’s seat, or the Han River — and then paid for the first one for years. Chapter 1 finds him on his second day in office, with a woman asleep in his bed and the phrase gift doing a great deal of work.

He is not noble and does not pretend to be. He keeps a black notebook, writes his own situation down in seven numbered lines when he cannot afford to feel it, and has decided that being a useful blade is a route rather than a fate. What he has said out loud, twice, is that he wanted a girlfriend; what he has not said to anybody is what he intends to become.

Born, age, height and office number are deliberately absent from this card: this book fixes each of them in the chapter that first says them out loud, and no card is allowed to invent ahead of that.

In this story

Chapter 4 is hers. She slams a table in a tea lounge, is told in a mild voice that the meeting itself is the evidence she has no options left, and is walked through her own file by a stranger: the overseas channels, the unfinished dispute, the endorsement in final review, the quiet downgrade of her sister.

She is not a victim in the flat sense the word usually does in these books. She is also not willing. The serial keeps both facts in place at once and refuses to let either one become an alibi.

Two years before the hotel there was an offline variety event, a draw for one photograph with a member of the audience, and a young man who looked at five women on a stage and chose the one who did not seem to belong to the noise. She does not remember him. He remembers it well enough to keep the print pinned by his desk.

What she wants now, in her own words, is a single fact: who the man was.

She recruits the way her family negotiates: by making refusal expensive, and then calling the result a partnership. In Chapter 3 she explains the choice of a prosecutor out loud — the Korean prosecution is itself a blade, and what she wants is an edge of her own hidden inside it, filed where nobody goes looking for private ownership.

The serial’s version of her is a character in a power fantasy. The biography of the real person of that name is not this book’s material to correct, to flatter, or to borrow.

She is used twice and appears once. Used first as a lever — the way a career is thinned out is described in Chapter 4 in a voice that never has to threaten anybody. Used again as a threat, politely: her sister’s trade could be offered to her instead, and she might take it first.

She appears in Chapter 5 on a sofa with a cushion in her arms, asking where her sister has been and why she could not be reached, and receiving an answer that is not an answer. She is considerate enough not to press, and hurt enough that this costs her something; both facts are in the same sentence of the raw.

She is the machine’s polite end. Nothing she does in either chapter is illegal, deniable, or even rude — which is exactly why both scenes end the way they do. The serial has not named her yet, and this edition does not name her either; a servant of power who is given a name too early tends to be given a conscience with it.

In this story

The golden entry is the reason he was picked: it makes commanding older women notice a man and consider him worth cultivating, and its note says plainly that being favored this way can cost him his own plan. Its weight, he decided long ago, is not on the word rich. It is on the note.

The purple ones behave like a diagnosis. Judicial Monster makes him impossible to fool and worse at feeling; Idol Killer tells him what an idol’s composure is hiding, and warns that the industry’s behind-the-scenes trades will find him first. Both of them are useful in exactly the way a wound is useful.

She enters in Chapter 9 as a cap pressed low in a supermarket line, and spends the whole walk home convinced the tall man behind her is a stalking pervert — which, from her side of the pavement, the evidence supported. The misunderstanding ends with a dropped key, a surrendered name, and him mangling that name into a former president’s.

Chapter 10 gives her the dark bedroom and the unreachable bulb, and him the chair. What she offers at her door afterwards — a cooked meal, a knock, anything, please let me help with something — is the chapter’s title and its only verdict on her: a person the internet convicted without a trial, offering help with no trial at all.

He has not appeared in person. His ringtone ruins a younger sister’s mood in Chapter 6; his voice arrives in Chapter 7, hurried and aggrieved at a declined call, then excited at channels reopening, then cooled at the word books. Jessica asks for every channel outlay, investment line, inventory cost and external contract by noon, and offers a lawyer as the alternative courier.

Krystal’s audit of him is delivered from a bed with a hugged pillow: the way he looks at her sister is not the way a man looks at a girlfriend, it is the way a man looks at company assets. Jessica does not argue. Not arguing is the loudest sentence she speaks all chapter.

Real people appear here as dramatis personae of a fictional serial; that is a storytelling choice, not a claim about anyone’s life, conduct or opinions. Portraits are press photography for real figures and commissioned illustration for invented ones, and each is labeled on the card that carries it. Scene plates set from a card portrait carry the same labeling on their caption line, and never invent a likeness a card does not already hold. New cards are added the cycle after a figure first appears, and bios grow by append, never by rewriting what a previous chapter settled.
