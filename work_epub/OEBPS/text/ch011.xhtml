Chapter Eleven

Moving Here Isn’t as Bad as I Imagined

The same night · a little past nine

Her bedroom · first dark, then warm yellow

One bulb, two bowls of rice, and a debt settled in crab

Bae Do-yoon handed her the dead bulb.

Jeon Bo-ram reached for it, and her fingers brushed the back of his hand by accident.

Her motion stopped for a beat; then she took the bulb properly, quickly, as if nothing at all had happened.

Bae Do-yoon, for his part, reacted to nothing.

He fitted the new bulb, screwed it tight, and checked the socket once more.

“Go turn the switch on and try.”

Jeon Bo-ram hurried to the doorway and pressed the switch.

Click.

The bedroom lit up again.

Warm yellow light spilled across the room all at once: the bed, the wardrobe, the suitcase still not fully unpacked, every one of them brought plainly out of the dark.

Jeon Bo-ram stood at the doorway looking at her lit bedroom, the happiness on her face very nearly impossible to hide.

“It lit up!”

The tone was of a person who has just solved an enormously important problem.

Bae Do-yoon stepped down off the chair and set it back in its place.

“It was only a bulb.”

Jeon Bo-ram said it seriously: “To me it’s important.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her once.

She lowered her head a little, embarrassed, and her voice went smaller. “If it’s too dark at night, I really can’t sleep.”

She did not explain the reason.

Bae Do-yoon did not ask it. He only said: “From now on, call building management for this kind of thing in the daytime. If you absolutely must change it at night, cut the power first.”

Jeon Bo-ram nodded. “Understood.”

Bae Do-yoon put his phone back in his pocket.

“Anything else you need help with? If not, I’ll head back.”

“Nothing, nothing!” She waved both hands fast.

But having said it, she seemed to remember something, and looked at him with a little hesitation.

“Um…”

Bae Do-yoon stopped mid-step. “Something else?”

Jeon Bo-ram pressed her lips together, lightly. “Actually… I have some late-night food here.”

His brow lifted a fraction. “Late-night food?”

She nodded, and then, as if afraid he would misunderstand, hurried to add: “It isn’t specially prepared. A friend sent it over before, and I can’t finish it alone.”

“What kind of late-night food?”

Jeon Bo-ram said it small: “Marinated crab.”

The living room went quiet for one full second.

The motion of Bae Do-yoon preparing to leave stopped, entirely naturally, where it was.

He looked at Jeon Bo-ram. “Do you have soy-sauce crab?”

She nodded. “Mm. And a little spicy-sauce crab too.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent for two seconds. Then he said: “Excuse me, then. I won’t be polite.”

Jeon Bo-ram: …

She looked at him — a man who had changed his entire decision in an instant and still kept a perfectly straight face — and could not hold the laugh in.

Bae Do-yoon explained, without a change of expression: “I’m not greedy.”

Jeon Bo-ram asked, holding her laugh: “Then what is it?”

Bae Do-yoon said it seriously: “Mainly, I don’t like wasting food.”

The corners of her mouth curved up. “Then please sit, Mr. Bae Who Doesn’t Waste Food.”

In their earlier talk she had learned his name; it came out of her now with the ease of a neighbor.

The late-night table · set out because a friend sent too much

Two bowls of rice, one pair of chopsticks each, and no ceremony whatsoever

From the preservation box, first

Soy-sauce marinated crab — ganjang gejang

The lid opens and its particular sweet-savory smell crosses the whole room at once; the crabs sit deep in their dark sauce, glossy.

From the preservation box, second

Spicy-sauce marinated crab

A little of it, red and fierce, for whoever wants the other kind of trouble.

House rule of this table: it goes with rice, and nobody complains about the salt.

Bae Do-yoon sat at the dining table.

Jeon Bo-ram went into the kitchen, brought out the preservation box, and served two bowls of rice. The moment the box opened, that sweet freshness of soy-sauce crab spread through the room in one wave.

Bae Do-yoon had meant to taste a polite amount.

Then he saw the crabs sitting in their sauce, and understood that his agreement just now had been too reserved by half.

Jeon Bo-ram handed him the chopsticks. “Can you eat raw-marinated?”

“I can.”

“Won’t it be too salty?”

“With rice, it’s fine.”

“Then eat this one.” She lifted a piece of crab body onto the small plate in front of him. “This piece has the flavor in it best.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her once. “Thank you.”

Jeon Bo-ram blinked. “I’m the one who should be thanking you. You’re the one who helped me enormously.”

The atmosphere between them had gone much lighter than before.

The two of them sat across a small dining table. The light was warm. Outside the window, Seoul’s night neon.

The scene was, honestly, a little strange: a female idol who had just moved in, and a man from next door she had barely met, sitting together past nine at night eating marinated crab because of a broken light bulb. If outsiders knew, a great many absurd guesses would grow out of it.

But at this moment neither of them mentioned any of that.

Jeon Bo-ram held her rice bowl and ate in small bites.

Bae Do-yoon ate more seriously than she had expected. He was not the type to perform deliciousness; but every bite went in steady, and his chopsticks barely stopped.

Jeon Bo-ram watched him a while and could not help asking: “Is it good?”

Bae Do-yoon looked up.

“Mm.”

“Really?”

“Really.”

“Good.” She looked relieved. “I was afraid it wouldn’t suit you.”

Bae Do-yoon said: “You underestimate marinated crab.”

Jeon Bo-ram laughed; after a while she asked again:

“Mr. Bae, did you just move here?”

“Mm.”

“A work transfer?”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. “Something like that.”

Jeon Bo-ram nodded. “Then what kind of work do you do?”

The motion of his chopsticks paused, imperceptibly.

He raised his eyes to her.

Jeon Bo-ram seemed only to be asking in passing: her eyes held plain curiosity, nothing deeper meant by it.

Bae Do-yoon set the crab shell on the plate and said, in a natural tone:

“Public service, I suppose.”

“A civil servant?”

“Something like that.”

“Wow.” Jeon Bo-ram was genuinely surprised. “You don’t look like one.”

Bae Do-yoon asked: “I don’t look like a civil servant?”

Jeon Bo-ram looked him over seriously.

Clean short hair; a straight, upright build; sitting there in homewear he still carried some inexplicable pressure about him.

She shook her head. “Not really.”

“Like what, then?”

Jeon Bo-ram thought about it. “Like… an entertainer?”

Bae Do-yoon was silent for a moment.

“Do you entertainers look at everyone and see an entertainer?”

Jeon Bo-ram waved both hands fast. “No, no — that isn’t what I mean.”

She explained, a little flustered:

“It’s just that you’re very good-looking, and your air isn’t like an ordinary office worker’s.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

Only after the sentence was out did Jeon Bo-ram realize what she had said; her ears went red by slow degrees. She ducked her head and shoveled in a mouthful of rice, pretending nothing at all had happened.

Bae Do-yoon did not tease her further.

He only said, lightly: “Civil service has its good-looking ones too. Though I am on the outstanding side.”

Jeon Bo-ram very nearly choked on her rice at this deadpan self-praise.

“Mr. Bae, you are not very humble.”

“Humility doesn’t change the facts.”

This time Jeon Bo-ram truly laughed out loud.

What she had stopped doing, and what he never did

After the laugh she was a little surprised at herself: it had been a long while since she was this relaxed in front of a stranger. Or rather — since that incident, she kept her distance from strangers by instinct. From men especially. From men who lived nearby and might know who she was, most of all.

But Bae Do-yoon was strange. He did not press about her group. He did not ask the questions that embarrass her. He showed no fan’s excitement. He seemed to know who she was, and simply did not treat it as a special thing.

That sense of measure, of all things, was what made her comfortable.

Jeon Bo-ram stole a look at him.

Bae Do-yoon had his head down, peeling crab, his expression focused as if handling serious work.

Is this person really only an ordinary civil servant?

Bae Do-yoon caught her line of sight and looked up. “What is it?”

Jeon Bo-ram shook her head at once.

“Nothing.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her once, and did not expose her.

The two went on with the late meal. Along the way Jeon Bo-ram asked very ordinary things: where he had moved from, what hours he kept, whether he often worked overtime.

Bae Do-yoon’s answers were all appropriately vague.

“It depends.”

“Not fixed.”

“If the seniors are in a good mood, maybe a little earlier.”

Hearing this, Jeon Bo-ram wore the face of deep fellow-feeling.

“The senior-junior culture is really tiring, isn’t it?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her. “You’re tired too?”

Jeon Bo-ram froze. She had only been answering in passing; she had not expected him to ask it back.

She looked down at the rice in her bowl, and after a few seconds said, softly:

“Entertainers are about the same.” Then she smiled. “But I’m used to it.”

Bae Do-yoon did not say you’ve worked hard. That comfort was too cheap; it would have been a brush-off.

He only carried the topic aside. “So your fear of the dark at night — is it work stress?”

Jeon Bo-ram’s fingers stilled, very slightly.

She did not answer at once.

After a while she said, in a very light voice: “Not entirely.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “Then I won’t ask.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked up at him, surprise in her eyes.

Most people, hearing an answer like that, would press: Why? What happened? Is it because of that incident?

Bae Do-yoon did not. He seemed genuinely only to have confirmed a boundary, and then stopped exactly there.

Jeon Bo-ram felt the relief come up in her all at once.

She said, small: “Thank you.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted a piece of crab meat and asked, smiling: “Thank you for the bulb, or thank you for not asking?”

Jeon Bo-ram stilled, then laughed. “Both.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“Then I’ve earned quite a lot tonight.”

“Is the marinated crab enough?”

“Enough.” He paused, and added: “If there is more next time, you may call me again.”

Jeon Bo-ram looked at his utterly straight face and could not help asking:

“Call you when the light breaks, or call you when there’s marinated crab?”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. “Best if both.”

Jeon Bo-ram laughed again.

This evening had begun with nothing but one broken bulb. By the time Bae Do-yoon left, it was nearly half past ten.

Jeon Bo-ram saw him to the door and stood at the entry edge.

“Thank you really for today.”

“You’ve thanked me many times already.”

“Then… good night.”

Bae Do-yoon changed his shoes and looked back at her.

Warm yellow light fell from behind her; in the pale blue loungewear she looked much softer than anything the stage had ever put out.

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “Good night.”

He walked back next door. Between the two doors, no more than a few steps.

Jeon Bo-ram stood at her doorway watching him go in. Only when the neighboring door had closed did she slowly close her own.

The room went quiet again.

The bedroom light was on — not glaring, very warm.

Jeon Bo-ram stood where she was for a while, and then the corner of her mouth curved.

Moving here isn’t as bad as I imagined.

· · ·

On the other side of the wall.

Bae Do-yoon returned to his own room and sat back down on the sofa.

The beer on the low table was no longer cold; the chips had gone a little soft.

He looked at his own living room. Empty. Clean. Almost no smell of living in it.

Then he thought of next door: the warm light, the fragrance, the marinated crab.

Bae Do-yoon was silent for two seconds, and then assessed, in a low voice: “This neighbor is not bad.”

He picked up his phone, meaning to go on reading his materials.

But when the screen lit, the search record was still sitting on the earlier page.

Search · still open

The record stays exactly where he left it an hour ago

T-ara · Jeon Bo-ram · the bullying incident

Bae Do-yoon looked at those characters, his fingertip pausing over them.

The Jeon Bo-ram at the dining table just now and the Jeon Bo-ram in the news seemed like two people.

Two files carrying one name

The search page’s versionA person nailed to her past by countless strangers’ labels

The dining table’s versionCannot sleep when the bulb breaks; is embarrassed about treating a neighbor to marinated crab; laughs at a joke before she can stop it

One name held two ways at once is not yet a contradiction. It is only a case that has not been tried.

He does not know the truth of that old storm, and he will not judge it from one late meal. But the small entertainer next door is no longer merely a few search keywords: she is a living person — afraid of the dark, serious about her thanks, and willing to share very good marinated crab with a neighbor she has barely met.

He closed the search page and put the phone aside.

The next morning · before work

The corridor between two doors, then the elevator

A cap, a mask, a trash bag, and a joke about thanking him daily

When Bae Do-yoon woke the next morning, the first thing to surface in his head was not the Prosecutors’ Office, nor the files he meant to read today.

It was soy-sauce crab.

He sat on the edge of the bed in silence for several seconds, and concluded the matter was somewhat dangerous. A prosecutor whose head is full of the idol-next-door’s late-night food before work is not, precisely, a praiseworthy state.

He washed, changed, straightened his tie.

He opened the refrigerator and looked.

Inside: mineral water, beer, and half a pack of kimchi. Nothing else.

He stared at the empty refrigerator for two seconds and closed the door.

Inventory · two kitchens, one floor

Same building, same week of moving in

Her kitchen████░marinated crab from a friend, rice, a pot that smells of dinner

His kitchen█░░░░mineral water, beer, half a pack of kimchi

Both just moved in. Hers already has marinated crab; his looks like it has been searched and seized. Both observations are his, and neither is filed anywhere.

As he went out, the door next door opened at exactly the same moment.

Morning-corridor inventory: the neighbor, off to the trash room

Cap black, back on duty

Mask white, covering nose and mouth; the eyes above it doing the talking

Jacket casual beige, nothing stage-like anywhere

Hands one trash bag, held like a small alibi

Fully disguised for a journey of eleven meters: the door, the corridor, the trash room, the door.

The eyes, however, crinkled in greeting before either of them said a word.

Plate set from the morning-corridor scene, Chapter 11 — cap, mask, trash bag

Their lines of sight collided.

She plainly paused for a beat, then curved her eyes a little, shyly.

“Good morning.”

“Morning.” Bae Do-yoon nodded.

Jeon Bo-ram looked at his shirt and jacket.

“Off to work this early?”

“Mm.”

“Civil servants work hard too, huh?”

Bae Do-yoon thought about it. “Depends on the seniors’ mood.”

Jeon Bo-ram stilled for a second, then laughed a little despite herself. The laugh was light, but markedly more natural than the one at her knocked-on door last night.

Then, as if remembering: “Thank you really for yesterday.”

Bae Do-yoon pressed the elevator button.

“If you thank me every single morning, I’ll start feeling pressure.”

Jeon Bo-ram froze for two seconds before catching up with the fact that he was joking.

Her eyes curved.

The elevator doors opened.

Bae Do-yoon stepped in and looked back once.

“Going.”

“Mm. Take care on the road.”

The doors closed slowly.

Jeon Bo-ram stood in the corridor until his shadow was gone, and only then withdrew her line of sight.

· · ·

The same morning, a little later

Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office · lobby, then his desk

Cold light, coffee, paper — the soft evening rinsed clean off

By the time Bae Do-yoon reached Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office, last night’s soft domestic smell had been rinsed away by the building’s cold lighting, its coffee smell, its paper smell.

The lobby was full of coming and going: administrative officers hugging files walked fast; lawyers in suits spoke into phones in low voices; summoned suspects sat in the corners, faces numb and uneasy at once.

The Prosecutors’ Office is never a place that lets a person relax.

Bae Do-yoon badged through the gate.

He had just sat down at his own desk when his assigned investigative officer, Kim Mi-ju, came over carrying a stack of files.

Thud.

The files landed on the desk, very nearly blocking his view across it.

Kim Mi-ju pushed her glasses up. “Prosecutor Bae — these are for you to look at first, this morning.”

Bae Do-yoon looked up. “This morning?”

Kim Mi-ju nodded. “Yes.”

She paused, then added one more sentence: “There are more this afternoon.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at the pile of files in front of him, and fell into silence.

A note on debts of crab

Everything traded in this chapter is small and none of it is cheap: a bulb for a meal, a meal for a name, a question for a boundary, a joke for a laugh that costs a woman something to give a stranger. Neighbors keep no ledger. This book does, and it files the evening under assets.
