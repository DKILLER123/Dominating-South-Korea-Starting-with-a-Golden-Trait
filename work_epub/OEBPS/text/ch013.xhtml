Chapter Thirteen

Investigating at JYP

The same afternoon · the file open at page one

Criminal Division 3 · his desk, one famous name on the cover

A petition, not a referral — and the difference matters

Bae Do-yoon’s brow lifted, very slightly.

Bae Suzy. Twenty-one years old this year.

JYP’s most in-demand entertainer: high exposure, her face in advertisements, in dramas, in variety shows; crowned with the nickname the Nation’s First Love; hot in the way only a few names in the country are hot.

He read on.

A few pages in, he had found the first thing worth noticing: this material was not an ordinary police referral file. Strictly speaking, it was not yet a criminal case formally inside the Prosecutors’ Office review procedure at all. It was closer to a sensitive bundle asking the prosecution for a preliminary judgment.

He turned pages.

The content itself was not complex. In outline: Bae Suzy had recently suffered several abnormal approaches and continuing harassment. At first only online comments. Then a large volume of emails. Then gifts and letters posted to the company. Most recently, suspicious persons appearing near the agency, at public schedule venues, and around what appeared to be non-public schedule movement routes.

The problem was this: to this day JYP had not confirmed the harasser’s true identity.

The online accounts might be more than one; the email addresses were temporarily registered; the signatures on the sent gifts could not be told true from false. The suspicious figures staff had photographed remained, because of distance and angle, clues only — no identity to be drawn from them. JYP could not even say whether these acts were the work of one person.

Bae Do-yoon read page by page.

Kim Mi-ju stood at the desk’s edge, watching his reaction.

She had expected some flicker of surprise at the name, or at least one “that Bae Suzy?” There was none. From the moment he opened the bundle, his attention sat entirely on the matter itself.

After a while Bae Do-yoon closed the petition letter at the top and asked: “They didn’t report it to the police?”

Kim Mi-ju nodded. “No report was filed.”

Bae Do-yoon was not surprised. He set the petition letter a little to the side.

“The reason?”

Kim Mi-ju said: “JYP’s position is that Bae Suzy is exactly now in the phase where her fame is climbing fastest, and her exposure is too high. If she goes to a police station in the status of victim and the news leaks, the entertainment media will very likely report it at full volume.”

She went on: “The company fears that once the story is out, the public’s focus will not be on the harasser. It will be on Bae Suzy herself.”

She paused, and her voice dropped lower.

“Her private schedules, the safety of her address, gaps in the agency’s security, even her recent work arrangements — all of it could be taken up by the media and guessed at, over and over.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

This, at least, was realistic. When a public figure reports a crime, the reporting is very often not merely the reporting. Once word leaks, the headlines are not obliged to be restrained.

Headlines that would exist the morning after a leak — the company’s fear, itemized

What JYP is actually afraid of

“Bae Suzy appears at a police station late at night.” · “Top female entertainer suspected of being stalked by an obsessive fan.” · “Major gap in JYP’s security.” · “Bae Suzy’s private schedule apparently leaked.”

If those headlines appear, the person twice-harmed is, as ever, the victim herself. And a stalking matter reported at full volume can breed imitators, or push the original harasser’s emotions a step further up. JYP’s unwillingness to make it large first is not hard to understand.

Bae Do-yoon opened the bundle again. “So they chose to hand petition material to the Prosecutors’ Office through their legal department, hoping we judge first whether an investigation needs to be opened?”

“Yes,” Kim Mi-ju said. “They want it handled as quietly as possible. At least until the harasser’s identity is confirmed, they do not want word getting out.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at the attachment index.

Plenty of material. Equally plenty of problems.

He turned to one of the pages and let his gaze rest on the record of a suspicious person near a non-public schedule. His finger tapped the paper, lightly.

“The problem is not that they didn’t report it.”

Kim Mi-ju asked: “Then where is the problem?”

“The problem is that they already know something is wrong, and still cannot say who is doing it. If they keep handling it inside the company alone, then when his conduct escalates again, it will be too late.”

Kim Mi-ju, low: “JYP fears the leak.”

“Understandable,” Bae Do-yoon said. “But fearing a leak is not the same as being unable to investigate.”

He set the bundle back on the desk.

“This matter gets handled quietly — and it gets advanced formally.”

Hearing that sentence, Kim Mi-ju took out her notebook at once.

Bae Do-yoon did not hurry to a conclusion. He ran through the bundle once more at speed, then wrote a few keywords on a sticky note. He set the pen down.

“This is not an ordinary police referral. So the first step is not discussing prosecution, and not discussing how to characterize it.”

“Then what is it?”

“First, confirm three things.”

Three confirmations, dictated before any conclusion

01Whether there is continuity between these acts

02Whether these acts were carried out by one person, or by one group

03How the other side obtained part of the schedule information

The third is the sensitive one. Appearing near public schedules can still be explained as an ordinary fan chasing a schedule. Appearing near non-public ones cannot: either somebody inside is leaking, or the schedules are being obtained illegally — and neither is fixed by simply tightening JYP’s security a little more.

Kim Mi-ju’s pen tip stopped for a beat at the third point.

Instructions to JYP legal, dictated at the desk

What the supplementary submission must look like

Source: Prosecutor Bae Do-yoon, pen down, voice level

Timelinecomplete and in chronological order — not sorted separately into online, email and scene; every act tagged with its time, place, discovering staff, related evidence, and whether Bae Suzy was nearby

Screenshotseach one matched to its original data

Parcelsphotographs are not enough: dispatch records, courier company, sending location, payment method; originals, if still held, not to be discarded

CCTVoriginal files, not screen captures: agency vicinity, public schedule venues, parking lots, vehicle entrances and exits

Sealed apartanything touching Bae Suzy’s address, private schedules, vehicle information or staff contacts — no copying, no lending, no office discussion without my confirmation; you watch this yourself, and do not hand it to unrelated people

And: contact goes through the legal-room lawyer and Bae Suzy’s manager. For now, Bae Suzy herself does not come to the Prosecutors’ Office — the moment she appears here, the case has not begun and the news already has.

Kim Mi-ju was a little surprised. “Not question the person herself first?”

“Not ‘not questioning’,” Bae Do-yoon said. “There is no need to bring her out front at the first step. Confirm the material’s completeness through legal and the manager; when we judge it necessary, we decide whether to question her, and in what manner. If questioning is unavoidable, arrange it in a way that does not expose her movements.”

Kim Mi-ju nodded.

She had been worried, a little. A prosecutor only days into the building, handed sensitive material carrying an entertainer’s name — many newcomers’ first reaction would be excitement, tension, even a wish to use the occasion to shine. Bae Do-yoon had none of it. From the first sentence he had been controlling risk.

Bae Do-yoon’s tone stayed light. “JYP fearing the media is understandable. But fearing the media is not a reason to keep not handling it. The less they want exposure, the more steadily the procedure must be walked before things slip out of control.”

Kim Mi-ju listened without interrupting.

He opened another page of the bundle.

On it, a blurred CCTV capture: a figure in a cap and mask standing near a parking-lot entrance, the angle far too oblique, the face unreadable.

Bae Do-yoon looked at it for several seconds. “The time this person appears — is it after the public schedule ended?”

Kim Mi-ju bent to the note. “According to JYP’s explanation, it is after the movement route was temporarily changed.”

Bae Do-yoon’s gaze sank, a shade.

“Which means this route was not a public schedule.”

“Yes.”

He tapped the capture lightly with his pen tip.

“Then this can no longer be handled as mere fan comments.”

The ladder, rung by rung

Extreme comments online are one thing. Gifts and letters posted to a company are another. Approaching at a public event can still be called a fan losing control. Each rung, alone, has a defense ready for it.

But a figure standing near the movement route of a non-public schedule changes the nature of the whole file. It means the person is not merely hoping to see her once. It means he is continually looking for the chance to get close.

Comments, gifts, approaches: each rung alone is defensible. The ladder is the crime.

Kim Mi-ju asked, low: “Prosecutor — are you saying we can open an investigation?”

Bae Do-yoon did not answer at once.

He read a few more pages before speaking. “We cannot characterize it directly yet. But there is already necessity to open a preliminary investigation. What we are examining now is not whether fans like her. It is who is crossing the line. First identity; then nature.”

“Then the police side…”

“No grand transfer for now,” Bae Do-yoon said. “When JYP has completed the material, I will compile a preliminary opinion for the chief prosecutor. If we later direct a police investigation, that too goes non-public.”

“The focus is confirming the harasser’s identity?”

“Yes. Platform accounts, email sources, dispatch records, CCTV trajectories — string them together and first judge whether it is one subject. If it is one person, that is continuing harassment. If it is not, we confirm whether schedules are leaking from inside.”

At the words internal schedule leak, Kim Mi-ju’s expression turned serious as well.

This was no longer simply an entertainer harassed by a fan. A schedule leak can reach into agency staff, outsourced security, event organizers — even the sasaeng networks that have followed entertainers for years.

Bae Do-yoon re-sorted the bundle and set it at his hand.

“This material does not leave my desk.”

“Yes.”

“When you contact JYP legal, say it plainly: if they want the prosecution’s judgment, they must provide complete material. Not only the parts they wish to give.”

Kim Mi-ju nodded. “I’ll convey it.”

“And this: tell them we will keep the circle of those in the know as small as we can. But judicial procedure is not an agency’s public-relations tool. What must be examined does not go unexamined because it is troublesome.”

Kim Mi-ju was silent a moment, then answered seriously: “Understood.”

Bae Do-yoon looked once at the name on the cover.

A very conspicuous name. And the more conspicuous the name, the less it may be handled conspicuously. In entertainment news she is an entertainer, a star, a public figure. In this bundle she is, first of all, a victim who may be being continually approached and continually harassed.

He closed the folder. “Handle it first by the standards of an ordinary criminal case.”

Kim Mi-ju paused.

Bae Do-yoon said: “No special consideration because she is an entertainer. And no ignoring of risk because she is an entertainer. She needs protection not because she is famous, but because these acts already show danger signals.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at him, and after a moment nodded lightly.

“Yes, Prosecutor.”

Bae Do-yoon clipped the sticky note into the bundle’s first page.

“Go contact JYP legal.”

“Now?”

“Now.”

He took up his pen again.

“The more they fear exposure, the less we may drag this until the day something actually happens.”

Kim Mi-ju left. Leaving, she could not help one more glance at Bae Do-yoon.

His calm, and the line of his thinking on the case, sat far beyond what she had expected.

Is this what genius is?

Kim Mi-ju, plainly, could not possibly know the effect of Bae Do-yoon’s entry, Judicial Monster (purple).

Entry note · Judicial Monster (purple), still running quietly

Working ona petition bundle with a famous name on the cover and holes under it

Visible ascalm, and a line of thinking that keeps arriving at the point everyone else walked past

Not visible asanything at all. No chime, no flash, no explanation offered to the officer standing at the desk

Kim Mi-ju files it under genius. The entry prints no correction.

Around four in the afternoon, Kim Mi-ju brought over JYP’s supplementary material.

This time the folder was visibly thicker. By quantity alone, JYP seemed very cooperative.

But Bae Do-yoon had turned pages for less than five minutes when his brow drew together.

Kim Mi-ju noticed his expression. “Is there a problem with the material?”

“A big problem.”

He drew several pages out and laid them on the desk.

Kim Mi-ju bent to look, and was silent too.

Bae Do-yoon turned a few more pages.

“And this timeline. They write ‘around evening,’ ‘after the event ended,’ ‘around when the vehicle left.’ Descriptions like that have no investigative value. I want specific times, not feelings.”

Kim Mi-ju said, low: “JYP is probably afraid of privacy leaks.”

“I know,” Bae Do-yoon said, closing the folder, tone level. “Which is why what they gave us is not investigation material. It is public-relations material.”

What they gave us is not investigation material. It is public-relations material.

Kim Mi-ju stilled.

Bae Do-yoon leaned back in his chair, thought for a moment, and said: “Contact JYP legal.”

Kim Mi-ju lifted her head. “Now?”

“Now.”

He pushed the folder aside.

“Tell them we are coming over in a little while.”

Kim Mi-ju was plainly surprised. “To JYP?”

“Mm.”

“Prosecutor — you mean to confirm the material on site?”

“If we keep asking them to supplement, we will only receive more processed things,” Bae Do-yoon said. “We need to see the originals.”

Kim Mi-ju looked at him and did not speak for a moment.

She had not expected him to move this fast. Many new prosecutors, handed a sensitive matter like this, would first write another report, wait for the chief’s instruction, or have their officer chase the material again and again. Bae Do-yoon had found the material unusable and gone straight for the source.

She took out her phone at once. “I’ll contact JYP legal now.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded — and then, before she dialed, added one sentence: “Don’t put it too formally.”

Kim Mi-ju stopped. “Your meaning?”

“We are not going to search, and not going to summon. We are only confirming original material, quietly.”

He said it, then glanced at the office’s coming and going.

“Also — change into plain clothes before we go.”

Kim Mi-ju paused, faintly. “Plain clothes?”

“Prosecution people appearing at JYP in formal dress will be noticed,” Bae Do-yoon said. “If the media photograph it, they will write tomorrow’s headlines for us.”

Kim Mi-ju understood quickly.

He went on: “This matter is still at preliminary investigation. The smaller the circle of those in the know, the better. Inside JYP as well — not everyone there needs to know that Bae Suzy is being harassed.”

“Yes. Understood.”

Kim Mi-ju went to contact JYP legal.

A little over ten minutes later the call came back. JYP agreed to let them enter the company and view the relevant original material — on condition of prior identity registration, and accompaniment by legal-room staff throughout.

This, too, sat inside Bae Do-yoon’s expectations. JYP was not a small company, after all; and the bigger such a company is, the more it prizes access control.

But precisely for that reason.

If the company’s internal management is truly this strict — how has that unknown person approached Bae Suzy, again and again?

Plate set from the JYP field visit, Chapter 13 — plain clothes, badge left at the office

Kim Mi-ju looked around once.

Access control, cameras, visitor registration, a security booth: everything looked thoroughly proper, thoroughly official.

She said, low: “The security here looks very strict.”

Bae Do-yoon also glanced at the camera over the entrance.

“Which makes it stranger.”

Kim Mi-ju paused. Bae Do-yoon did not explain further.

Soon a man in a dark suit came out from the elevator direction. Mid-thirties, thin-framed glasses, an air cautious without ceasing to be polite. He reached the two of them, confirmed identities first, then inclined himself slightly.

“Prosecutor Bae. Officer Kim.”

He lowered his voice. “I am Park Dae-jun of JYP’s legal room. Thank you both for coming in person.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “Sorry for the sudden visit.”

“Not at all — it is our side whose material preparation was insufficient.”

Park Dae-jun spoke with great courtesy, and yet with a few parts of carefulness left in the tone. He did not linger at the visitor entrance; once the temporary badges were confirmed issued, he led Bae Do-yoon and Kim Mi-ju toward the internal meeting room.

JYP’s office area was stricter than the outside suggested. At the corridor’s end, signs for practice rooms and recording studios; along the wall, staff hurrying past with documents in their arms; from another passage, young people of trainee age crossing.

Bae Do-yoon did not look around.

He followed quietly at Park Dae-jun’s side, his gaze touching only now and then the positions of access controls, the angles of cameras, the flow lines of people.

But his not looking at others did not mean others were not looking at him.

From the moment they entered the office area, lines of sight had been settling on Bae Do-yoon, quietly. At first only passing staff glancing once more. Then even several young people standing at a practice-room door could not help turning their heads.

At a practice-room door, half aloud

Who is that?

A new actor? He doesn’t look like a trainee, does he? But his looks are too…

The sentence broke off halfway, because Park Dae-jun was visible leading the way ahead, and the voices dropped at once.

A strange face in a building like this is news; a strange face that looks like that is a rumor.

Bae Do-yoon seemed not to notice any of it; his face kept its usual lack of surplus expression.

Kim Mi-ju, though, noticed.

She had been observing JYP’s internal security layout; but walking on, she found the way people looked at Bae Do-yoon somewhat wrong. It was not the look given to a prosecutor. It was closer to guessing at a new entertainer who had suddenly appeared in the company.

Park Dae-jun had plainly noticed too.

He tilted his head and smiled, low-voiced: “The Prosecutor’s image is very good. I’m afraid they all take you for a newly arrived entertainer.”

Bae Do-yoon’s steps did not stop; he only glanced at him, lightly.

“Lawyer Park is joking.”

“Not a joke,” Park Dae-jun said, lowering his voice. “People here are sensitive about this kind of thing. A stranger’s face appearing in the company — and one whose outward conditions stand out exactly so — is very easily misread.”

Hearing this, Kim Mi-ju turned her head toward Bae Do-yoon by instinct.

Inside the Prosecutors’ Office, because of the identity and the occasion, what she had noticed in him was mostly his calm, his judgment, his way of working. But standing here in JYP, in a building full of entertainers and trainees, she realized suddenly that his outward form was indeed exceptional. Tall and straight, shoulders and back very straight. Even today, dressed down for discretion in simple plain clothes, that clean, decisive air was not suppressed. His features’ lines were clear, his brows and eyes calm, his nose high; there was no deliberately groomed precision on his face, and that made him, rather, more worth looking at than many packaged male entertainers. Silent especially, the whole of him read as composed.

That air, in the Prosecutors’ Office, makes people feel him young but not easy to approach. In an entertainment company, it makes him easy to mistake for an unannounced new actor, or a debutant the company is quietly preparing.

If Bae Do-yoon were not a prosecutor and entered the entertainment world instead, he would probably do very well too. On this face and this air alone, plenty of people would turn their heads.

The thought stayed in her head for only an instant.

Quickly she pulled her attention back to the matter in front of them. They had come to JYP today not to tour an entertainment company, and certainly not to have anyone judge whether Bae Do-yoon should debut. Bae Suzy’s stalking matter was still unsolved.

And the deeper they walked into this strictly managed building, the more wrong it felt to Kim Mi-ju.

If JYP’s internal access control is strict to this degree — how has that unknown person reached Bae Suzy’s non-public schedules, time after time?

Bae Do-yoon, plainly, had arrived at the same thought.

Bae Suzy

JYP entertainer, twenty-one; the Nation’s First Love, in this file first of all a victim

Advertisements, dramas, variety shows — a face the country already knows by heart

Named on a petition cover before she is named in any room; not yet reported to any police station

Comments, then emails, then gifts, then figures at non-public routes: a ladder nobody can yet prove is one climber. Her company edits her timeline to protect her; her prosecutor asks for the times, not the feelings.

The petition

On public-relations material dressed as investigation material

“Around evening,” “after the event ended,” “around when the vehicle left”: a company afraid of leaks will edit its own victim’s file until the file protects the company instead. Nobody in this chapter is lying. That is what makes it dangerous. A timeline written in feelings cannot catch a man who plans his appearances around cameras — and the prosecutor’s whole insistence this afternoon is one plain sentence: give me times.
