Chapter Twenty-Eight

The First Person Who Came to Mind Was Her?

The same afternoon · the cleared meeting room, continued

Window light on the table; condensation sliding slow down the cup wall

One regrettable answer, still on the table between them

“Regrettable?” The word made Bae Do-yoon blink, caught off guard.

Bae Suzy propped her chin in her palm, looking at him, a smile in her eyes.

“Prosecutor Bae is this excellent, and good-looking besides, and yet has no girlfriend. Viewed from an objective angle — isn’t that very regrettable?”

Bae Do-yoon did not take it up at once.

Bae Suzy seemed to read his silence; her smile deepened.

“You don’t need to be so wary. I said it: objective angle.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“That sentence of yours just now — its subjective component is not low.”

Bae Suzy stilled, and then laughed out loud.

“So you could hear it all along.”

“I merely haven’t exposed it every time.”

“Then why expose it now?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Because you didn’t look like you were asking in passing.”

When that sentence fell, Bae Suzy’s fingers around the cup stopped.

She had only meant to push the topic lightly toward the ambiguous; she had not expected Bae Do-yoon not to retreat, but to catch it directly.

She held his eyes a moment; the smile in them grew slowly more distinct.

“Prosecutor Bae.”

“Mm.”

“Talking in that tone keeps leaving the door open for misunderstandings.”

Bae Do-yoon’s expression did not change.

“You have made that very complaint once before today.”

“Because you have indeed been manufacturing that possibility all along.”

“You were the one who asked first.”

Bae Suzy narrowed her eyes faintly, as if struck true and unwilling to admit it.

“So — is this a counterattack?”

“No.”

“Then what is it?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her a moment.

“An answer.”

Two very simple words.

And yet they did not fall past her; they landed, softly, somewhere in her chest.

She lowered her eyes to the ice in the cup, and asked, lightly:

“If I really threw a temper — what then?”

“Then I wait until you have finished throwing it.”

Bae Suzy lifted her eyes to him.

“You wouldn’t find it troublesome?”

“I wouldn’t.”

“Why not?”

“Because if I am the one staying to comfort you, then I have already decided to take on that portion of trouble. Whatever happens, it is within my psychological expectations.”

Bae Suzy’s breath paused, faintly. The sentence was spoken too calmly — and the calmer it was, the less it sounded like a joke.

She looked at him; several seconds passed before she gave one low laugh.

“Prosecutor Bae.”

“Mm.”

“Do you know — saying it like that is more of a rule violation than saying pretty words directly.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“A rule violation?”

“Mm.” She nodded lightly. “Because you look entirely unlike someone comforting anyone — and yet the words, once said, make a person feel… as if for a little while, the trouble could really be handed over to you.”

But she quickly lifted her head again, as if unwilling to be led by him, and drew the topic back toward the ambiguous.

“But what I asked just now wasn’t that.”

“Then what did you ask?”

The corner of Bae Suzy’s mouth curved, slowly.

“I asked how Prosecutor Bae would comfort someone.”

“I already answered.”

“But that was only the content.” She watched him, smile gathering in her eyes. “I want to know — in what tone of voice would you comfort someone?”

This time Bae Do-yoon plainly stilled for two seconds.

Bae Suzy caught the pause; the smile in her eyes deepened.

“Is this question difficult?”

“A little harder than the last one.”

“Why?”

“Because a tone of voice is hard to describe.”

Bae Suzy cupped her chin in both hands and leaned a little forward.

“Then no need to describe it. You can demonstrate.”

The sentence was more direct than any of her earlier probes.

Having said it, Bae Suzy herself knew it crossed a line a little. But she did not retreat; she only looked at Bae Do-yoon, laughter in her eyes, and a curiosity she made no effort to hide.

Bae Do-yoon let a moment pass before answering.

After a while, he spoke, in a lowered voice:

“Bae Suzy.”

He did not call her Ms. Bae. He called her name.

Bae Suzy’s gaze paused, faintly. The single address came too suddenly; she even forgot to go on smiling.

The room, at the lowered voice

Her hypothetical, lowered: a temper thrown in full; a trouble handed across the table; a comforter who would not count it troublesome.

His answer, level as ever: waited out to the end; the trouble pre-accepted, sitting already inside his expectations of himself.

Said that calmly, it stops sounding like a joke — and for a little while the trouble feels hand-over-able, which is the most rule-violating thing of all.

Two people, one closed door, and a tone of voice still undescribed. Then, from her: descriptions fail — demonstrate instead.

“You don’t have to force yourself to be strong now, and you don’t have to hurry to make anyone feel at ease,” he said.

Bae Suzy’s fingers tightened, lightly.

Bae Do-yoon went on: “You may feel annoyed. You may feel wronged. You may say nothing at all. I am here.”

Bae Suzy looked at him without blinking.

She had meant to watch Bae Do-yoon grow cornered — to see whether a man this calm could be questioned into silence. She had not expected him truly to demonstrate; still less had she expected that once he lowered his voice and called her name, the effect would be this.

Quiet. Restrained. And yet it made something in the chest soften, for no reason she could name.

She lowered her eyes; several seconds passed before she gave a small laugh.

That laugh was not the composed one from before. It carried a little of the fluster of someone struck.

“Prosecutor Bae.”

“Mm.”

“What you just said…” She paused, as if seeking the right wording. “…has already exceeded the ordinary range of comforting people.”

Bae Do-yoon’s tone returned to its usual level; he said, lightly:

“You asked me to demonstrate.”

“I didn’t expect you really would.”

“But you were asking seriously.”

Bae Suzy laughed. “I was plainly teasing you.”

Bae Do-yoon gave a light laugh.

“And yet you wanted to hear it. Didn’t you?”

Bae Suzy looked at him; the corners of her mouth pressed together slowly. After a while she lowered her head and laughed out.

“Prosecutor Bae — you are truly dangerous.”

“You have said that many times today.”

“Because every time, it is true.”

Bae Do-yoon did not refute it.

Bae Suzy lifted a hand and touched her earlobe, like an unconscious small motion. The tips of her ears were a little red — not obvious, but visible under the bright meeting-room light.

She herself seemed to notice; she put her hand down at once and, pretending nothing was the matter, took up her coffee.

But the coffee was no longer cold.

She drank a sip, and set it down again.

She does not press. She only looks at him, quietly, waiting for whatever the silence gives up first.

“Prosecutor Bae.” Bae Suzy curved her eyes. “I have one last question.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her and lifted a brow. “Ms. Bae — your questions are a little many today.”

“Truly the last one. When I’ve asked it I’ll let you out; otherwise my manager may really call the police.”

Bae Do-yoon glanced toward the door. “Ask, then.”

Bae Suzy did not speak at once. She seemed to be weighing her wording — or perhaps stretching these few seconds on purpose.

Outside the window the light lay on the table; on the wall of the iced americano cup, beads of water slid slowly downward.

After a while she asked, softly:

“Prosecutor Bae — the girl you like, what would she be like?”

As the words fell, Bae Do-yoon’s motion stopped. He looked at Bae Suzy once.

The question asked neither fact nor hypothesis. It asked his heart.

Bae Suzy did not press him either. She only looked at him quietly, a little expectation in her eyes, and a little probing. It was asked like a joke — but she did not truly want only a joke’s answer.

Bae Do-yoon thought about it seriously for several seconds.

And just as he tried to offer a rational, blurred, sufficiently safe answer — a person surfaced in his mind.

Many years ago · Seoul

An unfamiliar street corner; later, a stairwell behind the practice rooms; later still, one late-night call

Before the stage, before the lights, before the praised photographs

Irene, before Irene

A girl with a not-large suitcase at an unfamiliar street corner, newly come to Seoul from Daegu, nervous to death and working hard to look composed. Not yet debuted. No stage, no lights, none of the later beautiful photographs people would praise.

Only a trainee: uncorrected accent, few words, the habit of watching others first and opening her mouth carefully afterward. By several accidents they grew familiar. Her first friend in Seoul was him.

He saw the times she could not hide it: sitting in the stairwell after practice, head lowered, rubbing her ankle, the rims of her eyes already red and still insisting I’m fine. And the late-night call at last, silence a long while, then one low question: Bae Do-yoon, am I perhaps not suited for this place?

He did not say many comforting words then. He walked a stretch of road with her, bought her a hot drink, and told her: if you still want to stay tomorrow, don’t decide today. Later she truly stayed — year after year, growing steadier, more beautiful, harder for others to read through.

And yet in his eyes she has remained that girl at the unfamiliar street corner, making herself look unafraid.

Irene.

The girl who, many years ago when she had first come to Seoul, dragged a not-large suitcase and stood at an unfamiliar street corner — nervous to death, and still striving to look composed.

At that time she had not yet debuted. No stage, no lights, none of the later beautiful photographs that people praised.

She was only a trainee come to Seoul from Daegu. Unfamiliar with the place and the people, her accent not yet fully corrected, not many words; she had the habit of observing others first, then opening her mouth carefully.

Later, by several accidents, the two of them slowly grew familiar.

Her first friend in Seoul was him.

Bae Do-yoon had always remembered this. Only he had never placed it in any special position. To him, Irene was a friend — a person present across many years, a person with whom he need not exchange greetings at every meeting nor explain too much specially. A person who, sending only one line — are you busy? — let him know she probably truly had something. A person who, though very much afraid of troubling others, always showed a little of her real temper in front of him.

She was not good at showing weakness. Even in those first hard years in Seoul she always said I’m okay first. She hid her sadness, hid her unease, hid her homesickness.

But Bae Do-yoon had seen the times she could not hide them.

He had seen her, worn out by practice, sit in the stairwell with her head lowered rubbing her ankle, the rims of her eyes already red and still insisting, I’m fine.

He had seen her at last, on some late night, call him; silent a long while; and then ask, in a low voice, only one sentence:

“Bae Do-yoon — am I perhaps not suited for this place?”

He had not said many comforting words then. He had only walked a stretch of road with her, bought her a hot drink, and told her:

“If you still want to stay tomorrow, don’t decide today.”

Later she truly stayed. Year after year. Little by little she grew more composed, more beautiful, and less easy for others to see through. But in Bae Do-yoon’s eyes she seemed always that girl who, first come to Seoul, stood at the unfamiliar street corner making herself look unafraid.

Thinking here, Bae Do-yoon’s brow drew together, lightly.

Why her?

Why, when Bae Suzy asked what the girl he liked would be like, was the first person to leap into his mind Irene?

They had known each other too long. He had almost grown used to Irene’s existence. Worrying about her — because of friendship. Remembering her habits — because of friendship. Hearing the wrongness in her tone — because they had known each other too long. Frowning when she occasionally lied and said she was fine — because he understood her. Glancing at his phone without meaning to when she had not been in contact a long while — also because they were friends of many years.

Bae Do-yoon had always explained it this way, and had always found the explanation sufficiently reasonable.

But if she was only a friend — why, when this question appeared, was she the first he thought of?

Bae Suzy had been watching him all along, and noticed the change in him.

The smile in her eyes quieted, slowly.

“This question…” she asked, softly. “Is it very hard to answer?”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his head; their gazes met.

Bae Suzy was still smiling, but the smile was no longer as light as before. She was clever; she sensed that behind the answer there might be something else.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, silent a moment.

“It isn’t hard to answer.”

“Then what is it?”

Bae Do-yoon knew he could give many safe answers. For instance: liking clear-headed people, liking independent people, liking people with a sense of measure. None of these would be wrong — and all of them could correspond to the Bae Suzy sitting before him.

But just now, when those words began to condense into a concrete outline in his mind, he hesitated.

“I have never seriously thought about this question before.”

Bae Suzy looked at him several seconds. “And now you have?”

“Just now, I thought about it a little.”

“And the answer?”

“Clear-headed. Resilient. With her own judgment.”

“Not easily pushed along by her environment; not giving up what she wants to do out of fear; though it is very hard, always saying she is okay first; quiet-seeming, but in truth very stubborn.”

Saying this, he stopped. Because as he spoke, he himself became aware of it.

This was not an abstract answer. This was a person.

Finding: not an abstract answer — a person. The file notes no name; the silence supplies one all the same.

Safe answers were available (clear-headed, independent, measured — all fitting the listener herself) and were refused at the moment of condensing. Refusal unexplained on the record.

Bae Suzy met his gaze; the smile in her eyes thinned a little.

She was not failing to understand. On the contrary — she understood too well.

An answer like this, if given in passing, would not be so concrete. Only when a person holds a reference in his mind does he describe the type he likes the way one describes someone who truly exists.

Bae Suzy blinked, lightly.

She kept her tone natural; she even smiled once.

“She sounds like a very good person.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“Mm.”

Bae Suzy’s fingers around the cup moved, lightly.

She had only meant to tease him — to see whether a man as restrained as Bae Do-yoon, asked about the type he liked, would give a blurred and safe answer. She had not expected that though he spoke with restraint, he seemed to leak the true answer without meaning to.

And at that answer, a little disappointment rose in Bae Suzy’s heart.

The disappointment came somewhat suddenly; she herself found it a little laughable. It was only a question asked in passing; she and Bae Do-yoon had after all met only a few times today and before. And yet she was a little disappointed — because she had always been confident in her own charm.

This was not conceit. It was the clear-headed self-knowledge formed across all those years standing before cameras, on stages, at the center of countless gazes. She knew she was beautiful; she knew she was clever enough, and good enough at keeping distance. She knew when to smile, when to retreat, when one light joke could make the air turn ambiguous. She knew also that most men, faced with such a question from her, found it hard to remain wholly unmoved.

Especially just now.

Bae Do-yoon had not failed to respond. He caught her words; he looked at her; and when she asked what if I need comforting, he truly lowered his voice and called her name. That one “Bae Suzy,” and that “I am here,” still seemed to fall upon her ear even now.

That was why she had asked this last question.

She was not so naive as to expect his answer to lean toward her. But in the instant of asking, she had indeed held a little expectation — that his answer would contain at least a little that came near her; even one blurred word she could claim half in jest, and she could catch it in passing and draw the air back into light ambiguity.

But what Bae Do-yoon said was none of these.

Bae Suzy listened quietly; the light, floating competitive spirit in her heart was suddenly touched.

Not jealousy — at least not yet that. It was a subtle refusal to concede.

So not everyone sees her first.

So in the heart of a man like Bae Do-yoon there is also a place earlier, deeper, more concrete.

And that place is not one that beauty, fame, well-timed jokes, or one brief ambiguity can easily squeeze into.

The discovery made her a little dispirited. It also made her more curious about Bae Do-yoon.

Because a man who is not easily drawn by her into losing his judgment is, rather, the one more likely to stay in her mind.

Bae Suzy bent her head over the cup and drank. She did not let that dispiritedness linger long. One of the things an excellent artist is best at is tidying an emotion away before anyone can read it.

She understood measure well, and knew clearly: to ask further now would no longer be ambiguity, but pressing.

But that refusal to concede in her heart did not wholly scatter. She rarely met this situation. It was not that no one had ever refused her, nor that she thought everyone ought to like her. It was only that Bae Do-yoon just now had been so plainly close to her. She had almost thought that if she stepped one pace further, he would give an answer letting her keep probing.

And yet what he thought of at the last was someone else.

This made Bae Suzy a little dispirited, and a little wanting to laugh.

That she too should have such a moment. That because a man’s answer about the type he liked did not lean toward her, so plain a gap should arise.

Before, she probably would not have admitted it. But now she had to admit: Bae Do-yoon had indeed given her a competitive spirit.

She wanted to know who that person was — the one concrete enough to make him silent, make him frown, even make him briefly lose focus.

And what kind of person could make a man as calm and restrained as Bae Do-yoon show so obvious a crack before a single question.

She lifted her head again, smiling very naturally.

“Prosecutor Bae’s answer just now sounds very much like describing someone you have known a long time.”

Bae Do-yoon smiled, lightly.

Bae Suzy tilted her head.

“Did I guess right?”

“Sort of.”

Bae Suzy nodded.

She did not press further.

Only that curiosity in her heart quietly deepened, one degree more.

On first persons and tidied faces

Ask a man who has never examined his own heart what the girl he likes would be like, and watch him reach for safe words — clear-headed, independent, measured — only to find the words condensing into a suitcase at an unfamiliar street corner, an uncorrected Daegu accent, a stairwell and a rubbed ankle, a late-night call and a hot drink and one sentence about not deciding today. He has an alibi for every item on the list (friendship, friendship, friendship, friendship, friendship) and it fails on first cross-examination: if only a friend, why her first? Across the table, a woman who has spent her whole career at the center of gazes feels, for the first time in a long while, not seen first — and tidies the disappointment away before it can be read, because an excellent artist’s skill is exactly that, and because pressing now would not be flirting but interrogation. She leaves the room carrying a competitive spirit she would not have admitted an hour ago: to know the name of the crack in a calm man’s armor. He leaves carrying something heavier and lighter both — the discovery that his oldest explanation no longer explains. And between them, on the table, one melted iced americano that neither of them will ever mention again.
