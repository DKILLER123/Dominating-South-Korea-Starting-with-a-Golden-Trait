Chapter Five

The Two of Them, Two Years Ago

Near noon

A rented one-bedroom, facing the wall of the building across the way

The address a young prosecutor can explain, which is the entire reason he lives there

Bae Do-yoon got home close to noon.

What he called home was a rented flat.

Small: one bedroom and one living room. A kitchen you could stand in alone. In the living room an old sofa and a low table, and outside the window the wall of the building opposite and a few trees trimmed within an inch of their lives.

It was not good. It was clean, it was quiet, and the people around it were not complicated.

Mostly staff from nearby offices, a couple of young teachers, and a family or two with someone in the civil service. The neighborhood was safe enough and the management could be described as responsible.

For a man in his position it fit perfectly.

Not flashy. Not conspicuous. And nobody could take one look at the doorway and see a chaebol standing behind it.

Lee Boo-jin, if she liked, could have put him in a good apartment in Gangnam and could have had the title drawn up in his name by the afternoon.

He could not live in one.

Because he is a prosecutor.

And that job is, in itself, sensitive: it means power, and it means being looked at.

In a country like this one the distance between a prosecutor and a chaebol is measured by people who do not have either.

A young man not long out of the system suddenly living in a mansion his income cannot explain, with an expensive car and clothes he would have to know the prices of — even with no evidence of anything, that is enough for a city to write the rest of the story itself.

I’m not going to hand anyone a knife. And I’m not going to be caught out on a flaw this stupid before I have anything under my feet.

The image, kept on display

Public-facing, maintained deliberately, reviewed by him more often than by any inspector

Incorruptiblethe rent is high for the area and normal for the salary

Low-profileno car, no logo, no photograph of him standing in front of anything

Ordinarythe sort of tenant the building’s concierge forgets on purpose

Cost of maintaining this: one apartment he does not like

Corruption is not the crime that gets a young prosecutor. Being seen to be able to afford his own life is.

He opened the door, dropped his keys into the dish by the entrance, took the jacket off, could not be bothered to change into slippers, walked into the bedroom and lay back on the bed flat, with his arm across his eyes.

The mattress gave a little under him.

From last night until this morning, everything that had happened was absurd.

Being arranged into a hotel. A woman in the room. A camera. Walking into an office. Meeting whoever has been running it all.

The woman who stands at the top of this country had told him, without much emphasis, that she had been watching him since he was a child.

She had said: an orphan. Clean. Suitable to cultivate. I am going to make a blade out of you.

With his eyes shut, the corner of his mouth pulled into a small, sarcastic line.

A blade? That’s a beautiful word for it. The plain one is: a tool.

Being held like this is a filthy feeling.

But he has no standing to turn the table over. He is too weak.

Even with both the halo of a rebirth and a golden finger handed to him at birth, a man can still be standing at the bottom of a hole, looking up, and completely stuck.

Bae Do-yoon does not like lying to himself.

The cooperation, restated by the party at a disadvantage

Consideration moving downward

Resources. Intelligence. Connections. Protection. A route upward that somebody else has graded.

Consideration moving upward

A blade, expected to handle the trouble she cannot be seen touching.

What the paperwork would say

A trade between two consenting adults.

What it is

Control. Only, from today, out in the open instead of in the walls. Before, the strings were invisible; now she has simply introduced herself.

He will not trust her, and he will not spend his life for her. Both of those decisions were made before he sat down in that office.

A woman who has stood that high has met a great many clever men and has used a great many of them.

In front of her, a young man’s conceit is usually the funniest object in the room.

Bae Do-yoon admits that he is clever.

He has a previous life in his head, he has entries, and his judgment is sharper than most people he has to work with.

Clever, though, is not the same as grown up. And it is certainly not the same as winning.

What Lee Boo-jin holds is resources, power, years, and an ocean of information that he is not standing in.

She does not need to deceive him.

She needs only to give him part of the truth, leave out the load-bearing part, and he will walk in the direction she wants, with excellent posture.

That is the dangerous kind. She will not put a knife to your throat.

She will walk you down a road she laid years ago, and let you keep telling people — and mostly believing — that you chose it.

· · ·

He lowered his arm and looked at the ceiling.

The most urgent problem is not pride.

It is that camcorder.

However lightly she has talked about it, what is on that tape is a handle that can cut his life open.

Has Jessica declined a great deal?

Of course she has.

She is not any longer the top of the heap, the girl every entertainment desk in the country was chasing at the height of Girls’ Generation.

But a lean camel still outsizes a horse.

The name Jessica still carries weight in this industry. She has fans. She has recognition. She has the kind of national memory that does not expire when a contract does.

And more than all of that: she comes with a story attached, and the story sells.

If that recording ever goes out, it is not a scandal about two adults in a room.

How it plays out, written in advance

01He is a prosecutor: put prosecutor and female idol in one headline, with a hotel and a recording in the second paragraph.

02The fans of this industry will tear him apart, and they will do it in shifts so that the tag never comes down.

03The public will ask how corrupt the prosecution is — a question that has nothing to do with the facts and everything to do with the mood.

04Media will arrive like sharks that have found blood in the water, and they will stay like sharks.

05And the prosecutors’ office, needing to be seen protecting its own clean image, will not shield him. It will offer him up as the case they were tough about.

None of this requires the tape to be true. It only requires the tape to exist.

The visible part, in order

FirstSuspended from duty

ThenAn internal investigation, opened for the record and closed for the cameras

ThenA disciplinary sanction, printed in language nobody outside reads

ThenA public apology, in a corridor, with lights he will hate

If it is classified badlyA cell, and the word “prosecutor” used as a lesson

And that is only the part that gets stamped.

The quiet version is worse and cheaper. Lee Boo-jin does not need to show the video to anybody. She needs only to let certain people know that a recording like this exists, and that it is in her hand. That is enough to make Bae Do-yoon lower his head at exactly the moments it costs him most to lower it.

His eyes went colder, degree by degree, while he thought it through.

So the camcorder has to be dealt with.

Not today.

Today he does not know where it is kept, how many copies were made, or who is allowed to touch it.

Going to grab it, or steal it, or threaten somebody about it, would be stupid in a way that gets remembered.

Before I pull this handle out, I have to learn the shape of the machine that holds it.

Thinking about the machine, he turned his head and looked through the bedroom doorway at the desk.

On the wall beside the desk a few photographs were pinned.

Not many. Bae Do-yoon is not a man who decorates a room with his own sentiment; he finds it untidy.

But one of them he has never taken down.

In it: himself, two years ago, and Jessica.

What the photograph contains

A stage event, a lucky member of an audience, and two people standing close enough for one frame.

He has not entered the prosecutor’s system in it. He is greener, and the unfair face is already there.

Her makeup is exact. Her expression has that coolness that belongs to her alone, and she is smiling at the camera a little — not warmly. Beautiful anyway.

Two years ago, Girls’ Generation had done an offline event for a variety programme.

A member of the audience was drawn by lot to take a photograph with one of them.

Bae Do-yoon had only been there to watch the crowd.

It is not that he is a devoted chaser of anybody. It is that the memories of his previous life made him feel something hard to sort out about people he used to be able to see only through a screen.

It’s like an old world’s shadow walking into the room while you’re still standing in it.

Then his name came out of the box.

Luck like that does not happen twice in a life, and he had already used up two lives’ worth.

The host asked him, on the mic, which member he wanted beside him.

Under the stage the crowd shouted names. Someone yelled Yoona. Someone yelled Taeyeon. Someone yelled Jessica.

Bae Do-yoon had looked at the few of them standing up there.

Yoona was beautiful. Of course she was. Being the face of the group is not a title you get given out of politeness; she stood the way a person stands when a camera was invented for her.

He picked Jessica.

For no other reason at all.

It was just that in that moment Jessica was standing under the lights with a slightly cold face, and she looked like the only person on that loud stage who did not entirely belong to the noise.

He had found that interesting.

“I’ll take Jessica.”

A howl went up from the crowd, and some of it was delight and some of it was outrage on behalf of the other five.

Jessica had come over to him and nodded, politely, the way she would nod at any of a thousand fans.

“Hello.”

He remembered that he had smiled too.

“Hello.”

They came closer. The photographer raised the camera and did his job.

Click.

And there was the photograph.

A meeting too short to have any shape at all.

For Jessica it was one fragment out of hundreds of obligations, the sort that does not make it into a diary.

She has met too many people and stood for too many pictures to keep every face.

So it is perfectly normal that she did not know him last night.

Two years is a long time in any case, and the way he carries himself has changed: from a young student into a prosecutor one week inside the machine.

And with what the night had been, who would have the presence of mind to go digging in an old memory of a stage?

Bae Do-yoon looked at the photograph, and there was no single thing in his face.

It had been a chance. A short intersection between a stage and the people bought a ticket to stand in front of it.

And neither of them could have guessed that the second time they truly met would be inside a room at the Shilla.

Fate doesn’t content itself with pushing you around. First it gives you something that looks beautiful and lets you keep it for two years. Then it hands the same person back to you in the most ridiculous way available.

He sat up suddenly.

He went to the desk and took the photograph down off the wall.

The Jessica in it was still beautiful.

She did not know, then, that in two years’ time she would be walked into his hotel room by people she could not refuse.

And he did not know, then, that the girl he had picked out of a lineup on a stage would one day become the first lit fuse in his life.

He stood there looking at it for a long time.

Unavoidably, the images came back: her cold face. The anger held down inside a jaw. The disgust that crossed the bottom of her eyes once and was locked away again.

And this morning, her back to him, saying nothing while she dressed.

He looked down at the photograph and said, under his breath:

“This is rotten.”

It was not clear who the sentence was for: Lee Boo-jin, or the world, or the man he had been at two in the morning.

He put the photograph down gently, went out to the living room, poured a glass of water, and drank it in one pull.

Ice-cold, all the way down, and it did what water does: washed nothing, cleared the top layer of it.

He came back to the desk, opened a drawer, and took out a black notebook.

It is a habit of his.

When a situation is complicated he does not let his head carry it, because a head is not a fair witness.

Anger bends it. Shame bends it. Fear and want bend it.

Write it down, line by line, and the board in front of you stops being a feeling and starts being a set of facts.

He turned to a blank page, and at the top wrote one name.

Lee Boo-jin.

Then, under it:

Black notebook · page one

1. Objective: to raise a prosecutor who belongs to her alone.

2. Method: watching from the welfare home onward. Funding. Steering the route.

3. Present relationship: cooperation on the surface, control underneath.

4. What she holds: the Shilla Hotel recording, and Jessica’s name on it.

5. Exposure: public opinion, the prosecutors’ office, his whole life, prison if it is graded badly.

6. What can be used: the intelligence she supplies, the connections, the case entries, the protection.

7. Where she is open: the blade that came before him; the assistant at her elbow; the hotel machine; Jessica.

When he wrote her name in line seven the pen point stopped.

A few seconds later it went on, pressing harder than before:

Jessica: sent in last night. Not fully a volunteer. Confirm her objective, her position, and whether she holds any thread of her own.

Bae Do-yoon · 12:40 · in slippers, on a bed he has not made

He put the pen down and leaned back, and let out a long breath.

The board is bad.

It is not unsolvable.

At least he now knows who the hidden hand is. He knows the shape of the control he is under. And he knows something that matters more than either of them: Lee Boo-jin needs him.

Being controlled by somebody is frightening, yes.

But when the person controlling you also needs you, then that relationship has a gap in it.

And a gap is where everything gets in.

· · ·

He shut the notebook, and his eyes had gone quiet.

Lee Boo-jin wants to raise a blade.

All right.

Then he will be her blade — first.

He will borrow the whetstone. He will borrow the resources, the connections, and the corridor of power she has opened for him, and he will walk down it as far as it goes.

And when the edge is actually sharp, I’ll be the one deciding what it points at.

The same noon

The Shilla · the front door, going out

Sunlight, a glass door, and a woman with nothing visible on her face

Jessica left the hotel into good weather.

The glass doors at the entrance kept her reflection for a few metres as she walked: sunglasses, a mask, long hair, a trench coat, heels.

Armour, in five pieces

Sunglasses dark enough for a bright day, which is to say: for a day when the eyes must not be read

Mask black, plain, professional — the accessory of a woman who has been photographed at her worst before

Hair down, long, used as a frame rather than a style

Coat a trench, belt done up in a building where she could have let it hang

Heels high, and walked in exactly as if nothing has happened for two days

From the outside: cold, exquisite, unimprovable. The same Jessica who walks through a arrivals hall.

Nobody at that door knows what she got through last night. Nobody knows how tired the eyes are behind the glasses.

Plate set from her card portrait — the arrivals hall, Chapter 5

She put a car on the app and sat in the back and closed her eyes.

The pictures came, unavoidably.

She had been expecting somebody disgusting: a chaebol’s son, drunk on being one; or a middle-aged man who has made a hobby of using what he can get.

He had not been either.

The man last night was not gentle, and he had spent part of his fury on her body, and she hated him for that.

She was not, though, blind to the rest of it.

In some moments the way he looked at her was not the way of a man enjoying something he had been given.

We were put together by one hand, she thought, in the back of a car, with her mask on and nobody in the world able to see her mouth. He was just pushed onto a board instead of a bed.

And for no reason she could name, she had the feeling she had seen him before.

Not last night. Earlier.

Someplace cleaner. Lights, a crowd, somebody with a camera.

The memory is too blurred to hold, and for the moment it gives her nothing.

The car stopped under her building.

Jessica got out, put her face right in the mirror of her own mind, checked that nothing shows, and went up.

The door of the flat was unlocked, and there was television noise coming from the living room.

Krystal was on the sofa with a cushion hugged to her chest, and at the sound of the door she sat up at once.

“Unnie?”

Then, seeing her, her brow shut.

“Where were you last night?”

“Why would you never pick up? I called you until I lost count.”

Missed

A phone that has been face-down in a handbag since yesterday evening.

Unnie — calls all through the night, and then one more at the hour the offices open.

Unnie — messages sent, then stopped, then sent again.

Not one of them reached her, because the phone has been switched off at the bottom of a bag since yesterday evening. It is the only thing she has decided for herself in the last forty hours.

Jessica stopped, one hand still on the strap of her bag, in the middle of changing her shoes.

She looked down at her heels on the entry tile and something behind her eyes went darker.

How could she tell her?

That people from Samsung asked her out, then threatened her, then sent her to the Shilla, and that she spent the night with a strange man.

That they used the careers of two sisters as the stake on the table.

And above all, she cannot let Krystal understand that part of the reason she agreed is her.

Krystal

Singer and actress. The younger sister.

The one person whose name can move Jessica

Cold face, thin skin, and a habit of saying she is fine

Her career has been quietly downgraded since her sister left the group, and she has never once complained about it out loud, which is the fact that makes her dangerous to anybody who wants something from Jessica.

The soft place

Because Jessica knows her sister exactly.

On the outside Krystal looks cool, as if nothing is worth turning around for.

Inside she feels everything twice, and keeps the second pass.

If that girl found out her sister had been pushed into this trade because of her, she would come apart. And long after everybody else had moved on, she would still be blaming herself.

The line she cannot stop hearing

A backstage dressing room, years ago, a road case, stage shoes still on: “Unnie, I’m fine.”

Said in the voice of somebody deciding not to make a thing anyone else’s problem.

So nothing gets said.

Everything ends with me. The dirty part, the rotten part, the disgusting part, the part I can’t bear to look at. All of it is mine to carry, and I’ll carry it well enough that nobody at this table needs to know it exists.

She put her shoes on the rack, straightened them, and went to sit down next to her sister on the sofa.

From the television, somebody on a talk show was being applauded for a joke, and the sound of it was the ordinary noise of an ordinary afternoon in a flat where nothing has ever happened.

A word on who is being protected here

Notice what the woman does with the truth on the morning after the worst night of her life: she keeps it, and she keeps it from the one person entitled to ask. The text gives her a reason, and the reason is love with a bill attached. Tell her sister what was paid for her, and the girl will spend years trying to pay it back out of guilt. That is not cruelty in the writing. It is the exchange rate.
