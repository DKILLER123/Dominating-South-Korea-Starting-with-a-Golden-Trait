Chapter Thirty-Nine

Irene: I Once Confessed and Was Rejected

Later still · the same private room

The waiters gone; the griddle doing the talking; the consultation finished, the meal begun

The confession, over the meat

The moment the waiters were gone, Taeyeon was the first to take up the tongs.

“Leave it to me.”

Her hands found the preheated griddle with an ease that needed no rehearsal; a slice of meat down, and it took.

The slice met the heat and answered with a light “sizzle” — the fat pressed out by the temperature, the smell rolling through the room within seconds.

Irene sat across from her, watching the practiced turns, a little smile in her eyes.

Taeyeon looked up at her over the heat, a measure of confidence in her tone.

“You can trust my grilling.” She said it while snipping the thicker pieces open with the scissors. “I guarantee it will make your mouth water.”

Irene laughed at her.

She set her cup down and nodded along, obligingly.

“Then I’m very much looking forward to Unnie’s craft.”

“Just you wait.”

She said it with perfect airs, and her hands had no airs at all — every one of their moves precise.

She did not crowd the griddle. The slices went down in measured numbers; only when the edges had curled and the surface caught a clean, pretty brown did she turn them.

The heat held exactly where it should — no overdone edges, no raw resistance left in the meat.

Irene had started the meal still a little formal, but the smoke and the smell took her attention without asking.

Soon, the first slice was done.

Taeyeon lifted it, checked it once more against the light of the heat, and dropped it straight into the small dish in front of Irene.

“First piece. Yours.”

Irene smiled. “Thank you, Unnie.”

She picked up the slice, gave it a light pass through the seasoning, took a leaf of lettuce, and wrapped the meat, a slice of garlic, and a little of the sauce into it.

The moment it reached her tongue, the warm meat and the sauce spread out; the fat melted at exactly the right degree, a touch of char, nothing greasy.

The lettuce’s coolness did the rest, balancing the meat’s weight exactly.

Irene chewed slowly, her eyes curving a little.

The moment she swallowed, her face gave her away — content, and happy, almost immediately.

“Delicious!”

Taeyeon watched her be soothed by a single piece of meat and could not help but laugh.

“If it’s good, eat more.”

As she spoke, she turned the second slice.

“You don’t have to think about the complicated things today. And you don’t have to worry about whether the captain is doing well.”

She laid the grilled meat into the small dish again, her tone light.

“First, fill the stomach. Everything else after.”

Irene looked down at the extra meat in her dish; the smile at the corner of her mouth softened a little more.

“Ne.”

She answered softly, and took up the lettuce again.

First, fill the stomach. Everything else after.

Only when a few more slices had finished did Taeyeon finally set the tongs and the scissors aside.

All this time she had been turning meat, snipping meat, serving Irene’s dish — and had not eaten a thing herself.

It was not until the plate held a small stack of finished meat on it that she sat back, took up her own chopsticks, and began, unhurried.

Irene, seeing this, could not help herself.

“Unnie, eat more, too.”

“I know.”

Taeyeon answered with a laugh, took a slice, dipped it in sauce, wrapped it loosely in lettuce, and ate.

Two bites in, her hands went slow.

When had her gaze come to rest on Irene’s face, she did not know.

Irene had her head down, wrapping a piece with real attention; aware of the look across the table, her hands paused, almost nothing.

She looked up, straight into Taeyeon’s smiling eyes.

“Unnie, what is it?”

Irene, a little puzzled, lifted a hand and touched her own cheek.

“Is there something on my face?”

Taeyeon shook her head.

“No.”

“Then what is—”

Irene was more puzzled than before.

Taeyeon watched her; the smile at her own mouth grew a little more visible.

She sounded half like she was simply marveling, half like some moment had struck her out of nowhere.

“It’s just — how do you keep looking better and better, the longer I look?”

Chin in her hands, she kept looking at her — an open look, nothing teasing in it.

“How is it possible that someone this beautiful even exists?”

Irene’s composure, which had been holding, gave way a little; the base of her ears and her cheeks went warm.

She looked down, as if to escape the look, and pressed her lips together, lightly, almost against her will.

“Unnie—”

“Why are you saying such strange things—”

Seeing her this flustered made Taeyeon laugh even more openly.

“Where is it strange?”

Her tone, perfectly innocent: “I’m just telling the truth, you know.”

Irene did not answer. She put the wrap she had just finished into her mouth, using the eating to hide her own awkwardness.

Taeyeon watched her do it; the smile in her eyes went deeper.

“I think these days, at the company, there’s only Yoonah and Jessica who are—”

The sentence stopped mid-air.

A name, with no warning at all, had touched the one place that was not to be touched.

Irene looked at her.

Jessica.

Jung Soo-yeon.

That name had never been one she could let pass lightly.

Irene knew there were things she did not get to ask.

The storms Girls’ Generation had been through — what the outside world ever knew of them was scraps: rumors, and fragments, magnified.

What the ones caught in the middle had actually carried, what impossible choices they had made — probably only they themselves knew.

Taeyeon exhaled, looking at the meat still gleaming on the griddle — her appetite for it gone.

The choice · as Taeyeon remembers it

The company put the choice in front of them — and had left very little room to step back.

Everything had been pushed to the one point where it could not wait any longer: the group’s future, the members’ positions, the company’s attitude, the schedule and the business arrangements, and all the pressure that no one outside could see. All of it, compressed at once.

She had not not wanted Jung Soo-yeon to stay. How could she not have.

Captain — two characters. They sound like honor until they land on your shoulders, and then they are a responsibility with nowhere to run to.

So the choice had to be made out of her — even a choice that was not easy, even one that would hurt her.

Back then, the two of them had been close. Close enough that the outside world had even coined a name for it — the Taeseo CP.

Trending · The Taeseo CP

Cut the clips. Cut them again.

The harmonies — loop them. When they look at each other, that’s the moment. Save it.

The heat keeps rising, and it never stops rising — someone is always making it rise.

For Taeyeon, none of it had ever been just images on camera, ready to be consumed.

Jung Soo-yeon had been, truly, one of the most important members — an important teammate, an important friend. They had practiced together, debuted together, stood on stages that kept getting bigger, and kept going through the exhaustion and the pressure that never seemed to end.

That was precisely why the choice had cut so deep.

She had not wanted to watch her leave.

And she was the captain of Girls’ Generation. She could not make the decision with her feelings alone — if she let the situation keep slipping, the one hurt in the end might not be a single person. It could be the whole group.

Irene watched Taeyeon go quiet, and something tightened in her own chest.

A moment ago she had still been asking her how a captain is supposed to carry things. Now she understood, far more directly, that the “pressure” in Taeyeon’s mouth had never been a phrase you could brush past.

Some captains did not face only the small frictions between members, or problems that a few words of communication could fix. Sometimes, the choice was between feeling and duty — and it had to be made. Even when you knew, ahead of time, that whatever you chose, someone would get hurt.

The meat on the griddle ticked over softly; the smell was still rich. The air in the room no longer was.

After a few seconds, Taeyeon seemed to notice, at last, that she had gone somewhere.

She looked up, met Irene’s worried eyes, and managed the barest curve of a smile.

“Sorry.”

Her voice, low: “I suddenly remembered some old things.”

Irene shook her head.

“It’s all right, Unnie.”

She did not press. She lifted a finished slice and laid it into the small dish in front of Taeyeon. The motion was light. It was very deliberate.

“Eat a little, first.”

Taeyeon looked at the meat in the dish and froze, for a moment.

“Mm.” She answered in a low voice.

Irene watched her, her voice gentle.

“Didn’t Unnie say it yourself just now? Today, we eat properly first.”

Hearing that, Taeyeon finally let a small smile out.

“Ya — you pick that up fast.”

Irene let a little smile out too.

“Unnie teaches well.”

The griddle had turned over a round; Taeyeon took up the tongs again and flicked the slices whose edges had begun to curl.

Fat struck the metal in small sounds; the smell rose in waves.

Irene had her head down, laying out lettuce, ready for another wrap.

And then, as if something had just come back to her, Taeyeon looked over.

“Oh — right.”

“I have one question I’m terribly curious about.”

Irene’s hands paused a beat.

“Unnie, what question?”

Taeyeon did not answer right away. She moved the finished meat to a small dish first, and only then, unhurried, began:

“Before you debuted—”

“were there a lot of boys asking you out?”

Irene blinked, a little startled — she had not expected the question.

She looked down and laughed, the way you do when asked about something far away.

The memories of those school years, with the debut and the trainee life and the schedules in between, had grown distant — but brought up like this, they did not feel strange.

She thought a moment. “In those school years, yes. Quite a few boys asked me out.”

Taeyeon blinked — not surprised at all. “I knew it.” She propped her chin in her hands, her smile going deeper. “Looking like that — it was impossible for no one to fall for you. If I were a boy, I’d be asking you out too.”

Irene, made uncomfortable by this, went back to tidying the lettuce in her hands.

Taeyeon, clearly now interested, pressed once more.

“So — did you ever accept any of them?”

Irene’s hands stopped.

Watching her, Taeyeon added: “That is — have you ever been with someone?”

Irene shook her head.

“No.”

Taeyeon raised a brow.

“Not a single one?”

“Mm.”

Irene nodded lightly, remembering.

“I was young back then — curious about a lot of things, it’s true. But romance, I don’t think I was much interested in it.”

04The explanation — young, curious about a lot of things; romance, however, never much interesting → then the pause: “Except—”

The question that was never asked out loud, the one the room heard anyway: except what.

She paused — something had surfaced; her eyes shifted.

“Except—”

Taeyeon caught the turn in her voice at once.

“Except what?”

Irene looked down at the lettuce in her hands, her fingertips holding the edge of a leaf; the calm on her face had been turning, bit by bit, into something else.

A little resignation. A little the sense that the memory in question was slightly embarrassing.

At last, she gave a small, bitter laugh.

“It’s just — I once confessed to someone.”

Taeyeon had been about to lift a slice into her own bowl; the motion stopped, suspended in midair.

“Hm?” She looked up — disbelief in her eyes, all of it.

Irene glanced at her. “But I was turned down.”

Taeyeon looked as if she might have misheard.

Slowly, she set her chopsticks down and looked at Irene — her face, rarely, a little out of control.

“What did you say?”

Irene, thrown even more off-balance by the reaction, felt the tips of her ears go red.

Taeyeon kept staring, as if the thing itself was incomprehensible.

“You confessed to someone—”

“and were turned down?”

Irene was quiet for a moment, then nodded, at last.

“Mm.”

I once confessed to someone — and I was turned down.

Taeyeon’s eyes went wide.

Oh my god!

She was genuinely not sure her ears had not just betrayed her.

Irene.

A face like that, a presence like that — of course, in her school days, everyone had been after her.

Even if she had never once, growing up, gone after anyone herself — Taeyeon would have found that perfectly reasonable.

And now Irene was saying that she had — that she had once gone after someone herself.

And worse — she had been turned down.

She looked at her and could not get a word out for a long time.

The meat still hissed on the griddle. Taeyeon had even forgotten to turn it.

A good while later, one sentence came out, slowly.

“That person — is his eyesight not very good?”

Irene, hearing that verdict, went still for a beat — then laughed, unable to stop.

“His eyesight is excellent.”

There was no resentment in it. It sounded like she was remembering something that had been finished a long time ago.

Taeyeon’s face had not moved an inch from incomprehension.

She took up the tongs again and turned the slice that had nearly been forgotten, her brow still knitted.

“His eyesight is good?”

“Then why did he turn you down?”

Irene lowered her eyes and smiled.

Taeyeon, meanwhile, had already started taking the possibilities seriously.

She thought them over, and over, and at last, as if she had found the one explanation that would barely hold, she tried:

“Or is it—”

“he doesn’t actually like women? Is he gay?”

Irene blinked — then laughed, helplessly.

“Unnie, where did that come from — he’s perfectly normal. His orientation, included, is perfectly normal.”

“Then what else could it be?” Taeyeon truly could not find any other possibility.

In her reckoning, a face and a presence like Irene’s being wanted by everyone in school had been a matter of course.

Even doing nothing, just sitting there quietly, someone would have come near, sooner or later.

And it had been Irene, on top of everything, who had done the confessing.

On the confession

A confession that was refused does not become a wound — it becomes a fact. The kind of fact that sits in the corner of the room, where you can put it, and keep eating. What this chapter records is not the name. It is the reaction: the tongs suspended in midair, the oh my god that is half disbelief and half refusal, and the joke about eyesight, which is the only door the captain knows how to walk through.

The room’s economy, for this chapter, is the economy of the small dish: everything of value gets placed in it a little at a time — the first piece, the second piece, the answer to a question no one asked — and nothing is forced. The griddle hisses on, unhurried, as if it owns the whole evening. The fact that was just told is already on the table; it is simply not named. Naming, in this room, waits for the meat.
