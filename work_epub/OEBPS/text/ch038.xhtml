Chapter Thirty-Eight

Irene’s Troubles, Taeyeon’s Guidance

Evening · the SM Entertainment building

The practice rooms and the recording studios, emptying; the elevator, busier than usual; one girl standing still by the lobby side

The captain, waiting for the captain

Later in the day, the SM building had begun to take on the mood of clocking out.

The people who had been busy all day in the practice rooms and the recording studios were starting to move out, one by one. The elevator mouth held more people than usual, and even the first-floor lobby had grown lively — now and then the sound of staff exchanging greetings, and artists talking in lowered voices.

Irene stood by one side of the company lobby, waiting quietly for someone.

She was dressed simply today, her hair falling down along her shoulders; the whole of her looked clean and gentle.

But she had no hurry, the way everyone else did. She stood where she was, glancing now and then toward the elevator — plainly, waiting for a person.

At that moment, Seulgi, having just packed her bag and ready to leave, came over.

Seeing Irene still standing there, she was a little surprised.

“Irene, you’re not leaving yet?”

Irene turned; seeing it was Seulgi, she gave a small smile.

“Go ahead, you all.”

Softly, the explanation: “I’m waiting for Taeyeon Unnie.”

“Ah, that’s why.”

Seulgi’s face closed over the understanding at once, no more questions asked. “Then we won’t wait for you.”

“Mm.”

Irene answered, lightly, the smile on her face still soft, and soft.

“Be careful on the way.”

“Okay, you too.”

Seulgi waved, and went out first with her bag, along with the others.

The lobby came and went; Irene still stood where she was, waiting patiently.

After a good while, the elevator doors opened again.

This time, the ones who came out were the members of Girls’ Generation.

They had apparently just finished a schedule, or a practice; walking out, they talked, casually, as they went.

It was only an ordinary clocking out — and yet on them, the presence that belongs to the top group’s seniors was still very strong.

Seeing them, Irene’s expression turned serious a shade, and she went up to them, quick.

“Hello, senpais.”

The greeting first, polite, respect in the tone.

The members of Girls’ Generation were all her seniors — and the direct, top-ranking great seniors of the company.

Even though she was, usually, the bright-tempered one, at a moment like this she was, unavoidably, a little reserved.

The group turned their eyes at the sound.

Seeing it was Joo-hyun, the softness came back onto their faces.

“Oh — it’s Joo-hyun.”

“Not home yet?”

“Waiting for someone?”

Under the seniors’ eyes, asked one after another, Irene unconsciously straightened her back a little further; the polite smile on her face.

“Yes — I’m waiting for Taeyeon Unnie.”

Hearing the line, the group’s eyes went, by reflex, to Taeyeon.

Taeyeon

Leader of Girls’ Generation; the captain’s captain

SM Entertainment; the direct great-senior whose elevator the girl in the lobby is standing by

Seen here for the first time: the crowd parts around her without her asking — and someone has been waiting, specifically, for her

Speaks in a teasing line and a serious one, alternating; takes a new captain’s trouble the way she takes a menu — without hurry. The one who knows what the word captain weighs, because she has carried it.

The captain’s captain

And Taeyeon, standing in the crowd, was as if she had long known Joo-hyun would be waiting for her here — her expression still calm; only one look, and one small nod.

The lobby · the read the senpais do not file

The answer that is giventhe waiting-question asked; the “just came down a moment ago” answer given — fast, and with a shake of the head

The read that is heldthe posture on display — standing, waiting, patient, in the lobby’s middle — and the answer it was attached to, which it flatly refuses to be

The verdictthe senpais see it, and each understands, to some degree; none of them points it out — each carrying a trace of a smile; and the more they look, the quieter Irene becomes than usual

“Have you been waiting long?”

Irene shook her head, quickly.

“No — I just came down a moment ago, too.”

She said it out loud, but that posture of hers — standing obediently in the middle of the lobby, waiting for a person — no matter how it was looked at, did not look like “just came down a moment ago.”

The seniors nearby watched it, and each of them understood, to some degree. But none of them pointed it out; instead, each of them carried a trace of a smile.

Under their eyes, Irene became more and more at a loss for where to put herself; she could only hold the polite expression and stand there — quieter, all over, than her usual.

After a brief farewell to the other members, Irene left the company with Taeyeon.

The sky had gone dark, gradually. The streets near the company were still lively. Shop signs came on, one after another, along the road; the passersby were many — the nerves a whole day had kept pulled tight loosened, without being asked.

The two of them walked, side by side, toward the commercial street nearby.

Taeyeon wore a hat; Irene had lowered her presence a shade. Neither of them had bothered to wrap up tight — but walking in the crowd, both of them were, on instinct, lower-key than usual.

A little later · the BBQ restaurant, the private room

The door closed; the small plates out; the griddle not yet hot

The consultation, before the meat

They had walked a while when they came, just then, on a BBQ place.

The smell hit them from inside — the fat of the grilled meat mixed with the sauce; the two of them, who had only been a little hungry, were hungry at once, properly.

When the waiter at the front came up, Taeyeon asked, straight, for a private room.

This was not for any pretension of theirs.

It was only that both of them were, now, somewhat famous. Even coming out to eat after work, if they were recognized, it would, nine times out of ten, draw a swarm of fans and passersby to circle. Photos, commemorative shots, greetings — even being uploaded to the internet — all of it the most normal thing in the world.

During working hours, they might still have mustered the energy to handle it.

But it was past work, now.

After a full day, nobody wanted to “perform” anymore.

They just wanted one meal, in peace — not to be surrounded by a crowd taking photos within minutes of sitting down, not to have to manage their expressions even when picking up a piece of meat.

The private room was, without question, the right choice.

When the waiter had taken them in and the door was closed, the noise outside was walled off, a great deal of it.

Only then did Irene seem to let go, properly; one light breath out of her.

Taeyeon looked at her once and smiled. “Hungry, right?”

Irene, holding her iced americano, nodded, honestly.

“A little.”

Taeyeon pushed the menu over to her.

“Then order first. Today, don’t think about anything — first, eat well.”

“Okay.”

Irene looked down at the menu, the corner of her mouth curving up, unasked, a little.

After they had ordered, the waiter took the menu and left the room, quickly.

The private room · before the meat

The griddle not yet hot · the small plates, already out

The small plates — kimchi, pickled radish, sliced garlic, the dipping sauce set in a neat row to the side of the table

In her hands — an iced americano held with both hands, drunk a little; the mind clearly not entirely on the food

The meat has not arrived. The consultation has.

Irene sat across from Taeyeon, the iced americano in both her hands. She drank a little, head down — looking like she was waiting for the food seriously. In fact, her mind was clearly not entirely on the food.

Taeyeon watched her for a while, and suddenly smiled.

“Joo-hyun-ah.”

Irene lifted her head.

“Mm?”

Taeyeon’s elbow rested lightly on the edge of the table. Teasing in the tone — but no pressure in it.

“You went out of your way to invite me out today — it’s not simply to treat me to BBQ, is it?”

Irene hesitated, a beat.

She had still been wavering on how to bring it up; Taeyeon had seen it first.

Seen through, gently and directly, like that — she was, instead, a little embarrassed.

Irene was silent for two seconds, then looked at Taeyeon.

“Unnie.”

“Actually… there’s something on the team I wanted to consult you about.”

Taeyeon’s smile drew in a little, and her face went serious.

She did not rush her; she only watched Irene, quietly, waiting for the rest.

Irene pressed her lips together once.

“I’m also a first-time captain.”

“Sometimes I’m afraid I’ll do it badly — and afraid the members will hold a grudge against me.”

The captainship · as it is held, before the counsel

The outside viewan affirmation — the oldest, the longest-trained, the steadiest; the one given the responsibility to lead

The inside viewthe two characters, captain — only from the place itself do you know they are not light at all

The rolenot just Joo-hyun — also Irene: the look-after-the-members one, the speak-first-to-the-staff one, the never-panic-first one

The demandgentle, steady, reliable; and the direction-giver for the members when they are lost

The fearwords too heavy, wounding; too gentle, and the team not managed; not reaching everyone; the members’ silence read as favoritism, or as not up to the job

The pressure, long accumulated, has passed the point of being sayable to someone the same age — which is why the chair across the table has to be the captain of Girls’ Generation.

To be a captain, from the outside, sounds like an affirmation. Oldest, longest in training, steadiest in character — the one given the responsibility to lead the team.

But only when you stand in that place do you know that the two characters, captain, are not light at all.

Much of the time, she is not just Joo-hyun.

She is also Irene.

The one in the team who looks after the members; the one who speaks first, facing the staff; the one who must not be the first to panic when a problem comes.

She is to be gentle, steady, reliable — the one who gives direction when the members are lost.

But she herself, too, gets lost.

She is afraid her words will fall too heavy and wound a member; afraid that too much gentleness will leave the team unmanaged.

Afraid she will not reach every one of them; afraid the members, saying nothing out loud, will think, inside, that she plays favorites — or that she is not up to the job.

This pressure, accumulated a long time, becomes the kind of trouble that cannot easily be said to someone the same age.

So she had come to ask Taeyeon.

Taeyeon was also a captain — and the captain of Girls’ Generation, so presumably experienced.

Having heard it, Taeyeon nodded, lightly. No surprise on her face; no perfunctoriness either. She understood what Irene was feeling, this moment.

The captain of Girls’ Generation · the first time, as remembered

The speaker first concedes the ground: she was the same as the listener, at the start — a first-time captain, under a weight of pressure that had no name then.

The private doubt, asked in that first season — whether she could really do it — is answered here not with confidence but with company: the same doubt, in the same chair, years ago.

What the seat adds: many members, different personalities, their own thoughts and their own emotions — plus the company’s and the outside’s expectations, present in the room whether invited or not.

The sigh. The rule felt from the seat: more mature than the others, or at least so — no falling apart at will, and no fragility on display.

The listener, taking it in: almost every line lands, in the heart, without missing.

“You were the same as I was at first,”

Taeyeon said, slowly. “The first time I was a captain, the psychological pressure was great.”

“Back then I thought it too — can I really do this?”

“The members, so many; each one with a different personality. Everyone has their own thoughts, their own emotions. And the company, and the outside — all with their various expectations.”

There, she let out one quiet sigh.

“Standing in the captain’s seat, sometimes you feel — you have to be a little more mature than the others. You can’t fall apart at will, and you can’t look too fragile either.”

Irene listened, seriously. Something in her heart moved, a little.

These lines — almost every one of them, without missing, landed in her heart.

She nodded, her tone sincere. “Unnie is far greater than me.”

“After all, Girls’ Generation has so many members — and you’ve been through so much.”

Taeyeon, at the serious face, laughed, once.

“All right — stop praising me.”

She waved her hand, her tone looser. “Praise me any more and I’ll be embarrassed.”

Irene, with it, let a little smile out.

Taeyeon picked up her glass of water, drank a mouthful, set it down — and only then looked at her again.

“But — I can give you some advice.”

Irene sat up straighter at once.

That face of hers, set for serious listening, made Taeyeon laugh, once more, unable to help it.

“Don’t be so tense. It’s not an exam.”

She thought a moment, and her tone went slowly serious.

The guidance · four lessons, as laid down

01The load — a captain does not carry every problem alone. The new captain’s error: everyone looked after, every problem seen first, the team kept from ever being wrong — impossible. A member, not a manager: the responsibility is borne, not converted, wholesale, into a personal burden.

02The grievance — not to be feared. A voiced grievance is trust in the captain, and trust in the team’s room for talk. What is truly troublesome is the silence: everyone bottles it up, the surface stays fine, and the distance grows.

03The fit — no member accepts you the same way as the next. Some need more questions; some resist being pressed. Some want the problem named straight; some need the roundabout, gentle way. The captain’s hardest place: not making everyone the same person — but moving a team of different people forward, together.

04The leaning — learn to rely on the members, sometimes. Always the perfect, reliable one, and the members never learn that you also get tired, also get afraid. Show the real state, at the right moment — the captain at the front blocking the wind, who sometimes turns around and asks, of the team she leads, for a little help.

The fourth one lands — and the one who heard it lowers her eyes, digesting: the older sister, the captain, the one who has always carried a little more and shown a little less unease. And Taeyeon’s words let her know: the winding can loosen — it does not have to stay that tight.

“First — a captain is not one person carrying every problem.”

“A lot of people, new to being captain, carry one mistake: they think they must look after everyone, must see every problem before it happens, must keep the team from ever being wrong.”

“But that is, in fact, impossible.”

“You are one of the members, too. Not the manager, and not a manager sent by the company.”

“You can bear responsibility — but you cannot make all the responsibility your burden.”

Irene nodded, thoughtfully.

“Second — don’t be too afraid of the members holding a grudge.”

“A grievance is not necessarily a bad thing.”

“If the members are willing to voice their displeasure, it shows they still trust you — and trust that this team can be talked to.”

Taeyeon smiled.

“What’s truly troublesome is when everyone says nothing and bottles it up. The surface looks fine — and the distance grows further, and further.”

That line put Irene into silence.

She really was afraid of conflict. Sometimes, even when she sensed a member’s emotion, her first move was to think whether she herself had done something wrong — rather than asking, straight out.

Taeyeon, as if seeing what was in her head, went softer in her tone.

“Your nature is delicate, and you’re good at looking after people — that’s your strength.”

“But a captain, sometimes, needs to be the one to move first.”

“Not a strong order to everyone — but, when a problem shows, open the topic.”

“Even just one line — ‘Lately, is something bothering you’ — is better than pretending not to see.”

Irene nodded, lightly.

Taeyeon went on:

“Third — don’t try to make every member accept you the same way.”

“Everyone’s personality is different, and the way they need to be cared for is different, too.”

“Some people need you to ask a few more times. Some don’t like being pressed. Some want you to point the problem out straight; some need you to go around it, gentle.”

She paused, and added, with a smile:

“The hardest place of a captain is right here — not making everyone the same person, but, when everyone is different, keeping the team moving forward, together.”

Irene listened, very seriously — serious enough that she almost reached for her phone to write it down.

Taeyeon, at the face, went on:

“One more — and this one matters.”

Irene turned to her at once.

“You have to learn to rely on the members, sometimes.”

Irene froze a beat.

“Rely on the members?”

“Mm.”

“Don’t always let only them rely on you.”

“If you always look perfect, always reliable, the members may never know you also get tired, also get afraid.”

She smiled, her eyes soft.

“Letting them see your real state, at the right moment — that is not a bad thing.”

A captain is not always the one standing at the very front blocking the wind — sometimes you can turn around and tell everyone: “I’m a little tired — could you help me.”

Irene’s eyes dropped, as if digesting the line, properly.

The members were young. She was the older sister, and the captain — so she had always thought she should carry a little more, and show a little less unease.

But Taeyeon’s words let her know: perhaps she did not need to keep herself wound so tight, forever.

Taeyeon looked at her, and said, last:

“In short — don’t rush.”

“You don’t become a good captain on day one.”

“You’ll make mistakes, get torn, not know what to do — it’s all normal.”

“But if you truly care about the members, and are willing to communicate — they can feel it.”

Having heard it, the stone that had been hanging in Irene’s heart seemed, at last, to drop a little.

She lifted her face and said, seriously:

“Thank you, Unnie.”

Taeyeon smiled.

“What are you thanking me for.”

At just that moment, the private room’s door was knocked, and the waiter came in with the meat they had ordered, and the side dishes.

The griddle’s temperature was up; the beef went on, and in the first instant gave one light sizzle — and the smell spread, quickly, through the room.

Taeyeon picked up the tongs, turned the meat over, her tone back to easy, again.

“All right — captain-consultation time is paused, for now.”

She looked at Irene once and smiled.

“What matters most right now is eating meat.”

Irene, too, could not help laughing.

“Mm.”

On the captain’s consultation fee

The consultation is priced in meat. The door is knocked at the precise moment the last lesson has been digested, and the verdict on a whole career’s worth of captainship is: what matters most right now is eating meat. That is Taeyeon’s method in its truest form — counsel that can be interrupted, because it was never meant to be carried home. It was meant to be set down, one course at a time, and then eaten around.

The chapter’s last word is the smallest sound in it: Mm. Irene’s first unburdened sound of the evening, delivered over a hot griddle. And the room’s economy deserves the record: a hat, a lowered presence, a private room — two famous women eating one meal without performing. The most expensive thing in that room was the one thing neither of them was doing: managing an expression. The captain who holds a team together learned, in a booth, the sentence the team does not get to hear until the captain decides to say it — I’m a little tired — and the chapter closes with the sentence finally, safely, off the table and onto the meat.
