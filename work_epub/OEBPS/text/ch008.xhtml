Chapter Eight

Prosecutor Bae, Welcome to Seoul Central

First day in office · morning, Seoul still cold

His one-bedroom · in front of the mirror

A tie done straight, and a face with no business being in a building like that

Today was Bae Do-yoon’s first official day of work.

He stood at the mirror and adjusted his tie without hurry.

The uniform of a brand-new geomsa, read in his own mirror

Suit black, pressed, shoulders sitting exactly where a suit should sit

Shirt white, first-day stiff

Tie dark, done straight, checked twice

Hair just washed; the front strands still carrying a little damp

The mirror gives him back a man cold of brow, straight of nose, young and clean in a way the room cannot explain.

Less a man walking into the Prosecutors’ Office on his first morning, more a model stepping out of a pictorial shoot who took a wrong turn.

Plate set from his card portrait — the mirror, first morning, Chapter 8

Bae Do-yoon looked left and right in the glass. Then one brow went up, faintly.

“Tsk.”

He studied himself a few seconds more and could not help the sigh:

“A face like this not debuting — it is a loss for the entire Korean entertainment industry.”

He turned sideways a little and checked his proportions in the glass.

“Even if I don’t debut, modeling would do. Rich women would love me, guaranteed.”

And at rich women, Lee Boo-jin’s silhouette arrived in his head on schedule.

Ugh!

No. No. Scrub that.

He laughed at himself a little. It was only a thought.

He was never going to be an idol or a model. From the day he chose the prosecutor’s road there had been no exit ramp. These years he had walked one step at a time to here, and every step of it looked standard, clean, pretty — nothing to pick at.

The ladder, as a file would list it

012008 — enters Seoul National University, College of Law

022012 — 54th Judicial Examination, final pass list; name at the top, first place

032013 — Judicial Research and Training Institute, 44th class

04February 2015 — appointment: Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office, Criminal Division 3, new prosecutor

Read as a résumé it is the model rookie the Korean judicial system dreams of: clean origin, top marks, no family web, no chaebol background, no faction. Even his private life is too tidy to gossip about.

In 2008, when he entered SNU’s law college, the Korean judicial system stood at the hinge of its reform. The law-school graduate institute system had begun its rollout; the old line of law undergraduate plus Judicial Examination was being gradually replaced. Plenty of people said the entrance to the legal profession would change shape entirely.

But for an orphan with no family name and no resources, the road that could actually change a life remained exactly one.

The Judicial Examination.

A narrow door — so narrow that countless people broke their heads against it and never got in. And yet, getting in meant tearing an opening out of the bottom of society with your bare hands.

Korea was too competitive. Or rather, the whole of Asia was too competitive. From high school to university, from university to examination, from examination to first desk, every stage screened people as if screening them to death.

And the Judicial Examination most of all.

The SNU library, three in the morning

The reading room lights still on. Tables stacked with fat codes, case compilations, notebooks, coffee cans, energy drinks.

Somebody reciting constitutional precedents while slashing highlights through them like a machine gun. Somebody who broke over a second-round essay question, crying in the restroom. Somebody on three hours of sleep for the fourth day running, face grey, eyes red as if they would drip.

And worse: the pills some of them took to stay awake. Legal ones, grey ones, ones with no source anybody discussed.

They ran themselves like machines that must not stop. While the machine still worked, you kept feeding it fuel.

Bae Do-yoon had thought it grotesque, once. Then he discovered he was no better.

He stayed up too. The Judicial Examination is not won by being clever; clever only shortens a few detours. What pushes a man through is repetition close to self-torture — a dozen hours a day, like locking yourself into a cell with no windows.

Not yet a prosecutor, and already serving time.

But he differed from the others in one respect.

His memory was too good.

Most things, seen once, he kept in outline. Seen twice, he could recite their structure. Seen three times, even certain annotations and precedent numbers surfaced in his head on demand.

At first he called it talent. Later he understood it sat outside the border of what talent means.

He did not know whether it was some compensation folded into the rebirth. But to this day he had seen nothing like an entry effect for it: no system notice, no ability description, no ledger line. It sat in him like a second heartbeat — original equipment.

He had tested it, once. A thick criminal case collection, opened at a random page, read, closed.

An ability built for exactly one thing: an examination that compresses knowledge to its density limit.

So he had come out ahead of an army of ten thousand.

Competitive? Can you out-compete me?

In 2012, when the 54th Judicial Examination’s final pass list went up, his name stood at the very top. First place.

That day, half of SNU’s law college talked about him: welfare-home origin, Seoul National University, examination chief, and a face like an entertainer’s. Labels like that, stacked together, are naturally combustible.

Some envied. Some burned. Some called him a genius; some called him lucky. And some said, behind his back, with a certain curl of the mouth, that a man with that background reaching this height must surely have someone behind him.

Bae Do-yoon heard that one in the library stairwell, with a can of cheap coffee in his hand.

He had smiled at it, because it was not entirely wrong.

I do have someone behind me.

And I still climbed here on my own back. If I hadn’t passed, Lee Boo-jin might genuinely have fed me to the Han River fish.

In 2013 he entered the Judicial Research and Training Institute with the 44th class, and it was another slaughter. Everyone inside had already proved himself elite; and elite against elite, the ranking went on, because the destinations after graduation differed too much: judge, prosecutor, big firm, in-house counsel, ministry desk. Every road its own door.

To become a prosecutor your grades could not slip: criminal practice excellent, interviews clean, internship evaluations good, background review cleaner.

His background was clean. He was an orphan — which to an ordinary person is loneliness, and to the prosecution is an advantage.

Through the Institute’s two years his record stayed insultingly pretty. His prosecutorial internship report was circulated as a model.

Mentor’s evaluation, Judicial Research and Training Institute, 44th class

Judgment calm, logic rigorous, suited to prosecutorial practice.

In February 2015, shortly after the 44th class completed, the appointment notice reached him.

Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office

Personnel order · new prosecutor appointment · procedure complete

Bae Do-yoon — Criminal Division 3, new prosecutor

In black and white, with every stamp in its place. Nobody can find a flaw in this transfer: the grades suffice, the record suffices, the evaluations suffice. Everything about it is reasonable.

He was glad, and uneasy in the same breath.

Seoul Central. The storm’s center — the most sensitive, most crowded, closest-to-power front-line organ in the whole Korean prosecution. Chaebol cases, political cases, corruption cases, the big files the media breathes on. Countless interest lines cross inside that building.

He would have to be extremely careful. One wrong step inside, and there is no climbing back.

He looked at himself: suit pressed straight, tie square, brows calm. He did, admittedly, look the part of a new prosecutor.

Only the nonsense running under his skull was not the state of mind a first-day rookie should have.

He frowned, and after a moment shook his head.

Enough. Not now. Today is day one.

What matters most on a first day? Not solving a case and earning merit. Not outwitting chaebols. Certainly not walking in hot-blooded, convinced you can rescue Korean justice single-handed.

The most important thing on day one is exactly one thing.

Don’t make mistakes.

Internal memo · to himself, at the door

Day-one visibility control

Source: one reborn man’s previous life of watching rooms

Conclusion: stay low. Learn the people, the cases, the factions and the rules inside the building first; everything else later. And as for Lee Boo-jin — while the so-called cooperation stays unexposed, the man everyone sees is only a pretty-record rookie out of the Training Institute. Nobody joins those two names in one sentence. While that layer stays under the waterline, he has room to operate; and if the sky falls, the tall ones hold it up first.

He picked up his document bag, changed shoes at the entry, killed the lights, and went out.

Click.

The lock settled behind him.

The building was not luxurious, but its position was good: near a subway station, convenient streets. At this hour the corridor usually offered one or two doors opening, no more.

Today was different.

He had just stepped out when dragging sounds arrived from next door.

“Careful with this side!”

“The sofa doesn’t go in yet — the boxes are in the way.”

“That carton says fragile on it. Don’t stack on it.”

Strange men’s voices, out in the corridor.

Bae Do-yoon paused and glanced over. The unit next to his — empty for as long as he could remember — stood wide open with its lights on. Movers carried things in and out: cartons, garment racks, suitcases, a floor lamp, a small bookcase, furniture wrapped tight in dust sheets.

A doorway that had been cold and quiet for half a year was suddenly crowded.

He remembered the place standing empty for at least six months: the landlord willing to rent, the price not cheap, the floor and layout not especially good, and so nobody coming.

And today, out of nowhere, a new neighbor.

Bae Do-yoon gave it one glance. First day at work; he had no intention of growing side branches. And neighborly socializing had never interested him. In Seoul, most people can share a floor for years without learning the surname next door. Everyone is busy living; nobody has spare attention for a stranger’s life.

He withdrew his eyes and started for the elevator.

At that moment a mover carrying a carton backed out of the next door and nearly walked into him.

“Ah — sorry!” The man twisted aside fast.

Bae Do-yoon stepped back half a pace. “It’s fine.”

The mover looked at him — plainly knocked a little sideways by the suit and the face — and grew notably more polite.

“Just moved in today. There’s a lot of stuff; it might get a bit noisy.”

Bae Do-yoon nodded. “No problem.”

He left.

Just past eight-thirty

Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office · the gate

The building every big case in the country eventually flows into

He stood at the entrance and looked up at the letters on the building’s face.

Seoul Central District Prosecutors’ Office.

One of the most important district prosecutorial organs in Korea: most political cases, chaebol cases and major economic cases end up flowing here.

How the machine is stacked, and what a new geomsa actually does

At the top stands the Supreme Prosecutors’ Office — the highest command organ of the nation’s prosecution. Below it, the regional high prosecutors’ offices. Below those, the district prosecutors’ offices; and among districts, Seoul Central is the one everybody watches.

Bae Do-yoon’s rank in this stack: a new prosecutor in the criminal division of Seoul Central — the most front-line, most junior rung. The title is not high. The power is not the kind that flips tables.

His daily work will be ordinary criminal cases: fraud, injury, theft, drunk driving, traffic accidents, violent clashes. Police finish an investigation and send the file up; the prosecutor reviews the record, judges whether the evidence suffices, whether the suspect’s conduct constitutes a crime, whether supplementary investigation is needed — and then decides to indict, not to indict, or to request an arrest warrant.

It sounds majestic. In practice it is reading, writing opinions, meetings, calls, and chasing paperwork.

A prosecution is a machine for turning paper into consequences; a new prosecutor is the machine’s newest pair of hands.

He went in, passed security, and reported to personnel as the notice instructed. Signature, ID card, department confirmation. The procedure was quick.

The clerk handed the card over with a practiced smile.

“Prosecutor Bae — welcome to Seoul Central.”

Bae Do-yoon took the card. His name and title, printed on it.

He looked down at it, and found less excitement in himself than he had expected. From today he was formally inside the system. To outsiders he is the examination chief, the Training Institute’s excellent graduate, a new prosecutor with a pretty file. In here, he is a newcomer.

He put the card away and walked toward the criminal division’s floor.

The corridor was full of people: phone voices, footsteps, printer noise, all mixed together.

It was his first day in office.

On first days and narrow doors

A man who once squeezed through the narrowest door in the country knows exactly how to behave at somebody else’s threshold: eyes down, voice level, no shine on you. What Bae Do-yoon does not yet know — what the reader is permitted to know a few paragraphs early — is that the door which will cost him the most attention this year is not the Prosecutors’ Office gate. It is the one next to his own, with the movers in it.
