Chapter Thirty

I’ve Always Seen You as a Younger Brother

Eight o’clock at night · the Shilla Hotel

Entrance brightly lit; doormen in uniform, cut from one mold

One summons of two words, and one man who is nobody’s on-call employee

At eight in the evening, Bae Do-yoon appeared at the Shilla Hotel.

The moment he stepped out of the car he saw the hotel entrance blazing with light, uniformed doormen standing by the steps, their movements so standard they might have been carved from the same mold.

Bae Do-yoon lifted his head, glanced at the hotel’s sign, and gave a light sigh.

Lee Boo-jin had a habit.

One “come over” — as if he were an employee in her office, callable at any hour.

He walked into the lobby.

A few steps in, Lee Boo-jin’s assistant appeared not far away.

“Hello, Mr. Bae. Please follow me,” the assistant said, tone light and even.

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her.

“She didn’t say what for?”

The assistant smiled faintly. “President Lee said: you’ll know when you arrive.”

Bae Do-yoon was not surprised.

“She truly never betrays my expectations.”

The assistant, as if long used to this tone of his, kept her polite smile.

“This way, please.”

Bae Do-yoon said no more and followed her toward the elevators.

He was not here for the first time.

Lobby, corridor, the private elevator, and then that upper-floor office quiet to the point of excess — every arrangement along the way was very Lee Boo-jin.

The same night · the upper floor

An office too quiet, lit soft; two knocks, one “enter,” one door closing behind

A woman in black at the window, holding tea, waiting like a meeting arranged long ago

When the elevator doors opened, the assistant led Bae Do-yoon to the familiar office door.

She knocked, lightly, twice. From inside came Lee Boo-jin’s voice.

“Enter.”

The assistant pushed the door open; Bae Do-yoon went in; behind him the door was quickly closed.

The office light was soft. Lee Boo-jin, in black from head to toe, stood by the window with a cup of tea in her hand — looking less like someone who had just finished work than like someone waiting for a meeting arranged long ago.

Hearing footsteps, she turned and smiled faintly.

“Long time no see.”

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her and went straight to sit on the sofa.

“I’d rather not see you. What is the matter you called me for?”

Lee Boo-jin did not take offense; if anything her smile grew a little more distinct.

“Every time we meet, your first sentence is this unpolite. Are you like this at the Prosecutors’ Office too?”

“Not at the Office.” Bae Do-yoon put his coat aside. “Office people generally don’t summon me to a hotel at eight in the evening.”

Lee Boo-jin walked over slowly and sat opposite him.

“And yet you came.”

“Because I know a person like you won’t waste my time for chat.”

Hearing this, Lee Boo-jin seemed in good mood.

She took up the teapot and poured him a cup, unhurried.

Bae Do-yoon watched the motion and lifted a brow.

“The grand eldest princess of Samsung personally pouring tea — should I first confirm whether there are cameras nearby?”

Lee Boo-jin pushed the cup toward him.

“What’s wrong — afraid I’ll clip the tea-pouring footage and send it to the Prosecutors’ Office?”

“Not afraid of that.” Bae Do-yoon lifted the cup and looked at it, but did not drink at once. “It’s just that when you are suddenly this polite, it’s generally not a good thing.”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him; a flicker of smile passed through the bottom of her eyes.

She did not dislike Bae Do-yoon’s way of speaking. Before her he was not exactly polite, but neither was he rude; those thorny replies were more like some default mode of getting along between the two of them. He had accepted the cooperative relationship between them, and knew her resources and her judgment were useful to him. But acceptance was acceptance — Bae Do-yoon never let himself look like one of her people.

This point, Lee Boo-jin appreciated.

Obedient people were too many. Smart, clear-headed people who always knew to leave themselves a boundary — those were rare.

The host’s assembly · black, window, tea

Colour black from head to toe; the office’s soft light allowed no brighter claim

Position at the window, city night behind; turned only when footsteps reached the rug

Hands one porcelain cup held, then one teapot poured for the guest; hospitality administered like a deposition

Manner smile faint, distance intact; the distance thinning a little only when laughed at properly

Assembly unchanged in lineage from every prior meeting in this office: the room is the wardrobe, and the wardrobe is the warning.

Lee Boo-jin looked at him and suddenly said:

“You don’t need so much hostility toward me. I’ve always seen you as a younger brother.”

Bae Do-yoon had just brought the cup to his lips; at these words his motion stopped.

He lifted his eyes to her.

“President Lee — every time you say this kind of thing, I feel you are about to charge a fee next.”

Lee Boo-jin laughed out.

“Am I so un-human in your eyes?”

“Not un-human.” Bae Do-yoon put the cup back. “Just not much like a person who outputs affection for free.”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him, half smiling, half not.

“So you don’t believe it?”

“Whether I believe it isn’t important,” Bae Do-yoon said. “What’s important is: you’d better not really treat me as a younger brother. Your way of raising younger brothers — ordinary people can’t bear it.”

Lee Boo-jin naturally understood the meaning in his words, and did not avoid it.

She lifted her cup, expression composed.

“Aren’t you grown quite well now?”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, silent for two seconds.

“Do you know that sentence of yours is very much like a kidnapper saying to the victim, years later: see, you’re living quite well now?”

Lee Boo-jin was finally made to laugh by him.

When she laughed, that usual distance of hers thinned a little — but only a little.

“This mouth of yours — after entering the Prosecutors’ Office, it’s gotten sharper and sharper.”

“Professional necessity,” Bae Do-yoon said, lightly. “After all, not everyone can, like you, make people shut up with a single look.”

“You didn’t shut up.”

“So I’m sitting here, not standing outside waiting for your summons.”

The smile in Lee Boo-jin’s eyes deepened.

“That sentence sounds like reminding me we are in a cooperative relationship.”

“I thought no reminder was needed.”

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her.

“You just now said you see me as a younger brother.”

Lee Boo-jin could not help laughing again.

The office air, with this back and forth, lightened a great deal. The tea fragrance was very faint; the night view outside the window bright. Two people sat in an expensive, quiet room saying not-exactly-gentle words, and yet there was no taste of drawn blades. Each had long gauged the other’s temper: knew how far each kind of word could go, knew which sentence was a joke and which was a real warning.

Lee Boo-jin set down her cup; her tone slowed.

“Actually — saying I see you as a younger brother isn’t entirely a joke.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted a hand at once.

“Stop.”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him. “I haven’t finished.”

“I know,” Bae Do-yoon said. “But this kind of opening of yours is usually very dangerous.”

“Where is it dangerous?”

“You first lower my guard, then hand me something sufficiently troublesome.”

Lee Boo-jin asked, smiling: “Then — has your guard lowered?”

“No.”

“Then that shows you’ve progressed.”

Bae Do-yoon drank a mouthful of tea, slow and easy. Lee Boo-jin put the teapot back and asked, as if in passing:

“How is it at the Prosecutors’ Office? Still used to it?”

Bae Do-yoon had thought she would go straight to the real business; hearing this sentence, he lifted his eyes to look at her.

“You called me today just to ask this?”

“Can’t I ask?”

“You can.” Bae Do-yoon drank a mouthful of tea. “It’s just a bit unlike you.”

Lee Boo-jin smiled.

“Am I, in your eyes, a person who doesn’t care at all about a cooperation partner’s work state?”

“Not that,” Bae Do-yoon said.

Lee Boo-jin was made by him to give one light laugh.

“Looks like you have quite a lot of opinion about me.”

“Not opinion. Experience.”

“Then take it as only ordinary concern tonight,” Lee Boo-jin said, looking at him. “How is it?”

Bae Do-yoon did not think much; there was nothing to hide.

“So-so.”

He leaned back into the sofa, tone flat.

“Just entered — odd jobs, organizing materials, following sunbaes running procedure, writing things nobody wants to write. The top handed me a case related to an artist. Troublesome, but not a big case.”

Lee Boo-jin’s tea-lifting motion paused, faintly.

She did not take it up at once; she only lifted her eyes to look at Bae Do-yoon.

“You only just entered the Prosecutors’ Office, and they handed you a case?”

Bae Do-yoon nodded.

“Mm.”

Lee Boo-jin was not smiling any more.

She put the cup back on the table; her voice remained calm.

“Where is it strange?”

Bae Do-yoon lowered his head to look at the tea in his cup. The tea’s colour was very pale; on its surface a little of the window light was reflected.

He was silent a moment, then said: “Too early.”

Lee Boo-jin did not interrupt him.

Bae Do-yoon went on: “I only just entered the Prosecutors’ Office system. By reason, even if there truly is a case, they wouldn’t this quickly throw specific clues into my hands. Especially artist-related cases: they look not big, but easily drag in public opinion, agencies, media, fans, capital — neither side is easy.”

Saying this, he lifted his eyes to Lee Boo-jin.

“Handing this kind of case to a newcomer isn’t like cultivation. It’s more like probing.”

Hearing this, a little satisfaction entered Lee Boo-jin’s eyes.

“You thought it through yourself?”

“Thought about it,” Bae Do-yoon did not deny. “Actually, before this I had also considered the question. I think some people above are making small moves — but specifically, I still can’t see clearly.”

He said it very calmly. No excitement of a newcomer being heavily used; no displeasure of a person being utilized; only taking the matter as an unreasonable phenomenon and then taking it apart to look.

This was also where Lee Boo-jin found him interesting.

Many young people, just entered into the system, are most easily flushed in the head by the two characters opportunity. A case thrown into the hand and they think themselves valued. But Bae Do-yoon’s first reaction was not joy — it was suspicion of why it was his turn.

Lee Boo-jin looked at him and slowly said:

“Recently, there will be a relatively large personnel change on the Prosecutors’ Office side.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his head and looked at her once.

This sentence, from Lee Boo-jin’s mouth, carried a different weight.

Of personnel changes inside the Prosecutors’ Office he had not heard zero wind; but he had only just entered, and much of the news still stood behind a layer of fog, not truly visible. Lee Boo-jin was different. She said will be — which meant that in certain circles this matter was already not rumor but a near-confirmed result.

Bae Do-yoon frowned.

“But what does this have to do with me?”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him and did not speak.

Bae Do-yoon spread a hand, his tone carrying a bit of self-mockery.

“I only just entered the system — a shrimp-soldier, a crab-general. How those positions above move: even by turns it wouldn’t reach me to be affected, right?”

Lee Boo-jin laughed.

“Your positioning of yourself is quite humble.”

“Not humble. Fact.”

“Fact?” Lee Boo-jin repeated it lightly. “Bae Do-yoon — do you really think that in a personnel change, only those sitting in high positions are affected?”

Bae Do-yoon withheld his answer for a moment.

Lee Boo-jin took up her cup and drank a mouthful, unhurried.

“When the people in high positions change, the people below are the first to be rearranged.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

Lee Boo-jin went on: “Who is transferred to which department, who follows which sunbae, who suddenly gets the opportunity to touch cases, who is arranged to handle work that seems troublesome but carries no real power — none of these are random.”

Bae Do-yoon understood her meaning.

“You’re saying the case in my hand isn’t just a case?”

“I didn’t say that.”

“But you mean that.”

Lee Boo-jin smiled faintly.

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her.

“Chatting with you — if you don’t catch the words, you easily get led into a ditch.”

Lee Boo-jin did not deny it.

“Then you think — why you?”

Bae Do-yoon leaned in the sofa, fingers lightly tapping the cup’s edge.

“Maybe because I’m new: background clean, no side taken, and if something goes wrong, easy to cut apart.”

Lee Boo-jin nodded.

“And?”

“Also maybe because my record still looks good. If something is really found, above can say the newcomer has drive; if nothing is found, they can say insufficient experience.”

“Continue.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his eyes to her.

“Are you here to examine me today?”

“No,” Lee Boo-jin said, tone calm. “Only to see whether you’ve been shouted ‘Prosecutor Bae’ by those Prosecutors’ Office people until your head is dizzy.”

Bae Do-yoon laughed a little.

“Then you can rest assured.”

The smile in Lee Boo-jin’s eyes shallowed a little, but did not wholly disappear.

“That is good.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her.

“You called me today — surely not really only to confirm whether I’ve adapted to the workplace?”

“About that.”

“About?”

“I wanted to know whether inside you’ve been arranged into some strange position,” Lee Boo-jin said. “Now it seems — indeed a bit.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent a few seconds.

He knew Lee Boo-jin would not ask these things for no reason. Her concern was never simply concern. But strangely, he did not dislike it. The relationship between him and Lee Boo-jin had long ceased to be simple grace-or-grudge or dependence. They had cooperated several times and probed each other several times. Bae Do-yoon was clear she would use him; Lee Boo-jin was clear Bae Do-yoon would in turn use her information. So she asked, and he answered. Nothing to put on airs about.

Bae Do-yoon asked, low: “What wind have you heard?”

Lee Boo-jin did not answer directly.

She only said: “On the Prosecutors’ Office side, some people will go up, some will come down. The more it is such a time, the more someone is needed to do things — to prove themselves still useful, or to prove others unsuited to sit in certain positions.”

Bae Do-yoon understood.

“So this case may be a knife handed out by someone?”

“Or bait.”

Bae Do-yoon frowned.

“For whom?”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him, tone very light.

“That is the place you need to see clearly.”

Source reliability: when Lee Boo-jin says will be, rumor has already become schedule. The addressee of the knife-or-bait is the one place the listener must see clearly himself.

Bae Do-yoon did not speak. The office was quiet for a few seconds.

Then he suddenly laughed a little.

“A person like you is really suited to being a teacher.”

Lee Boo-jin lifted a brow, faintly.

“Why?”

“Because you never directly lecture the answer,” Bae Do-yoon said. “You only take responsibility for throwing the problem in front of a person, then watching whether they can answer it.”

Lee Boo-jin smiled.

“Then — how is your answer?”

“At present it can only count as just seeing the question stem.”

“Not bad,” she said. “At least you didn’t take the problem for a reward.”

President Lee’s class · one lesson, no handout

Rule one — do not rush forward just entered; the Prosecutors’ Office is not a school and not an examination hall.

Rule two — good grades, quick reactions, clean work: useful, but not enough.

Rule three — know why others make you do a thing; especially when the thing feels very reasonable.

Worked example: a newcomer taking a case — cultivation. A young man handling an artist case — familiarizing with the public-opinion environment. A small case — controllable risk. Each reason, alone, unproblematic.

The turn: all the reasons gathered together are not necessarily only coincidence.

Homework returned: just seeing the question stem. Grade: not bad; at least the problem was not taken for a reward. Further questions declined by the examiner, on the ground that the student dislikes others judging for him.

Bae Do-yoon set down his cup.

“Then what else is needed?”

“Knowing why others make you do a thing,” Lee Boo-jin said. “Especially when you feel this thing is very reasonable.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her quietly.

Lee Boo-jin went on: “The more reasonable an arrangement is, the easier it makes a person relax their guard. A newcomer receiving a case can be called cultivation; a young man handling an artist case can be called familiarizing with the public-opinion environment; a case not big can be called risk controllable. Every reason, looked at alone, is unproblematic.”

She paused.

“But all the reasons gathered together are not necessarily only coincidence.”

Bae Do-yoon lowered his eyes, as if in thought.

Lee Boo-jin did not say too much more. She knew Bae Do-yoon was not a person who failed to understand. Some words, pointed to here, were enough; said too plainly, they would instead be like making the judgment for him. And what Bae Do-yoon disliked most was other people making judgments for him.

After a while Bae Do-yoon said: “So you called me tonight to remind me not to too much believe the case given from above.”

“Also not that.”

“Then what is it?”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him and said, lightly:

“To remind you: don’t too much believe the position they give you.”

Bae Do-yoon lifted his head.

This sentence was lighter than the ones before, and yet heavier.

Lee Boo-jin’s tone was steady: “Cases change, and people change. You now think yourself only a newcomer; but in others’ eyes you may already have been placed in a certain position.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent a moment, then suddenly asked: “Then in your eyes?”

Lee Boo-jin looked at him.

“What?”

“What position am I in your eyes?”

Lee Boo-jin did not answer at once.

She looked at Bae Do-yoon, a very faint smile in her eyes.

“You want to hear the true words?”

“Since I asked, that shows I have psychological preparation.”

“Now it is still hard to say,” Lee Boo-jin said. “But at least not shrimp-soldier, crab-general.”

Bae Do-yoon gave a light tsk.

“You people — when praising, you all like to leave half.”

Lee Boo-jin laughed.

“Leave half — only then is there a reason for the next meeting.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her and laughed, helpless.

“I knew this tea wasn’t drunk for free.”

Lee Boo-jin pushed the teapot over.

“Then drink a bit more.”

Bae Do-yoon lowered his head to look at the cup, and in the end refilled a little for himself. His mouth said disdain; his motion was not exactly resistant.

Lee Boo-jin saw it; her smile grew fainter, and truer by a little.

What they are to each other · a ledger of the in-between

Not friends, not family, not superior and subordinate. Two people standing in different worlds, each knowing the other not wholly trustworthy, and each acknowledging the other indeed useful.

Cooperated several times; probed each other several times. She will use him; he will in turn use her information. So she asks, and he answers; nothing to put on airs about.

Her reminder tonight is not pure goodwill; his listening is not complete trust. And yet both are clear: in this city of Seoul, to be able to say a reminder to this degree is itself already a kind of sincerity.

The tea was not free. The reminder, by some exchange rate neither will name, was.

The time was about right. What should be asked had been asked; what should be heard had been heard.

Lee Boo-jin had called him tonight seemingly only for a few casual words of concern, yet every turn of the talk circled the recent wind direction at the Prosecutors’ Office. He knew she would not mention these for no reason. But since tonight she threw no new trouble at him, he was also too lazy to keep sitting here playing tai-chi with her.

Bae Do-yoon set down his cup and stood up.

“All right — time isn’t early. I’ll head back first.”

Lee Boo-jin sat on the sofa and did not speak at once.

She only lifted her eyes to look at him, a faint, if-there-if-not smile in them.

And just as Bae Do-yoon took up his coat and was about to walk to the door, she opened her mouth, unhurried:

“In such a hurry to go?”

Bae Do-yoon’s step paused.

He turned his head to glance at her, his face somewhat strange.

“What — you mean to keep me here overnight?”

The sentence was originally only a casual counter-thrust. After all, he knew Lee Boo-jin’s style by now: every time she spoke she liked to leave half a sentence, and her doing of things always seemed to have the next three steps arranged in advance. Bae Do-yoon’s intent was only to plug her with this line, and incidentally remind her that he was not a person she could call at will and keep at will.

But Lee Boo-jin, hearing it, only looked at him, lightly.

And then she nodded.

“Mm. That’s right.”

…

Bae Do-yoon froze.

He stood in place, his expression blank for an instant. Several seconds later, as if confirming he had not misheard, he opened his eyes wide.

???

What did this mean? Keep him here overnight? At the Shilla Hotel? And said by Lee Boo-jin’s own mouth?

A pile of messy thoughts flashed through Bae Do-yoon’s brain in an instant. He looked at Lee Boo-jin sitting on the sofa, his gaze turning slowly from shock to wariness, and finally taking on a bit of sheer incredulity.

Could it be that she was finally going to make her move on him?

Beast!

Lee Boo-jin plainly saw through those not-quite-proper thoughts of his.

The corner of her eye twitched faintly, and then, without the least ceremony, she rolled her eyes at him.

“Little one — don’t think wildly.”

Bae Do-yoon came back to himself, gave a light cough, and tried to restore his expression to normal.

“I didn’t think anything.”

“It’s already written on your face.”

“Then perhaps you misunderstood.”

Lee Boo-jin was too lazy to keep dragging this out with him.

She lifted her cup, her tone returning to that unhurried composure.

“You may go out. My assistant will take you to the room.”

Bae Do-yoon looked at her, his brow faintly drawn.

He certainly did not think Lee Boo-jin was really bored enough to purely arrange a hotel room for him. Tonight’s conversation, up to here, looked relaxed, but in truth every sentence had stepped on a point: she asked whether he was adapting at the Prosecutors’ Office, asked about the case in his hand, and mentioned the coming large personnel change. And now she suddenly kept him. However he thought about it, it could not be only because she good-heartedly felt his ride home at night too tiring.

Bae Do-yoon looked at Lee Boo-jin for a few seconds.

Lee Boo-jin, however, had no intention of explaining.

She only sat there, expression calm, as if the later matters had long been arranged properly and she waited only for him to walk over himself.

Bae Do-yoon in the end did not ask again. He knew: if Lee Boo-jin did not want to say now, asking was useless.

So he took up his coat and walked out of the office.

Outside the door the assistant was already waiting. Seeing him come out, she lowered her head slightly.

“Mr. Bae — please follow me.”

The assistant said no more, only turned and led the way in front.

Soon the two stopped before a door. She took out a room card and swiped the door open for Bae Do-yoon. The lock gave a slight sound. The assistant pushed the door open and stepped aside:

“Mr. Bae — this is tonight’s lodging the President arranged for you.”

Bae Do-yoon stood at the doorway and did not immediately enter.

He looked at the assistant. The assistant’s face still carried no extra expression: polite, calm, as if whatever was in the room had nothing to do with her.

Bae Do-yoon was silent two seconds, then suddenly asked: “There isn’t anything strange inside, is there?”

The assistant lowered her head slightly.

“You’ll know when you enter.”

Hearing this sentence, Bae Do-yoon could not help laughing a little.

“The people by your President’s side — even their way of speaking grows more and more like hers.”

The assistant did not take it up. Bae Do-yoon had not expected her to answer, either.

He shook his head and finally stepped in.

The same night · the arranged room

One floor lamp lit; warm light on carpet and sofa edge; door closing soft behind

A sweep of the eyes: tea table, floor window, half-drawn curtains — and one slender figure with its back to him

The room was very spacious.

The lights were not fully opened; only the floor lamp on the living-room side was lit, warm light falling on the carpet and the sofa’s edge, making the whole space seem quiet and soft.

The door closed lightly behind him.

Bae Do-yoon stopped at the doorway.

Almost at the instant the door closed, that scattered, casual look on his face faded away.

He did not walk further in.

He stood in place, his gaze sweeping the room slowly.

The tea table.

The floor-to-ceiling window.

The half-drawn curtains.

And on the distant sofa, that slender figure with its back to him.

The younger-brother clause · declared, priced, and declined

I. The declaring party states: I’ve always seen you as a younger brother. Fee presumed payable upon declaration, per the counterparty’s standing experience.

II. The counterparty stipulates: you’d better not really treat me as a younger brother; your method of raising younger brothers is beyond ordinary bearing.

III. Evidence of past cultivation admitted: aren’t you grown quite well now? — compared by the counterparty to a kidnapper’s follow-up visit; comparison not struck from the record.

IV. Praise to be delivered in halves only; the withheld half constitutes the consideration for the next meeting.

V. The tea is not free. Refill obligation accepted by the counterparty in motion though disputed in word.

Don’t too much believe the position they give you.

On half-left praise and rooms with figures in them

A chaebol heiress summons a prosecutor at eight o’clock, pours his tea herself, and tells him she has always seen him as a younger brother — a sentence he correctly prices before it finishes leaving her mouth. What follows is not affection but education: in a personnel change the high seats move and the low seats are rearranged; a case handed to a newcomer too early is a knife or a bait with the addressee left blank; and the most reasonable arrangements are the ones to hold at arm’s length, because reasonableness is how guards are lowered. He answers her examination well — just seeing the question stem — and she pays him in the only currency she issues: half a compliment, kept back to manufacture the next meeting. And then, at the door, the woman who leaves half of every sentence unsaid says the one thing he assumed was a joke and means it entirely: stay the night. A room is assigned, a card swiped, an assistant trained to the house style of you’ll know when you enter. He knows better than to trust the case, better than to trust the position; tonight he learns the harder discipline of the school: not trusting even the quiet of a corridor, because in Seoul a reminder spoken plainly is sincerity, and a room given silently is a sentence whose second half is sitting on the sofa with its back to the door.
