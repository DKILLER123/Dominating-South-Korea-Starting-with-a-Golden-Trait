Chapter Three

I Only Wanted to Date Girl-Group Idols

The same morning, still

The Shilla · behind the door with no plaque

One conversation, and the terms of a life

He had to admit it: Lee Boo-jin was a clever woman.

If he had not been grabbed on his way to SM Entertainment, if he had not been forced onto the law route — the young man who had stood in front of that dormitory mirror had thought exactly the thought she had just described.

Use the face. Use what he knew about the Korean entertainment industry. Find a way to debut. Get close to the girls other people only ever saw through a screen.

It was vulgar. It was also honest.

Hearing it described, out loud, in her tone of voice, felt like being stripped and then lectured on his posture.

She watched him and did not change the temperature of her voice.

“Bae Do-yoon. I did not ruin your life.”

“I pulled you off a road that was running inefficiently.”

He looked up at her. “By kidnapping me?”

She did not deny it.

“Sometimes efficiency matters more than manners.”

He was quiet for a few seconds. Then he said, quite pleasantly:

“You are really unlikeable.”

She smiled at that.

“Being unlikeable is fine.”

“Be afraid of me. Hate me. Want to overtake me. Even want to turn around and bite me later — all of that is fine.”

She leaned forward a little, and her eyes landed on his face and stayed there.

“But before any of it, you have to survive. Then you have to become strong.”

Bae Do-yoon said nothing.

Because the sentence was ugly and it was also true.

Every bit of his anger, every bit of his humiliation, every idea he had ever had about resisting — none of it carried weight yet.

A weak man’s anger is noise. A strong man’s anger is a verdict.

And he was nowhere near strong.

She settled back against her chair. “Anything else?”

He looked at her, and said it slowly.

“The woman in my bed last night — she was part of your plan too?”

Something in Lee Boo-jin’s eyes moved — so slightly that most men in this room would have missed it.

“Yes.”

His voice went down a degree.

“Did she know what she was walking into?”

“She knew what she needed.”

His brow closed.

The sentence sat badly, because it did not sound like an answer. It sounded like a chaebol’s way of not answering: two words that mean something else entirely in the mouth that says them.

“There’s no hurry to pity her,” she said. “In this industry every person has a price, and every person has something they cannot mention.”

“She is not entirely innocent. She is not entirely willing either.”

Bae Do-yoon went quiet.

He knew, in outline, what had happened to the former member of Girls’ Generation named Jessica.

Jessica

Singer. Label owner. The woman who was sent.

Formerly Girls’ Generation

Named here for the first time, in his head, across a desk from power

She is not innocent in this story and she is not willing in it either, which is the position the author has put her in on purpose. Her side of the night gets its own chapter, and its own price.

The gift

And that answer, neither innocent nor voluntary, was more irritating than either clean one would have been.

Because that is what real life is like.

It does not arrive in black and white.

It arrives as one mess, stirred together out of contracts, debts, ambition, fear, and trades, and the person holding the spoon is standing in an office like this one.

“You were not gentle with her last night.”

Bae Do-yoon’s eyes went cold all at once.

She did not stop.

“But today you regret it.”

“That is good. It means you haven’t fully gone bad.”

“You grade that too?”

“Of course.”

“I told you I’m raising you. I need to know whether you’re going to become a blade, or a mad dog.”

He looked at her for a while. Then:

“So. What’s the verdict?”

She looked back at him.

Said in the same voice one uses to sign off on a piece of equipment — and he notices the difference between being inspected and being believed in. Neither of them is warm.

A cold thread went through him.

Not because she was threatening him.

Because she really had been watching.

For years. Watching him grow, watching him choose, watching him struggle, watching the humiliation — and watching, after last night, to see whether he would be the sort of man who regrets it.

Being looked at like that, all of him, and then taken apart and graded: that is worse than any threat.

He said it under his breath, and he did not look away while he said it.

“One day I’ll make you pay for this arrogance.”

She was not angry. On the contrary, she smiled.

“Then work harder.”

She looked at him, and her tone was almost encouragement.

“I’m looking forward to it.”

He looked at her, and she looked at him.

She had heard the threat clearly. She simply did not care yet — because in her reading he was too young, too weak, and too easy to hold.

The edge he was showing now was a blade blank with a little cold light coming off it.

She also knew a blank gets sharpened.

Who it ends up cutting is not something she alone will decide.

The Judicial Monster line in the back of his mind had caught the pause in her eyes a minute ago, and he filed that away with everything else: a woman like this does not flinch at nothing. She flinched at “I’m not the first.”

“Anything else?”

He did not answer right away.

The office was quiet, and the city beyond the glass kept doing what cities do at that hour.

He knew how many questions he was going to be allowed today.

She answered because she wanted to answer. Anything she did not want to say, he could ask a hundred times and get nothing real.

Which meant the last question had to hit the root.

He looked up.

“One more. Why spend this much resource and this much time on me?”

“And why, specifically, does it have to be a prosecutor?”

When the question landed, there was no surprise on her face at all.

She had known he would ask it. From the second he walked in, she had been waiting for it.

She did not answer immediately.

She stood, came around the desk, and walked to the window.

The early light fell on her and made the side of her face very pale, so that there was nothing to read on it.

She looked out at Seoul, and spoke slowly.

“I told you I’m making a blade.”

Bae Do-yoon’s brow moved. “Then I imagine the Samsung group is not short of blades.”

How could a behemoth be short of people?

Any one of these would be cheaper and more obedient than he is.

He is a rookie with one week in the system. Yes, Seoul National University. Yes, he passed the bar exam. Yes, there is a future. And the thing a chaebol has never lacked is a future it can buy.

She smiled faintly, without turning around, and the glass took her face away from him.

“You think the blade I’m making is for Samsung?”

He said nothing.

“If that’s what you think, you’re wrong in a very large way.”

His eyes moved.

“What I am raising is not a blade of the Samsung group.”

“It is a blade belonging to me. Lee Boo-jin. Alone.”

The office went quiet for one second.

And Bae Do-yoon understood what the sentence actually cost to say.

Samsung is Samsung. Lee Boo-jin is Lee Boo-jin.

To the outside she is the chaebol’s eldest daughter, the head of the Shilla, one of the two or three women at the very top of the whole system.

The closer a person stands to the top of that structure, the less free they are.

She has power — that is not her problem. Her problem is that the power does not all belong to her.

Which is why she needs a blade that belongs to no one else: not the group, not the family, not a faction.

Only her.

Bae Do-yoon looked at her back and said: “That sounds like I’m not the first one.”

For the first time that morning, something crossed the bottom of her eyes.

Faint. Gone in a moment.

He caught it.

“You aren’t.”

“What happened to that one?”

She came back to the desk and let her fingertips travel along its surface as she walked, the way a person touches a railing they have climbed thousands of times.

“I have done something like this before.”

“I funded him. I raised him. I paved the road and put him where I needed him to be.”

She paused.

“Unfortunately — that time, I read the man wrong.”

“He betrayed you?”

“Yes.”

She did not say who he was. She did not say what he had done.

From the tone alone he could hear that it was not a small thing.

A woman like Lee Boo-jin does not admit a misjudgment in a sentence this easy. The fact that she said it out loud means the trouble that man caused was much worse than she is showing.

And that told him what he actually was.

I’m not the new blade. I’m the second bet, placed after the first one went bad.

What “being cultivated” contains, in her usage

01Funded, shaped, protected.

02Compared, constantly, against a man he has never met.

03Tested — including by a woman placed in his bed.

04Guarded against. He is a blade and a risk in the same sentence.

Nobody in this room has to say the fourth line aloud. He does not have to say it to himself, either, and it still costs him something to hear it.

“He betrayed me, and he cost me.”

“But I refuse to accept it.” She lifted her eyes to him. “So I went looking for somebody new. In the end I chose you.”

“Should I feel honored?”

“You’re entitled not to feel honored. But you should understand: it means opportunity.”

He did not speak, and she stopped circling.

For the first time that morning her voice went direct.

“We’ve put our cards on the table, so I’ll say it plainly.”

“I want you to climb. One step at a time.”

“Start at a district prosecutors’ office.”

“Then the special cases division.”

“Then the Central District Prosecutors’ Office.”

“Then up.”

“Climb until you are high enough.”

She let the sentence hang, and the weight in it did not come from her volume.

“High enough to solve some problems for me.”

“What problems?”

“You don’t need to know yet.”

He laughed. “That answer again.”

She ignored the sarcasm the way she ignored weather.

“Knowing too much, at your level, is a liability.”

“When you reach the level, I’ll tell you.”

He leaned back in the chair, cold-faced.

“So at bottom I’m still a tool.”

“If you insist on that reading, fine. I would rather call us a partnership.”

“Partnership?”

He will meet less resistance on the way up — and if he is clever enough, he may use what she hands him to build people of his own, and a network of his own, and stop being a man who is placed and start being a man who places.

Clause 4 · What he provides

Climb. Be useful. Be hers when it counts.

Power. Money. Women. Name it and it is supplied.

The four items in Clause 3 are offered out loud, in a room, without a lawyer in it. That is not naivety. It is the arithmetic of a woman who believes he has nowhere else to be.

He said nothing for a while.

She had been clear enough to be rude about it.

No concealment of the control. No charity costume. No words like duty, justice, or the nation wrapped around the thing to make it presentable.

What sat on the desk was the naked trade itself: I give you resources, you climb for me, and when you are strong enough you swing the blade for me.

And with that, several of the questions he had carried for years finally stopped being questions.

Why him? Because he is an orphan. Because his record is clean. Because he has no family to answer to. Because he is clever, and young, and easy to look at, and — by her own accounting — unusual.

Why a prosecutor? Because that is the road that reaches the place where power is actually run.

The Korean prosecution system is itself a blade.

What Lee Boo-jin intends is to hide an edge of her own inside the edge of a state instrument, where nobody thinks to look for private ownership.

Bae Do-yoon was under no illusion. This is not a partnership — not today.

What a partnership actually requires

Two people of roughly equal weight, or the word is a courtesy.

Between them: she sits on chaebol resources and he is one week old at a desk. She holds his past, his present, and the future stored on a camcorder in her drawer. What he holds is a face, a head, and an ambition he is not yet in a position to show anybody.

But a great many partnerships begin as unequal things. As long as he survives, as long as he climbs, as long as he takes enough out of her hands to stand on — a humiliating trade signed this morning can become a real contract, and one day, the reverse.

Bae Do-yoon stood up.

She watched him and did not hurry him. She was waiting for an answer.

He came around the front of the desk until the distance between them was short.

One: the eldest daughter of a chaebol house.

One: a rookie prosecutor with a rented apartment.

The gap between them is not a metaphor and nobody in the room was under any illusion about it.

But when he stood in front of her, his eyes did not move off hers.

He put out his hand and smiled. Actually smiled, for the first time in this building.

“Then here’s to the partnership, President Lee.”

She looked at the hand.

A couple of seconds later she smiled too.

She reached out and let it rest lightly in his grip.

“To the partnership, Prosecutor Bae.”

From this moment the shape of it really did change.

Before, he had been a man on a string and had not been able to see the hand.

Now he could see the hand.

Before, he had not known where the board was.

Now he had been permitted to sit down beside it.

Whether I go on being a piece or start being the one who turns the board over comes down to one variable: how fast I can climb.

He took his hand back. “If there’s nothing else, I’m going.”

She nodded. “Go.”

Then she picked up a private card from the desk and held it out to him.

The card he was handed

Plain stock. No ornament, no titles, no list of offices that would have filled half a page.

One name. One number.

He took it and looked once.

Then he took out his own phone, and they exchanged the numbers — the ordinary, mechanical little business of two people typing for each other.

It was such an ordinary gesture, and it was the most bitterly comic thing that had happened to him all day.

For years this woman has been steering my life from wherever she sits, and it is only this morning that I have been given a way to call her directly.

He put the card away and turned toward the door.

When he was almost there he stopped.

“Another question?”

He turned back, with the look of a man who has just remembered something mildly important.

“Oh — one thing, President Lee. I think you’ve got the wrong idea about something.”

Confusion surfaced, very briefly, on her face.

“What?”

He looked at her, and his face was completely serious.

“When I decided I wanted to be an idol, none of it was about climbing.”

She stared at him, and Bae Do-yoon smiled.

“I only ever wanted to date a few girl-group idols. That’s the whole thing.”

He opened the door, went out, and it closed behind him with barely a sound.

Lee Boo-jin stood where she was for a moment.

She had clearly not expected that answer.

From everything she had watched of him, she knew this young man was no saint: desire, ambition, resentment, and a pride that would take a hammer to dent.

What she had not expected was that he would say something so frivolous, so close to absurd, in a voice that steady and that unashamed.

A moment later she laughed.

“What an amusing little thing.”

She went back to the window and looked out at a Seoul getting brighter.

This country is small.

Small enough that power, money, the courts, the broadcasters, the entertainment industry and the chaebol houses are all packed into one net, sitting on top of each other.

Some people are born in the middle of the net.

Some people spend a whole lifetime caught in it.

And Bae Do-yoon —

There was something in her eyes that had not been there at seven o’clock. If it had to have a name, the name would be expectation.

“I’d like to see how far you get. Don’t disappoint me.”

Why the last line is a joke and not a character flaw

Readers sometimes ask why the hero keeps insisting he only wanted to date idols, as though the author is afraid of admitting ambition. It is the opposite. A man who says the embarrassing thing out loud, to a woman who owns his country, and walks out smiling — that is a protagonist announcing what he will not let anybody edit out of him. The rest is leverage.
