Chapter Two

Thoroughly Controlled by a Rich Woman

The same morning · before he goes out

The Shilla · the suite, then the sealed corridors behind it

One apology, one camcorder, and a door with no name on it

He had his hand on the door when he stopped.

Bae Do-yoon turned back and looked at the woman on the bed.

She was still facing away from him. Brown hair lay in a tangle at the side of the pillow, half a shoulder outside the duvet, and the pale skin there still carried what he had left on it.

He looked for a few seconds in silence. Then his brow slowly closed.

He did not think of himself as a bad person.

Not in the last life, at any rate.

He had never claimed to be a model of virtue and he had no saint’s ambitions; but the floor an ordinary man is supposed to keep — he had always believed he kept it.

Last night he had not kept it.

He had not held himself back at all.

A woman like that does not appear in a man’s room without knowing in advance what she is walking into.

Contract pressure, maybe. A deal she had struck with the mystery person. Or possibly she had wanted something out of it herself.

So the Bae Do-yoon of last night had not been polite about it.

He had treated her as the chip the mystery person pushed across the table, and as a piece of provocation with a pulse.

The terms he wrote at three in the morning

Clause I · Provocation

“You want to control me?”

Clause II · Acceptance

“You want to arrange my life?” Fine.

Clause III · Settlement

Then everything you send, I take. All of it, on the spot, without gentleness.

Signed by one party only. Counterparty not present.

In the morning he would read this back and not recognize the man who drafted it. That is what revenge feels like when you aim it at the wrong chair.

Carrying that temper, he had been nowhere near gentle.

But now the morning light was lying across the room, and the things alcohol and fury and humiliation had held down were floating back up, one at a time.

He regretted it.

The person I have been angry at this whole time is not the woman in this bed.

She might be bait. She might be an accessory. She might be a party to the bargain.

The more likely answer is duller: she is a piece somebody set on the board, exactly like him.

Bae Do-yoon hates being steered.

And last night he had taken the helplessness of being steered and spent it, deliberately, on another person who may not have had a choice either.

That sat badly in him.

He went back to the bedside and bent down for his jacket off the floor. Halfway to it, he stopped, and pulled the duvet up where it had slipped down to her waist.

The woman seemed to sense something. Her brow moved a little. She did not wake.

He looked at the pale side of her face and said, quietly:

“I’m sorry.”

Her shoulder shook, once.

The second the words were out he thought them useless.

It had already happened. An apology does not go back into the night and edit it.

He straightened up, and the complicated look in his eyes was pressed back down.

He is not a saint. He had no intention of dressing like one.

But from today I stop mixing up the person who owes for this with the person who was pushed out to pay for it. Whether she is innocent is still an open question. Whoever put her in my room is not.

Bae Do-yoon picked up the camcorder and turned for the door.

The door opened onto a woman standing in the corridor.

Twenty-seven, twenty-eight. Black business suit. Hair pinned back at the nape. A polite smile already in place, as though it had been waiting there longer than he had.

She did not look like the mystery person. She looked like a secretary. An assistant.

The assistant, read in two seconds

Suit black, professional, cut so that nothing about it asks to be looked at

Hair pinned back; not one strand doing any work

Face a polite smile, applied like a uniform rather than a mood

Hands empty, still, in front of her

She bows slightly when she sees him.

People who are frightened stand differently. She is not frightened, which is worse.

“Mr. Bae. The President is already waiting for you — please come with me.”

Bae Do-yoon was silent for a moment. Then he followed.

This was not his first time inside the hotel. When he was still an intern at the Judicial Research and Training Institute he had come twice with other prosecutors.

Only this morning did he learn where the Shilla actually keeps its depth.

Not the banquet halls. Not the suites. Not the private rooms that open only to the top tier of membership.

The real building begins behind corridors that look perfectly ordinary and are shut off one after another.

The way in, stop by stop

01First door. A card.

02Second door. A thumb, on glass.

03Third door. Two guards in suits. They do not search him, do not ask his name, and step aside the moment they see the woman who brought him.

04A dark wooden door with no plaque, no title and no marking of any kind.

He counted the turns in the corridor on the way in. Not because he planned to come back. Because a man who cannot see the wall should at least know how thick it is.

The further she took him, the heavier his chest got.

He had already guessed most of it.

What a corridor admits

Security specInside the Shilla, at this depth, for one person’s convenience

Who can buy itA very short list, in a country with a long one

Who is called PresidentShorter list again

Who is bothOne name, and he already knows it

The bare door is the loudest thing in the corridor.

No plate, no office hours, no name in vinyl. The plainer the door, the more it proves that whoever sits behind it has never needed a name to get a room to stand up.

The woman knocked twice.

“Come in.”

A woman’s voice, and very little weight on it.

The assistant opened the door and did not enter. She turned to him: “Mr. Bae — the President is inside.”

The door closed behind him.

The office was enormous.

One whole wall of glass gave onto Seoul in the early morning, and the Han River ran through it like a silver-grey thread somebody had dropped between towers and traffic.

On the walls hung a few paintings whose prices he could not guess from any angle, and whose value he could state within a second.

The most conspicuous object in the room was the desk in the middle of it.

Dark wood. Grain that sat very still. Corners polished until there was no edge on the thing at all.

It did not read as office furniture. It read as an artwork installed at the center of power, where a person might touch it every day and never once use it for anything.

Behind the desk sat a woman.

A pale suit. Hair pinned up neatly. A face carrying almost nothing on it.

The instant Bae Do-yoon saw her, the guess he had been walking toward all the way down the corridor dropped to the floor and stayed there.

Lee Boo-jin

Head of the Shilla. Eldest daughter of the Samsung house.

The name the corridor has been saying since the first door

Watching him, by her own account, since he was a child

She is not the mystery because she hides. She is the mystery because he has never been told what she wants, and is now about to be told, in part, on her schedule.

The hand

She raised her eyes, held two seconds on his face, and spoke without any ceremony.

“You don’t seem surprised.”

He did not answer straight away.

He was surprised, of course.

What the last few years had taught him was one thing only: the more surprised a man is, the less that fact is allowed onto his face.

He took a breath. “A little.”

He had grown up in an orphanage that the Samsung group sponsored. Once, Lee Boo-jin had come there for a goodwill visit.

What he remembers of that day, at the time he did not know it mattered

Reporters in a hallway that smelled of floor polish. A child stood up and asked to say something for the cameras, and the child had said a great deal of nothing at all, because that is what you are taught to say when important people have flown a long way to look at you.

He had never actually spoken to her. Not one sentence. Which is why it had never once occurred to him that the hand on his life belonged to that woman in the photographs.

He looked at the woman behind the desk.

Before this door opened, he had been nervous.

He could keep his face still; that does not empty a chest.

An existence like that, while it stays hidden, sits over your head like a fog. You don’t know who. You don’t know where. You don’t know from which direction the hand will come next.

A man in the dark is not afraid of the monster. He is afraid of not knowing what the monster looks like.

Now the answer was in the room and, strangely, he could breathe.

The answer was terrible. Worse than any he had prepared for.

But it was concrete.

Concrete means it can be watched. Watched means it can be taken apart. And taken apart, one day, means it can be beaten.

Bae Do-yoon glanced at her, then pulled out the chair across the desk and sat down.

The motion was natural. There was no politeness in it whatsoever.

She looked at him, and something at the bottom of her eyes moved like a reflection of a smile.

“You adapt quickly.”

He settled back against the chair. “Standing up to talk is tiring.”

“Aren’t you curious?”

He was curious. He was curious enough to be chewing through his own cheek.

Curious why she had picked him.

Curious how she knows where he is.

Curious what the woman in his bed actually stands for.

Curious when, exactly, all of it had begun.

But Bae Do-yoon only looked at her, quite levelly, and said: “Curious.”

“Except that if I ask and you don’t answer, the asking means nothing.”

“And if I ask and you answer with only the part you want me to know, that means nothing too.”

She looked at him for a few seconds. Then she laughed.

It was a light laugh — not the sound of a woman offended, more the sound of somebody finding that a thing has behaved as she expected it would.

“I rather like this attitude of yours. Shorting everything.”

“It isn’t shorting everything,” he said. “It’s knowing that right now I’m not in a position to ask for much.”

For a moment the office was very quiet.

The sentence was blunt. She did not deny it.

· · ·

She held out a hand across the desk. “What’s in your hand. Give it to me.”

Bae Do-yoon looked down at the camcorder.

Then he handed it over.

He did not hesitate, because he had a clear view of where he was standing.

The person sitting in front of him was a woman and not an army.

But she carries the surname Lee, and behind the surname stands the Samsung group.

Everything a person can do with that is a joke until he has something she needs. He has no illusion about the order of those sentences.

Nothing in this deck is a plan. A plan needs leverage, and this morning he does not own any.

Lee Boo-jin took the camcorder and opened the lid.

She did not avoid him while she reviewed it.

Footage · played in the presence of the man in it● REC

00:00 · a file that should not exist, played in an office at the top of a hotel

Sound. Nobody needs the picture, and nobody in this room is going to ask for it.

It goes on for considerably longer than either of them has been talking.

Her eyes travel down the time records, stop once on something, and one brow lifts.

Then she looked up at him, with a little surprise in her voice.

“That went on for quite a while.”

A pause. She measured him again, more slowly this time, and a smile of sorts arrived in the sentence.

“Looks like your health is good.”

It was such a small thing to say, said so lightly, that the room got colder rather than warmer.

Bae Do-yoon did not flinch. He leaned back in the chair and answered in the same register.

“I’m young, my body’s fine, and I’m not some antique. If it ran long — what’s the problem?”

She looked at him. He looked at her.

Young. Antique.

Neither of those words had anything to do with the camcorder.

Lee Boo-jin heard it perfectly: the boy was calling her old, and doing it in the tone of a man discussing equipment.

But who is she?

Why she did not throw him out

A person who has stood high a long time does not lose composure because an ant bit him.

And an ant, in her reading, was not insulting anybody. It was reacting. Temper without capacity is not frightening; at most it is a small entertainment, and she had been in too many rooms without entertainment to send this one away.

She smiled faintly, closed the lid, and put the camcorder down beside her, as casually as a man returns a borrowed pen.

“Your mouth is a good deal sharper than it used to be.”

“I have you to thank for that.”

She did not take the bait. She folded both hands on the desk and sat the way money sits: without hurry.

“All right. Whatever you want to know — ask. I’ll answer according to my mood.”

Bae Do-yoon went still for a moment.

He had a great many questions. All of them, however they circled, came back to the same starting point.

He looked up and asked it plainly.

“Why me?”

She barely considered it.

“Because you’re an orphan.”

“…”

He had braced himself. It still cut.

So a man with nobody is a man with no price. Apparently that’s the same thing as being free.

She watched his face and did not soften the tone.

“Don’t take it as an injury. In my reading, that isn’t a defect.”

“Then should I thank you for the compliment?”

“Being an orphan is not, on its own, enough. There are a great many orphans in this world. Very few of them are worth the resources.”

Bae Do-yoon said nothing.

He knew the second half was where this was going.

Sure enough.

“This is the second time we’ve met.”

“The first was at the welfare home you grew up in.”

“That day, every child was smiling.”

“You weren’t.”

His brow moved.

She looked at him the way one looks at a small, amusing fact that has survived twenty years of paperwork.

“You were standing at the very edge of the crowd. You were looking at the camera — and at us.”

“Your eyes didn’t look like a child’s.”

“No flattery. No nerves. No gratitude.”

“And none of what the others had, that hope of being chosen, of being praised, of being sponsored.”

She let the silence sit, and said it slowly.

“You looked like you were observing.”

The corner of Bae Do-yoon’s mouth twitched.

Because he was a reincarnator.

A child’s body, with an adult’s memory bolted in behind the eyes.

Of course he had not wept with gratitude: a new schoolbag, a box of colored pencils, and a few warm sentences from important people who would be in a car in ten minutes.

What had actually been going through his head that morning, at an age when children are supposed to be loud, was something else.

What is this charity worth, in money?

Which outlet runs the photograph?

How much of it does the home actually receive?

And in this hallway — who is the person whose word counts?

A child with those four questions in his face is odd, yes.

And on top of the oddness, he was carrying that damned gold entry.

Entry · Favor of Rich Women (Gold)

StateStirred — as if something touched it lightly, from across a desk

Present companyOne woman. Old enough to be his mother. Wealthy enough that the word rich is doing nothing for her.

He has spent years suspecting the entry is the reason. It is a strange thing, to be chosen by a gift you did not ask for and cannot switch off.

If I’m honest, that is the bigger reason.

He looked at her. “So you thought I was different?”

Lee Boo-jin gave a small nod. “Different. And suitable to cultivate.”

He laughed, and there was nothing in it. “Cultivate into a prosecutor?”

She held his eyes, and her voice did not move at all.

“Cultivate into a blade that is useful.”

Bae Do-yoon’s gaze went cold.

She talked over the coldness as if it had not been there.

“You’re clever. You’re handsome. You have no one behind you. Your record is clean.”

“More than that: you have a composure ordinary children don’t have.”

“Left to grow up on your own, you would probably have gone into entertainment and used that face and that head to climb.”

“That would have been too much of a waste.”

He laughed at that one — a cold little laugh, and he did not bother to hide the temperature of it.

“So you planned my life out for me?”

“When a man doesn’t know where his own value is, having someone plan it for him is not a misfortune.”

“And if he does know?”

She looked at him for a moment.

“Do you?”

He did not answer immediately.

“You wanted to go to an entertainment company,” she said. “You wanted to be an idol. You meant to debut on your face and use the Korean entertainment industry as a ladder. You thought that was a plan for a life?”

She shook her head, very slightly.

“That was only a young man’s misunderstanding of what a shortcut is.”

On the cruelty of accurate criticism

The hardest sentence in this chapter is hers, and it is hard because it is correct about a schoolboy’s dream and completely wrong about the man who had it. That gap — between the plan and the person who made it — is what the rest of the book is for. Chapters that flatter our hero are cheap; the ones that let somebody richer than him talk are worth the read.
